## Sample 1: The Introduction (Part 1, opening)

> The eternal God having made man a rational and free agent, able to
> understand and choose the good, and refuse the evil; to know, and love, and
> serve his Maker, and by adhering to him in this life of trial, to attain to
> the blessed sight and enjoyment of his glory in the life to come, has not
> failed to provide him with such necessities, without which these ends could
> not successfully be sought. When we had lost our moral capacity of pleasing
> him, that we might enjoy him, he restores us to it by the wonderful work of
> our redemption. In Christ he has reconciled the world to himself; and has
> given them a general amnesty, contained in the covenant of grace, of which
> nothing but men's stubborn and final unwillingness can deprive them.
>
> But so great is the blindness and stubbornness of men, that the majority
> refuse consent; being deceived by the pleasures, and profits, and honors
> of this present world; and make their supposed necessities or business the
> matter of their excuses, and the unreasonable reasons of their refusal,
> neglect, and delays, until death surprises them, and the door is shut; and
> they knock, and cry for mercy and admission, when it is too late (Matthew
> 25:10–12).

## Sample 2: Direction VII on delighting in God (Part 1, Ch. 3)

> **Direction 7.** Do not corrupt your minds and appetites with contrary
> delights.--Do not addict yourselves to fleshly pleasures: taste nothing
> that is forbidden. Sorrow itself is not such an enemy to spiritual
> delights, as physical, sinful pleasures are. O leave your animal and
> childish pleasures, and come and feast your souls on God (Isaiah 55:1–3).
> Away with the delights of lust, and pride, and greed, and empty amusements,
> and gluttony, and drunkenness, if ever you would have the solid and lasting
> delights! Do not think of joining both together. Consider carefully: can it
> be anything but the disease and wickedness of your heart, that can make a
> play, or a feast, or drunken, loose company, more pleasant to you than God?
> What a heart is that which thinks it a toil to meditate on God and heaven;
> and thinks it a pleasure to think of the lures of pride and greed! What a
> heart is that which thinks that indulgence in physical pleasure, looseness,
> and empty pursuits are the pleasure of their families, which must not be
> turned out; and that godliness, and heavenly conversation and exercises,
> would be the sadness and trouble of their families, which must not be
> brought in, lest it spoil their mirth; that thinks it an intolerable toil
> and slavery to love God, and holiness, and heaven, and to be put to work for
> them; and thinks it a delightful thing to love a whore, or excess of food,
> or drink, or amusements! Can you say anything of a man that is more
> disgraceful, unless you say he is a devil? It would not be so vile for a
> child to delight more in a dog than in his parents, or a husband to delight
> more in the ugliest prostitute than in his wife, as it is for a man to
> delight more in fleshly, empty pursuits than in God. Will you be licking up
> this manure, when you should be refreshing your souls in angelic pleasures,
> and getting a foretaste of the delights of heaven? Oh how justly will God
> drive such wretches away from his everlasting presence, who so abhor his
> ways and him! Can they blame him for denying them the things which they
> hate, or value so lightly that they prefer a lust to them? If they were not
> haters of God and holiness, they would never be so opposed even to the
> delights which they should have with him.

## Sample 3: Directions VIII–IX on melancholy and impatience (Part 1, Ch. 3)

> **Direction 8.** Beware of a melancholy bodily constitution; for melancholy
> people can scarcely delight in anything at all, and therefore not in God.
> Delight is as hard to them, as it is to a painful limb to find pleasure, or
> a sick stomach to delight in the food which it loathes. They can think of
> God with trouble, and fear, and horror, and despair; but not with delight.
>
> **Direction 9.** Beware of an impatient, peevish, self-tormenting mind,
> that can bear no hardship; and of overvaluing earthly things, which causes
> impatience when we lack them. Do not make too great a matter of fleshly
> pain or pleasure.--Otherwise your minds will be called to a continual
> attendance on the flesh, and taken up with continual desires, or cares, or
> fears, or griefs, or pleasures; and will not be permitted to find comfort
> in God. The soul that would have pure and high delights, must withdraw
> itself from the concerns of the flesh; and look on your body, as if it were
> the body of another, whose pain or pleasure you can choose whether you will
> feel. When Paul was caught up into the third heaven, and saw the
> inexpressible things, he was so far freed from the prison of the senses,
> that he did not know whether he was in the body or out of it. As the
> separated souls, that see the face of God and the Redeemer, leave the body
> to be buried, and to rot in darkness, and feel none of this to the
> interruption of their joys; so faith can imitate such a death to the world,
> and such a neglect of the flesh, and some kind of elevating separation of
> the mind, to the things above. If in this close union you cannot leave the
> body to rejoice or suffer alone, yet, as it is only a servant to the soul,
> so do not let its pain or pleasure be dominant, and control the high
> operations of the soul. A manly, courageous, believing soul, though it
> cannot lessen the pain at all, nor reconcile the flesh to its calamity, yet
> can do more, in spite of the pain, toward its own delight, than those
> unacquainted with it will believe.
>
> Some women, and emotional, weak-spirited men, especially in sickness, are
> so peevish, and of such impatient minds, that their daily work is to upset
> and torment themselves. One can scarcely tell how to speak to them, or look
> at them, without offending them. And the world is so full of occasions of
> provocation, that such persons are likely to have little quietness. It is
> unlikely that these should delight in God, who keep their minds in a
> continual, sore, raw state, incapable of any delights at all, and never
> cease their self-tormenting.

## Open questions

1. **"Physical, sinful pleasures"** (Sample 2, first sentence) is the weakest
   change. Baxter's contrast is between *spiritual* delights and delights of
   the *senses and appetites*. "Physical" is close but flat. Alternatives:
   "bodily," "worldly," or keeping "sensual" and relying on the glossary.
2. **"Indulgence in physical pleasure"** is wordy in a list of single nouns
   ("indulgence in physical pleasure, looseness, and empty pursuits"). A
   single word like "self-indulgence" is tighter but shifts the sense a
   little toward vice.
3. **"Empty amusements" / "empty pursuits"** now appear in close succession
   and both say "empty." Baxter used *vain* for both, so the repetition is
   faithful, but it may read as heavy. Fine to keep?
