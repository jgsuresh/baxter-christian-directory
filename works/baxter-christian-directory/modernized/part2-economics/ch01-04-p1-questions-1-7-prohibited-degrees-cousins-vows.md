# Part 2, Chapter 1: Questions 1–7, the prohibited degrees, marriage of first cousins, and vows

*Question 1.* What should one follow as a certain rule, about the prohibited degrees of consanguinity or affinity? seeing, 1. The law of Moses is not in force for us. 2. And if it were, it is very unclear whether it may by parity of reason be extended to more degrees than are named in the text. 3. And seeing the law of nature is so hard to read in this case. [7]

*Answer.* 1. It is certain that the prohibited degrees are not so fixedly and universally unlawful that such marriage may not be made lawful by any necessity. For Adam's sons did lawfully marry their own sisters.

2. But now that the world is peopled, such necessities as will warrant such marriages must necessarily be very rare, and such as we are never likely to meet with.

3. It is the law of nature which prohibits the degrees that are now unlawful; and though this law is unclear as to some degrees, it is not so as to others.

4. The law of God to the Jews, Leviticus 18, does not prohibit those degrees there named because of any reason proper to the Jews, but as an exposition of the law of nature, and so on reasons common to all.

5. Therefore, though the Jewish law ceases (indeed, never bound other nations) formally as that political national law; yet as it was God's exposition of his own law of nature, it is of use, and of consequential obligation to all men, even to this day; for if God had once told but one man, This is the sense of the law of nature, it remains true, and all must believe it; and then the law of nature itself, so expounded, will still oblige.

6. The world is so wide for choice, and a necessity of doubtful marriage is so rare, and the trouble so great, that prudence tells everyone that it is their sin, without flat necessity, to marry in a doubtful degree; and therefore it is thus safest to avoid all degrees that seem to be equal to those named in Leviticus 18, and to have the same reason, though they are not named.

7. But because it is not certain that indeed the unnamed cases have the same reason (while God does not acquaint us with all the reasons of his law), therefore when the thing is done, we must not censure others too deeply, nor trouble ourselves too much about those unnamed, doubtful cases. We must avoid them beforehand, because otherwise we shall cast ourselves into doubts and troubles unnecessarily; but when it is past, the case must be considered as I shall afterward explain.

*Question 2.* What if the law of the land forbids more or fewer degrees than Leviticus 18 does?

*Answer.* If it forbids fewer, the rest are nevertheless to be avoided as forbidden by God. If it forbids more, the forbidden ones must be avoided in obedience to our rulers.

*Question 3.* Is the marriage of first cousins, that is, of brothers' children, or sisters' children, or brothers' and sisters' children, unlawful?

*Answer.* I think not; 1. Because it is not forbidden by God. 2. Because none of that same rank are forbidden; that is, none that on both sides are two degrees from the root. I refer the reader for my reasons to a Latin treatise of Charles Butler on this subject, for in those I rest. As all the children of Noah's sons did marry their first cousins (for they could not marry in any remoter degree), so have others since without reproof, and none are forbidden. 3. But it is safest to do otherwise, because there is choice enough besides, and because many theologians being of the contrary opinion may make it a matter of scruple and trouble afterward to those that venture upon it without need.

*Question 4.* What would you have those do that have married first cousins, and now doubt whether it is lawful to do so?

*Answer.* I would have them cast away such doubts, or at least conclude that it is now their duty to live peaceably in the state in which they are; and a great sin for them to be separated on such scruples. The reason is because, if it is not certain that the degree is lawful, at least no man can be sure that it is unlawful. And for husband and wife to break their covenants and part, without a necessary cause, is a great sin; and that which no man can prove to be a sin is no necessary or lawful cause of a divorce. Marriage duties are certainly commanded to the married, but the marriage of first cousins is not certainly forbidden. Therefore if it were a sin to marry so, to them that doubted; or if they have since fallen into doubt whether it was not a sin; yet they may be sure that the continuance of it is a duty, and that all they have to do is to repent of doing a doubtful thing, but not to part, nor to refrain from their covenanted duties. No, nor to indulge or allow those troublesome scruples, which would hinder the cheerful discharge of their duties, and the comforting serving of God in their relations.

*Question 5.* What should those do that are married in those degrees which are not forbidden by name in Leviticus 18, and yet are at the same distance from the root as those that are named, and seem to have the same reason of unlawfulness?

*Answer.* If there is clearly a parity of degree, and also of the reason of the prohibition, then no doubt they must part as incestuous, and not continue in a forbidden state. But because theologians disagree whether there is in all instances a parity of the reason of the prohibition, where there is an equal distance as to degrees; and so in those cases some think it a duty to be separated, and others think it enough to repent of their union and not to be separated, because the case is doubtful (as the controversy shows), I shall not venture to cast in my judgment in a case where so many and such men disagree; but shall only advise all to prevent such troublesome doubts beforehand, and not by rashness to run themselves into perplexities when there is no necessity; unless they will call their carnal ends or sinful passions a necessity.

*Question 6.* But if a man does marry in a degree expressly there forbidden, is it in all cases a sin to continue in that state? If necessity made such marriage a duty to Adam's children, why may not necessity make the continuance lawful to others? As, suppose the king or parents command it? suppose the woman will die or be driven out of her mind with grief otherwise? suppose one has made a vow to marry no other, and yet cannot live single, etc.? Here I shall suppose that if a lustful person marries a kinswoman so that he may have a change, as foreknowing that he must be divorced, punishment, and not continuance in the sin, must be his sentence; and if one that has married a kinswoman is glad to be divorced, because he hates her or loves change, punishment must rebuke him, but he must not continue in incest.

*Answer.* 1. Natural necessity justified Adam's children, and such would now justify you. Indeed, the blessing "Be fruitful and multiply" did not only allow, but oblige them then to marry, to fill the earth (when otherwise mankind would soon have ceased); but it does not so oblige us now when the earth is filled. Yet I do not deny but that if a man and his sister were cast alone upon a foreign wilderness, where they justly despaired of any other company, if God should bid them there "be fruitful and multiply," it would warrant them to marry. But otherwise there is no necessity of it, and therefore no lawfulness. For, 2. A sinful necessity does not justify the sin. If the man or woman that should abstain will be mad or dead with passion, rather than obey God, and deny and mortify their lust, it is not one sin that will justify them in another. The thing that is necessary is to conform their wills to the law of God; and if they will not, and then say, They cannot, they must bear what they get by it. 3. And it is no necessity that is imposed by that command of king or parents which is against the law of God. 4. No, nor by a vow either; for a vow to break God's law is not an obligation to be kept, but to be repented of; nor is the necessity without remedy which such a one brings on himself by vowing never to marry any other; seeing chastity may be kept.

*Question 7.* Is it lawful for one to marry that has vowed chastity during life, and not to marry, and afterward finds a necessity of marrying, for the avoiding of lust and fornication?

## Baxter's notes

[7] The case of polygamy is so fully and plainly resolved by Christ that I do not take it to be necessary to decide it, especially while the law of the land makes it punishable by death.

## Translator's notes

- **Where this file starts and stops:** it begins with Question 1 of this run of cases on the prohibited degrees and vows, and ends with the bare statement of Question 7; Baxter's answer to Question 7 is in the next file. His Roman numerals (I.–VII.) are given as Arabic numbers.
- **Note [7]:** the marker stands at the end of Question 1, but the note is about polygamy, which Baxter declines to treat. His view of polygamy, and his statement that English law then made it a capital crime, are kept as his. "makes it death" is rendered "makes it punishable by death."
- **consanguinity or affinity:** kept. Consanguinity is relation by blood; affinity is relation by marriage (in-laws). **prohibited degrees:** the degrees of kinship within which marriage is forbidden. **two degrees from the root:** Baxter counts each generation from the common ancestor (the "root") as one degree.
- **Leviticus 18:** Baxter cites the chapter but quotes no verse from it. His argument that its list expounds the law of nature binding all people, and his advice to avoid unnamed degrees of the same distance, are kept exactly as he states them.
- **first cousins:** Baxter's word is "cousin-germans" (first cousins, children of siblings); he defines it himself in Question 3. His judgment that such marriage is lawful but best avoided is kept as he states it.
- **Charles Butler:** Baxter names a Latin treatise by him on this subject but gives no title; we have not identified it.
- **Adam's sons married their sisters; Noah's grandchildren married their first cousins:** Baxter's inferences from Genesis, kept as his; no references are given in the text.
- **"Be fruitful and multiply"** (Question 6, twice): Baxter quotes "Increase and multiply" without a reference; the words are from God's blessing in Genesis 1:28 (and again to Noah in Genesis 9:1), references not in Baxter's text. The NET reads "Be fruitful and multiply!" The sense Baxter leans on (a command to propagate) is unchanged. **to replenish the earth** (not in quotation marks in the source) is rendered **to fill the earth**, the NET's wording and the older sense of "replenish"; likewise "when the earth is replenished" becomes "when the earth is filled."
- **Word swaps:** **very dark / dark** (of a law) became **very unclear / unclear**; **hardly legible** became **hard to read**; **statedly** became **fixedly**; **must needs** became **must necessarily**; **like** became **likely**; **yea** became **indeed**; **after open** became **afterward explain**; **divines** became **theologians**; **forbear** became **refrain from**; **suffer** became **allow**; **comfortable** became **comforting**; **conjunction** (of the married couple) became **union**; **are disagreed** became **disagree**; **distracted** (with grief) became **driven out of her mind**; **else** became **otherwise**; **benediction** became **blessing**; **vicious necessity** (a necessity arising from vice) became **sinful necessity**; **remediless** became **without remedy**; **&c.** became **etc.**
- **that he may have change** (Question 6): "change" means variety in sexual partners; rendered "so that he may have a change."
- **censure; scruple(s); parity (of reason):** kept. To censure is to judge or condemn; a scruple is a doubt of conscience; "by parity of reason" means by the same reasoning applied to equal cases.
- **carnal; mortify:** kept as technical terms. Carnal means ruled by the flesh, unspiritual; to mortify is to put to death (sinful desires).
- **so it doth not us now** (Question 6): rendered "it does not so oblige us now," supplying the verb Baxter's ellipsis implies.
- **a necessity of doubtful marriage** (Question 1, point 6): kept as printed; the sense seems to be a necessity of marrying within a doubtful degree.
- **The married, divorce, and separation:** Baxter's counsel that couples in doubtful degrees must not part, and that those in clearly forbidden degrees must, is kept as he states it. Some readers today will find his framing of these cases dated; it is translated faithfully, not softened.
