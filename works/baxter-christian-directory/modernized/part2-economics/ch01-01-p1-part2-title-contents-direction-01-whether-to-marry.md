# Part 2, Chapter 1: Title and contents of Part 2; opening, and Direction 1 (whether to marry)

1. Directions about marriage; for choice and contract.

3. A disputation, or arguments to prove the necessity of family worship and holiness, or directions against the quibbles of the profane, and some sectarians, who deny it to be a thing required by God.

20. Directions for profitably reading the holy Scriptures.
21. Directions for reading other books.

**Chapter 1. Directions about marriage; for choice and contract.**

As the persons of Christians in their most private capacities are holy, as being dedicated and separated to God, so also must their families be: HOLINESS TO THE LORD must be as it were written on their doors, and on their relations, their possessions, and affairs. For which it is necessary, 1. That there be a holy constitution of their families. 2. And a holy government of them, and discharge of the respective duties of the members of the family. To the right constituting of a family belongs (1.) The right contracting of marriage, and, (2.) The right choice and contract between masters and their servants. For the first,

**Direction 1. Be careful that neither lust nor rashness thrust you into a married condition, before you see such reasons to invite you to it as may assure you of the call and approval of God.**

For, 1. It is God that you must serve in your married state, and therefore it is fitting that you take his counsel before you rush into it; for he knows best himself what belongs to his service. 2. And it is God that you must always depend on for the blessing and comforts of your relationship: and therefore there is very great reason that you take his advice and consent, as the chief things required for the match: if the consent of parents is necessary, much more is the consent of God.

*Question.* But how shall a man know whether God calls him to marriage, or consents to it? Has he not here left all men to their liberties, as in a thing indifferent?

*In the margin: Whether marriage is indifferent.*

*Answer.* God has not made any universal law commanding or forbidding marriage; but in this regard has left it indifferent to mankind: yet not allowing all to marry (for undoubtedly to some it is unlawful). But he has by other general laws or rules directed men to know in what cases it is lawful, and in what cases it is a sin. As every man is bound to choose that condition in which he may serve God with the best advantages, and which tends most to his spiritual welfare, and increase in holiness. Now there is nothing in marriage itself which makes it commonly inconsistent with these benefits, and the fulfilling of these laws: and therefore it is said that "he who marries does well," [1] that is, he does that which of itself is not unlawful, and which to some is the most desirable state of life. But there is something in a single life which makes it, especially to preachers and persecuted Christians, more usually the most advantageous state of life, for these ends of Christianity; and therefore it is said that "he who does not marry does better." And yet to individual persons, it is hard to imagine how it can fail to be either a duty or a sin; at least except in some unusual cases. For it is a thing of such great importance as to the ordering of our hearts and lives that it is hard to imagine that it should ever be indifferent as a means to our main end, but must be either a very great help or hindrance. But yet if there are any persons whose case may be so equally balanced with circumstances on both sides that to the most judicious man it is not discernible whether a single or married state of life is likely to contribute more to their personal holiness or public usefulness, or the good of others, to such persons marriage in the individual circumstantiated act is a thing indifferent.

*In the margin: Who are called to marry.*

By these conditions following you may know what persons have a call from God to marry, and who do not have his call or approval. 1. If there is the peremptory will or command of parents to children that are under their power and government, and no greater matter on the contrary to hinder it, the command of parents signifies the command of God: but if parents only persuade and do not command, though their desires must not be refused without cause, yet a smaller impediment may prevail than in case of a peremptory command. 2. They are called to marry who do not have the gift of continence, and cannot by the use of lawful means attain it, and have no impediment which makes it unlawful for them to marry. "But if they do not have self-control, let them get married. For it is better to marry than to burn with sexual desire" (1 Corinthians 7:9). But here the various degrees of the urgent and the hindering causes must be compared, and the weightiest must prevail. For some that have very strong lusts may yet have stronger impediments: and though they cannot keep such chastity in their thoughts as they desire, yet in such a case they must abstain. And there is no man who may not keep his body in chastity if he will do his part: yes, and thoughts themselves may be commonly, and for the most part, kept pure, and lustful imaginations quickly checked, if men are godly, and will do what they can. But on the other side, there are some that have a more tamable measure of concupiscence, and yet have no considerable hindrance, whose duty it may be to marry, as the most certain and successful means against that small degree, as long as there is nothing to forbid it. 3. Another cause that warrants marriage is when, upon a wise adding up of all accounts, it is evidently most probable that in a married state one may be most serviceable to God and the public good: that there will be in it greater helps and fewer hindrances to the great ends of our lives; the glorifying of God, and the saving of ourselves and others. And whereas it must be expected that every condition should be more helpful to us in one respect, and hinder us more in another respect; and that in one we have most helps for a contemplative life, and in another we are better furnished for an active, serviceable life, the great skill therefore in the discerning of our duties lies in the prudent weighing and comparing of the advantages and disadvantages, without the seduction of imagination, lust, or passion, and in a true discerning which side it is that has the greatest weight. [2]

## Baxter's notes

[1] 1 Corinthians 7:7, 38.

[2] Unmarried men are the best friends, the best masters, the best servants; but not always the best subjects: for they are quick to run away, and therefore venturous, etc. Lord Bacon, Essay 8.

## Translator's notes

- **Title page:** "Economics" is Baxter's word in its older sense, the ordering of a household (the "family" here includes servants as well as spouses and children); it does not mean the modern study of markets. "Appurtenances" (kept from the source title) means the things that belong to or go with family relations. "Family Relations" means the relationships within a household (husband and wife, parent and child, master and servant).
- **Contents:** the 1846 edition's table of contents gave a page number for each chapter (from 394 to 534); the page numbers were dropped. Its Roman chapter numbers are given as Arabic list numbers. Word swaps there: "cavils" to "quibbles"; "sectaries" to "sectarians" (members of separatist sects; Baxter's polemical word, kept in sense); "towards" to "toward"; "fellow-servants" to "fellow servants"; "holy conference" to "holy conversation" (glossary: conference = conversation); "profitable hearing" and "profitable reading" to "profitably hearing" and "profitably reading"; "scriptures" capitalized. "The profane" (irreligious, ungodly people) is kept.
- **Chapter 1 runs past this file:** the file stops at the end of the first part of Direction 1's answer (Baxter's three marks of a call to marry). Direction 1 continues in the next file with "Observations" on these reasons.
- **"HOLINESS TO THE LORD"** (opening paragraph): Baxter's capitals, an unmarked allusion to the inscription on the high priest's gold plate (Exodus 28:36), and probably also to Zechariah 14:20, where it is written on the horses' bells. Kept in Baxter's wording since he does not cite or quote a verse. The NET has "Holiness to the Lord" at Exodus 28:36 but "Holy to the Lord" at Zechariah 14:20.
- **"he who marries does well" / "he who does not marry does better" (1 Corinthians 7:38; flag):** Baxter's footnote [1] cites 1 Corinthians 7:7 and 38. His wording is his own paraphrase, not the King James text (which speaks of the one who "giveth her in marriage"), so we keep his words, modernized. The NET reads: "So then, the one who marries his own virgin does well, but the one who does not, does better." Both the King James and the NET make the verse about a particular man and a particular virgin ("giveth her in marriage" in the King James; "marries his own virgin" in the NET), while Baxter applies it to marrying and not marrying in general; the general sense he draws from it (marriage is good, a single life better for some) still stands in the NET.
- **1 Corinthians 7:9 (flag):** NET wording. The King James has "if they cannot contain, let them marry; for it is better to marry than to burn." The NET's "do not have self-control" keeps Baxter's link with "the gift of continence," but its "burn with sexual desire" spells out what the King James "burn" leaves implicit; Baxter takes it in that same sense (the urging of lust), so the argument is not changed. The NET text is two sentences where Baxter's King James quotation is one.
- **Lord Bacon** (note [2]): Francis Bacon, quoted from his *Essays*; Essay 8 is probably the essay "Of Marriage and Single Life," but we have not checked the numbering against the edition Baxter used. The words are given as Baxter gives them, modernized ("light to run away" to "quick to run away"); "venturous" (ready to take risks) is kept.
- **Dated views:** Baxter's assumption that parents may command grown children under their government to marry, and that such a command "signifies the command of God," reflects the household order of his time; it is kept as he states it. His view that a single life is usually better for preachers is also his own, stated as such.
- **Terms kept:** "indifferent" (neither commanded nor forbidden by God, so left to one's choice); "continence" (sexual self-restraint); "concupiscence" (sinful desire, here sexual desire); "circumstantiated act" (a term from the moral theology of the schools: the act considered with all its particular circumstances, as distinct from marriage considered in general); "peremptory" (absolute, not open to refusal); "passion" (strong emotion); "seduction" (in Baxter's sense, being led astray, not only sexually); "the profane."
- **Word swaps:** "privatest" to "most private"; "requisite" to "necessary" (opening) and "required for" (Direction 1); "several duties" to "respective duties" (glossary); "betwixt" to "between"; "Take heed" to "Be careful"; "approbation" to "approval"; "meet" to "fitting"; "rush upon it" to "rush into it"; "still depend upon" to "always depend on" (still in its older sense, always); "relation" (the married relationship) to "relationship" (but "relations" in the opening paragraph, meaning family relationships or relatives, is kept); "eligible" to "desirable" (most worth choosing); "which maketh it ... to be more usually" to "which makes it ... more usually" (dropping "to be," which no longer follows "makes it"); "how it can choose but be" to "how it can fail to be"; "of so great moment" to "of such great importance"; "hinderance" to "hindrance"; "poised" to "balanced"; "accidents" to "circumstances" (accident in its older sense, a circumstance that may or may not be present); "like to conduce" to "likely to contribute"; "do but persuade" to "only persuade"; "causelessly refused" to "refused without cause"; "preponderate" to "prevail" (carry the balance); "divers" to "various"; "that chastity ... as they desire" to "such chastity ... as they desire"; "there is no man but may" to "there is no man who may not"; "yea" to "yes"; "wanton imaginations" to "lustful imaginations"; "tameable" to "tamable" (spelling); "casting up of all accounts" to "adding up of all accounts"; "apparently" to "evidently" (apparently in its older sense, plainly); "pondering" to "weighing" (to ponder once meant to weigh); "commodities and discommodities" to "advantages and disadvantages"; "fantasy" to "imagination" (glossary); "to these ends" to "for these ends."
- **"As every man is bound to choose ..."** (in the Answer) is an incomplete sentence in the source, giving an example of the general rules in the sentence before it; kept as printed.
- **Pronouns:** Baxter's "a man," "men," and "he" in the Question and Answer mean any person considering marriage; kept as written.
