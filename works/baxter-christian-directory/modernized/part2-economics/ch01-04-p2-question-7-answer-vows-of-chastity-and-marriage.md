# Part 2, Chapter 1: The answer to Question 7, whether one who has vowed chastity may marry

*Answer.* I know that many great theologians have easily absolved those that under popery vowed chastity. The principal part of the solution of the question you must take from my solution of the Case of Vows, Part 3, Chapter 5, Title 2. At present this shall suffice to be added to it. 1. Such vows of chastity that are absolute, without any exceptions for later changes or difficulties that may arise, are sinfully made, or are unlawful *quoad actum jurandi*. [8]

2. If parents or others impose such oaths and vows on their children or subjects, or induce them to it, it is sinfully done by them, and the *actus imperantium* is also unlawful.

3. Yet as long as the *materia jurata*, the matter vowed, remains lawful, the vow does bind, and it is perfidy to break it. For the sinfulness of the imposer's act proves no more but that such a command did not oblige you to vow. And a vow made arbitrarily without any command does nevertheless bind. And the sinfulness of the making of the vow does only call for repentance; (as if you made it causelessly, rashly, upon bad motives, and to bad ends, or in bad circumstances, etc.) But yet that vow which you repent that you ever made must be nevertheless kept, if the thing vowed is a lawful thing, and the act of vowing is not made a nullity (though it was a sin). And when it is a nullity, I have shown in the place cited above.

4. A vow of celibacy or chastity during life, which has this condition or exception expressed or implied in the true intent of the one vowing, (unless anything happens which shall make it a sin for me not to marry,) may in some cases be a lawful vow; as to one that foresees great disadvantages in marriage, and would by firm resolution fortify himself against temptations and changeableness.

5. If there were no such excepting thought in the person vowing, yet when the thing becomes unlawful, the vow is not to be kept; though it obliges us under guilt for sinfully making it, yet God commands us not to keep it, because we vowed that which he forbade us not only to vow but to do.

6. Either the papists suppose such exceptions to be always implied by those who take their vows, or at least that they are contained in the law of God, or else surely they would never dare claim that the pope has power to dispense with such vows (as they have often done for princes, men and women, that they might be taken from a monastery to a crown). For if they suppose that the persons before the dispensation are under the obligation of their vow, and bound by God to keep it, then it would be too gross and odious blasphemy for the pope to claim a power of releasing them, and dissolving God's commands; and not only antichristianity, but antitheistical, or a setting himself above God Almighty, under pretext of his own commission. But if they only claim to dissolve such vows judicially or decisively, by judging when the person is no longer obliged to keep them by God's law, then they suppose that the obligation of God's law has ceased before they judicially declare it to have ceased. And if that were all that the pope undertook, he had no power to do it outside his own parish, nor more than any lawful bishop has in his own charge.

7. The matter of a vow of celibacy or chastity is then unlawful when it cannot be kept without greater sin than that life of chastity escapes, and which would be escaped if it were forsaken; or without the omission of greater duty, and omission of greater good, than that life of chastity contains or attains. For the further explaining of this, let it be noted that,

8. It is not every degree of sin which marriage would cure that will warrant the breach of a vow of chastity. As if I had some more lustful thoughts or instigations and stirrings in a single life than I should have if I married. The reason is because, 1. No man lives without some sin, and it is supposed that there are greater sins of another kind which by a life of chastity I avoid. And the breach of the vow itself is a greater matter than a lustful thought.

9. So it is not every degree of good which by marriage I may attain or do that will warrant it against a vow of chastity. Because I may do and get a greater good by chastity, and because the evil of perjury is not to be done that good may be done by it; till I can prove that it is not only good in itself, but a duty *hic et nunc* to me.

10. A man should rather break his vow of celibacy than once commit fornication, if there were a necessity that he must do the one. Because fornication is a sin which no vow will warrant any man to commit.

11. A man should rather break his vow of celibacy than live in such constant or habitual lust as unfits him for prayer, and a holy life, and keeps him in habitual danger of fornication, if there were a necessity that he must do the one. The reason is also because now the matter vowed has become unlawful, and no vow can warrant a man to live in so great sin (unless there were some greater sin on the other side which could not be avoided in a married life, which is scarcely to be supposed, however popish priests think disobedience to the pope, and the inconvenience and disgrace of a married life, etc. to be a greater sin than fornication itself).

12. If a prince vows chastity, when it is likely to endanger the kingdom for lack of a safe and sure succession, he is bound to break that vow; because he may not lawfully give away the people's right, nor do that which is injurious to so many.

13. Whether the command of a parent or prince may dissolve the obligation of a vow of celibacy, I have answered already. I now say but this, 1. When parents or princes may justly command it, we may justly obey them. But this is not one of those incidental evils which may be lawfully done, though unlawfully commanded. 2. It is to parents that God has committed more of this care and power about children's marriage than to princes. 3. Neither parents nor princes may lawfully command the breach of such a vow, (not nullified at first,) except in such cases as release us whether they do it or not; so that the resolving of the main case suffices for all.

14. He that by lawful means can overcome his lust, to the measure mentioned before, is under no necessity of violating his vow of single life.

15. I think that it is not one in twenty that have bodies so unavoidably prone to lust, but that by due means it might be so far (though not totally) overcome, without marriage, fornication, willful self-pollution, or violent, troubling, lustful thoughts. That is, 1. If they employ themselves constantly and diligently in a lawful calling, and are not guilty of such idleness as leaves room in their minds and imaginations for empty and filthy thoughts. If they follow such a calling as shall lay a necessity upon them to keep their thoughts closely occupied with it. 2. If they use such abstinence and plainness in their diet as is fit to tame excessive lusts, without destroying health: and not only avoid overfullness and gluttony, and empty amusements and pleasures, but also use suitable fasting, and tame the body by necessary austerities. 3. If they sufficiently avoid all tempting company and sights, and keep at a fit distance from them. 4. If they set such a restraint upon their thoughts as they may do. 5. If they use such a quality of diet and medicine as is most suitable for altering those bodily disorders which are the cause. 6. And lastly, if they are earnest in prayer to God, and live in mortifying meditations, especially in a constant familiarity with a crucified Christ, and with the grave, and with the heavenly society. He that breaks his vow to save himself the labor and suffering of these unpleasant means I take to be perfidious, though perhaps he sinfully made that vow. And no greater number are excusable for continence after such a vow than those that have bodies so extraordinarily lustful as no such other means can tame, and those mentioned before that have extraordinary circumstances to make a single life unlawful.

16. It must not be forgotten here that if men trust to marriage itself alone as the cure of their lust, without other means, such violent lusts as nothing else will cure may possibly be much uncured afterward. For adulterers are as violent in their lusts as the unmarried, and often find it as hard to restrain them. And therefore the married, as well as others, need to be careful to overcome their lust. And the more so because it is in them a double sin.

17. But yet when all other means fail, marriage is God's appointed means to quench those flames from which men's vows cannot, in cases of true necessity, release them.

## Translator's notes

- **Where this file starts and stops:** it begins with Baxter's *Answer* to Question 7 (whether one who has vowed lifelong chastity may marry when marriage becomes necessary to avoid lust and fornication); the question itself is at the end of the previous file. It ends with point 17, the last of the answer and of the chapter's text; the chapter's footnotes follow in the source.
- **Latin:** *quoad actum jurandi* (English: as to the act of swearing); *actus imperantium* (English: the act of those who command); *materia jurata* (English: the matter sworn; Baxter glosses it himself as "the matter vowed"); *hic et nunc* (English: here and now).
- **Note [8]:** "the grand controversies of this time" is not explained further; it may refer to the disputes of Baxter's day over the binding force of oaths and covenants, but we have not confirmed what he had in view.
- **Cross-reference:** "my solution of the Case of Vows, part iii. chap. v. tit. 2" is rendered "Part 3, Chapter 5, Title 2"; we have not checked that this place in the *Directory* treats vows. "I have answered already" (point 13) refers back to an earlier answer we have not located.
- **popery, papists, popish; the pope's dispensations:** Baxter's polemic against Rome, kept as written. His claim that popes have released princes and others from monastic vows so that they might take a crown is kept as his. To "dispense with" a vow is to grant an official release from it.
- **antichristianity, antitheistical:** kept as printed; "antitheistical" means opposed to God himself, as Baxter explains.
- **celibacy:** Baxter uses "celibate" as a noun ("a vow of celibate"); rendered "celibacy."
- **self-pollution** (point 15): kept; it means masturbation.
- **for continence after such a vow** (point 15): kept as printed. The sense seems to be "excusable for [failing in] continence," that is, for breaking the vow by marrying; "incontinence" may have been intended.
- **The reason is because, 1.** (point 8): Baxter numbers a first reason and gives no "2."; the next sentence ("And the breach of the vow...") may be the second reason. Kept as printed.
- **Parents not princes may not** (point 13, item 3): the source reads "Parents not princes may not lawfully command"; we read "not" as a misprint for "nor" and render "Neither parents nor princes may lawfully command."
- **Baxter's views on celibacy, lust, and marriage** (for instance, that not one in twenty is so prone to lust that diet, work, fasting, and prayer cannot overcome it, and that marriage is the last remedy) are kept exactly as he states them. His remedies of coarse diet and medicine for "bodily distempers" reflect the medicine of his day; they are translated faithfully, not softened.
- **calling:** kept; Baxter's word for one's vocation or occupation. **mortifying:** kept; to mortify is to put to death sinful desires.
- **Word swaps:** **divines** became **theologians**; **fetch from** became **take from**; **after alterations** became **later changes**; **perfidiousness** became **perfidy** (perfidious is kept); **ill** became **bad**; **forecited place** became **place cited above**; **votary** became **the one vowing**, and **their votaries** became **those who take their vows**; **fall out** became **happens**; **inconveniences** (point 4, in the older, stronger sense of harms) became **disadvantages**; **mutability** became **changeableness**; **durst never pretend** became **would never dare claim**; **pretend** (point 6) became **claim**; **oft** and **ofttimes** became **often**; **disobliging / disoblige** became **releasing / release**; **pretence** became **pretext**; **out of his own parish** became **outside his own parish**; **proper charge** became **own charge**; **opening** became **explaining**; **irritations** (in the older sense of provocations of desire) became **stirrings**; **ordinary** (lust, danger) became **habitual**; **hardly** became **scarcely**; **incommodity** became **inconvenience**; **like** became **likely**; **for want of** became **for lack of**; **accidental evils** became **incidental evils**; **vexatious** became **troubling**; **vain** became **empty**; **coarseness** (of diet) became **plainness**; **meet** became **fit**; **inordinate** became **excessive**; **fulness and gulosity** became **overfullness and gluttony**; **sports** became **amusements**; **convenient** became **suitable**; **physic** became **medicine**; **aptest** became **most suitable**; **distempers** became **disorders**; **ungrateful** (means) became **unpleasant**; **extraordinary accidents** became **extraordinary circumstances**; **the rather** became **the more so**; **&c.** became **etc.**
