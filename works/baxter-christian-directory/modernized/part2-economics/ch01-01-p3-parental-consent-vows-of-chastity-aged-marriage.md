# Part 2, Chapter 1: Promises made without parents' consent, vows of chastity, and marriage of the aged

*Question.* But what if the child has promised marriage, and the parents are against it? *Answer.* If the child was under the parents' government, and short of years of discretion also, the promise is void for lack of capacity. And if the child was of age, yet the promise was a sinful promise, as to the promising act, and also as to the thing promised during the parents' dissent. If the *actus promittendi* only had been sinful (the promise-making), the promise might nevertheless bind (unless it were null as well as sinful). But the *materia promissa* being sinful (the matter promised), to marry while parents dissent, such a child is bound to refrain from fulfilling that promise till the parents consent or die. And yet he is bound from marrying any other (unless he is released by the person that he has made the promise to), because he does not know but that his parents may consent hereafter; and whenever they consent or die, the promise then is obligatory, and must be performed.

The thirtieth chapter of Numbers enables parents to release a daughter that is in their house from a vow made to God, provided they disallow it at the first hearing. Hence there are two doubts that arise: 1. Whether this power does not extend to the releasing of a promise or contract of matrimony. 2. Whether it does not extend to a son as well as a daughter. And most expositors are for the affirmative of both cases. But I have shown you before that it is upon uncertain grounds: 1. It is uncertain whether God, who would thus give up his own right in case of vowing, will also give away the right of others, without their consent, in case of promises or contracts. And, 2. It is uncertain whether this is not an indulgence only of the weaker sex, seeing many words in the text seem plainly to imply as much. And it is dangerous upon our own presumptions to stretch God's laws to everything we imagine there is the same reason for; seeing our imaginations may so easily be deceived; and God could have expressed such particulars if he would. And therefore (when there is not clear ground for our inferences in the text) it is only to say, Thus and thus should God have said, when we cannot say, Thus he has said. We must not make laws under the pretext of expounding them: "whatever God commands you, be careful that you do it: you must add nothing to it, nor take anything from it" (Deuteronomy 12:32).

*Question.* If the question therefore is not of the sinfulness, but the nullity of such promises of children, because of the dissent of parents, for my part I am not able to prove any such nullity. It is said that they are not *sui juris*, their own, and therefore their promises are null: but if they have attained to years and use of discretion, they are naturally so far *sui juris* as to be capable of disposing even of their souls, and therefore of their fidelity. They can bind themselves to God or man; though they are not so far *sui juris* as to be ungoverned: for so, no child, no subject, no man is *sui juris*; seeing all are under the government of God. And yet if a man promises to do a thing sinful, it is not a nullity, but a sin; not no promise, but a sinful promise. A nullity is when the *actus promittendi* is reputative *nullus vel non actus*. And when no promise is made, then none can be broken.

*Question.* But if the question is only how far such promises must be kept, I answer by summing up what I have said: 1. If the child did not have the use of reason, the lack of natural capacity proves the promise null: here *ignorantis non est consensus*. 2. If he was of age and use of reason, then, 1. If the promising act only was sinful (as I said before of vows), the promise must be both repented of and kept. It must be repented of because it was a sin; it must be kept because it was a real promise, and the matter lawful. 2. If the promising act was not only a sin but a nullity (by any other reason) then it is no obligation. 3. If not only the promising act is sin, but also the matter promised (as is marrying without parents' consent), then it must be repented of, and not performed till it becomes lawful; because an oath or promise cannot bind a man to violate the laws of God.

*Question.* But what if the parties are actually married without the parents' consent? must they live together, or be separated? *Answer.* 1. If marriage is consummated *per carnalem concubitum*, by the carnal knowledge of each other, I see no reason to imagine that parents can dissolve it, or prohibit their cohabitation: for the marriage (for all I have ever seen) is not proved a nullity, but only a sin, and their *concubitus* is not fornication; and parents cannot forbid husband and wife to live together: and in marriage they do (really though sinfully) forsake father and mother and cleave to each other, and so are now from under their government (though not released from all obedience). 2. But if marriage is only by verbal union, theologians disagree about what is to be done. Some think that it is no complete marriage *ante concubitum*, and also that their union has only the nature of a promise (to be faithful to each other as husband and wife): and therefore the matter promised is unlawful till parents consent, and so not to be done. But I rather think (as most do) that it has all that is essential to marriage *ante concubitum*; and that this marriage is more than a promise of fidelity *de futuro*, even an actual delivery of themselves to one another *de præsenti* also; and that the thing promised in marriage is lawful. For though it is a sin to marry without parents' consent, yet when that is past, it is lawful for married persons to come together though parents do not consent; and therefore that such marriage is valid, and to be continued, though it was sinfully made.

*In the margin: Of vows of chastity.*

3. A third sort who are not called by God to marry are those who have absolutely vowed not to marry. Such may not marry, unless Providence releases them, by making it become an indispensable duty. And I can remember only two ways by which this may be done. 1. In case there are any of such strong lust that no other lawful means but marriage can suffice to maintain their chastity. To such marriage is as great a duty, as to eat or drink, or cover one's nakedness, or to hinder another from uncleanness, or lying, or stealing, or the like. And if you should make a vow that you will never eat or drink, or that you will go naked, or that you will never hinder anyone from uncleanness, lying, or stealing, it is unlawful to fulfill this vow. But all the doubt is whether there are any such persons that cannot overcome or restrain their lust by any other lawful means. I suppose it is possible there may be such; but I believe it is not one in a hundred. If they will only practice the directions given before, Part 1, Chapter 8, Part 5, Titles 1 and 2, I suppose their lust may be restrained: and if that does not prevail, the help of a physician may: and if that does not prevail, some think the help of a surgeon may be lawful, to keep a vow, in case it is not an evident hazard of life. For Christ seems to allow it, in mentioning it without reproof (Matthew 19:12), if that text is to be understood of castration: but most expositors think it is meant only of a confirmed resolution of chastity: and ordinarily other means may make this needless: and if it is either needless or perilous, it is unlawful without doubt.

2. The second way by which God may dispense with a vow of chastity is by making the marriage of a person become of evident necessity to the public safety. And I am able to discern only one instance that will meet the case; and that is, if a king has vowed chastity, and in case he does not marry, his next heir being a professed enemy of Christianity, the religion, safety, and happiness of the whole nation is evidently in danger of being overthrown. I think the case of such a king is like the case of a father that had vowed never to provide food or clothing for his children: or as if Ahab had vowed that no well should be dug in the land; and when the drought comes, it has become necessary to the saving of the people's lives: or as if the shipmaster should vow that the ship shall not be pumped; which when it leaks does become necessary to save their lives. In these cases God releases you from your vow by a change of the matter; and a pastor may dispense with it declaratively. But for the pope or any mortal man to claim more is impiety and deceit.

*Question.* May the aged marry, who are frigid, impotent, and incapable of procreation? *Answer.* Yes, God has not forbidden them: and there are other lawful ends of marriage, as mutual help and comfort, etc. which may make it lawful. [3]

## Translator's notes

- **Where this file starts and stops:** it begins at a *Question* inside Baxter's discussion of those who are not called by God to marry (Direction 1, "whether to marry"), continuing from the previous file without a new heading; the Direction's heading is in an earlier file. The numbered point "3." (a third sort not called to marry) continues a list whose points 1 and 2 come before this range. The file ends at footnote marker [3]; the rest of Chapter 1 continues in the next file.
- **Labels:** the second and third *Question* paragraphs ("If the question therefore..." and "But if the question is only...") have no *Answer* label in the source; Baxter answers in the same paragraph. None of the *Question* labels in this range are numbered.
- **government** (parents' government; ungoverned; government of God): kept; it means rule or authority over someone.
- **disoblige** (several times): in Baxter, to release someone from an obligation (not, as now, to offend). Rendered **release**.
- **oblige** ("the promise might nevertheless oblige"; "They can oblige themselves"): rendered **bind**.
- **for want of / the want of** became **for lack of / the lack of**; **forbear** became **refrain from**; **so be it** became **provided**; **intimate** became **imply**; **pretence** became **pretext**; **pretend to** became **claim**; **at age** became **of age**; **for aught I ever saw** became **for all I have ever seen**; **raiment** became **clothing**; **digged** became **dug**; **uncapable** became **incapable**; **every thing** became **everything**; **any one** became **anyone**; **not one of a hundred** became **not one in a hundred**.
- **apparent / apparently** (an apparent hazard of life; apparent necessity; apparently in danger): in Baxter's usage, evident, plain. Rendered **evident / evidently**.
- **perfect marriage** became **complete marriage** (Baxter's "perfect" means complete, finished).
- **verbal conjunction; their conjunction:** rendered **verbal union; their union** (a marriage made by words spoken, before it is consummated).
- **reach the case** became **meet the case** (fit it).
- **mutation of the matter** became **change of the matter**.
- **the third chapter of Numbers:** the source reads "third," but the law on a father disallowing a daughter's vow is in Numbers 30 (verses 3-5; Numbers 3 concerns the Levites). Corrected to "thirtieth"; the printed "third" is probably a misprint, but we have not checked another edition.
- **Hence there are two doubts arise:** "that" added for modern grammar ("Hence there are two doubts that arise").
- **I have showed you before:** a cross-reference to an earlier discussion of the same point (probably on vows); we have not located or checked it.
- **the weaker sex:** Baxter's phrase, kept as written. It reflects the view of his day that the Numbers law may be a concession to women's weakness; translated faithfully, not endorsed.
- **Deuteronomy 12:32:** Baxter adapts the verse into his own sentence ("whatsoever God commandeth thee, take heed that thou do it: thou shalt add nothing thereto, nor take ought therefrom"), shifting "I command" to "God commandeth," so it is treated as a paraphrase and kept in his words, modernized, in quotation marks. The NET reads: "You must be careful to do everything I am commanding you. Do not add to it or subtract from it!" (In the NET and the Hebrew numbering this verse is 13:1.) The sense Baxter leans on, adding nothing to God's law, is the same.
- **Latin in the text** (Baxter glosses some of it himself):
  - *actus promittendi:* "the act of promising" (Baxter: "the promise making").
  - *materia promissa:* "the matter promised" (Baxter's gloss).
  - *sui juris:* "of one's own right," legally independent (Baxter: "their own").
  - *reputative nullus vel non actus:* "reckoned as null, or no act at all" (rendered loosely; *reputative* means "by reckoning" or "in account").
  - *ignorantis non est consensus:* "of one who does not know, there is no consent."
  - *per carnalem concubitum:* "by carnal lying-together," that is, sexual intercourse (Baxter: "by the carnal knowledge of each other"). *concubitus:* "lying together, intercourse." *ante concubitum:* "before intercourse."
  - *de futuro:* "for the future." *de præsenti:* "in the present." These are the terms of the old marriage law for a promise to marry later and a present exchange of consent that makes a marriage.
- **carnal knowledge:** kept; it means sexual intercourse. **fornication:** kept.
- **forsake father and mother and cleave to each other:** an unmarked allusion to Genesis 2:24 (also Matthew 19:5); reference not in Baxter's text. NET Genesis 2:24 has "leave his father and mother and unite with his wife." Baxter's wording is kept; "cleave" means hold fast, cling.
- **theologians disagree about what:** for Baxter's "divines are disagreed what."
- **uncleanness:** kept; in Baxter it means sexual impurity.
- **Part 1, Chapter 8, Part 5, Titles 1 and 2:** Baxter's cross-reference ("part i. chap. viii. part v. tit. 1 and 2") to his directions in Part 1 against sins of the flesh; we have not checked that it points to the right place.
- **the help of a surgeon:** Baxter is reporting the view of "some" that castration might be lawful to keep a vow of chastity, short of evident danger to life, and himself calls it unlawful if needless or perilous. This is a 17th-century discussion of medicine and celibacy, translated faithfully.
- **Matthew 19:12:** cited, not quoted. The NET speaks of those "who became eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom of heaven," the phrase Baxter says most expositors take of a resolution of chastity rather than castration.
- **a king who has vowed chastity:** Baxter's one instance of public necessity; his "professed enemy of Christianity" heir is a hypothetical.
- **Ahab:** a hypothetical vow; the drought alludes to 1 Kings 17-18 (reference not in Baxter's text).
- **dispense with it declaratively:** that is, a pastor may declare that the vow no longer binds, without himself having power to release it. Kept.
- **the pope:** Baxter's polemic against papal dispensations from vows, kept as written.
- **frigid, impotent:** Baxter's terms for those unable to have marital relations or children; kept.
- **Footnote [3]:** "mistresses" in the older sense of women loved and courted, not illicit partners; kept. "a quarrel to marry" is rendered "a pretext to marry" (Bacon's "quarrel" means a ground or excuse). The saying is probably from Bacon's essay "Of Marriage and Single Life" (Essay 8 in the numbering of notes [2] and [6]), but we have not confirmed it. Its view of wives is Bacon's, quoted by Baxter, and reads as dated.
