# Part 2, Chapter 1: Direction 2 continued, the burdens of married life (points 12-20) and the marriage of ministers

12. There is a great deal of duty which husband and wife owe to one another; as to instruct, admonish, pray, watch over one another, and to be continual helpers to each other for the sake of their everlasting happiness; and patiently to bear with the infirmities of each other: and to the weak and backward heart of man, the addition of so much duty adds to their weariness, however good the work is in itself: and men should feel their strength before they undertake more work.

13. And the more they love each other, the more they share in each other's griefs; and one or other will be frequently under some sort of suffering. If one is sick, or lame, or in pain, or defamed, or wronged, or disquieted in mind, or by temptation falls into any wounding sin, the other bears part of the distress. Therefore before you undertake to bear all the burdens of another, and suffer in all another's hurts, it concerns you to observe your strength, how much more you have than your own burdens require.

14. And if you should marry one that proves ungodly, how exceedingly great would the affliction be! If you loved them, your souls would be in continual danger by them; they would be the most powerful instruments in the world to pervert your judgments, to deaden your hearts, to take you off from a holy life, to kill your prayers, to corrupt your lives, and to damn your souls. And if you should have the grace to escape the snare, and save yourselves, it would be with so much the greater difficulty and suffering, as the temptation is the greater. And what a heartbreak it would be to associate so closely with a child of the devil, that is likely to lie forever in hell! The daily thoughts of it would be a daily death to you.

15. Women especially must expect so much suffering in a married life that if God had not put into them a natural inclination to it, and so strong a love to their children, as makes them patient under the most annoying troubles, the world would before this have been at an end, through their refusal of so calamitous a life. Their sickness in pregnancy, their pain in giving birth, with the danger of their lives, the tedious trouble night and day which they have with their children in their nursing in their childhood; besides their subjection to their husbands, and continual care of family affairs; being forced to consume their lives in a multitude of lowly and troublesome tasks: all this, and much more, would have utterly deterred that sex from marriage, if nature itself had not inclined them to it.

16. And oh what an abundance of duty is incumbent upon both the parents towards every child for the saving of their souls! [4] What unceasing labor is necessary in teaching them the doctrine of salvation! which made God twice over charge them to teach his word diligently (or sharpen them) "to their children, and to talk of them when they sit in their houses, and when they walk by the way, and when they lie down, and when they rise up" (Deuteronomy 6:6-7; 11:19). What an abundance of obstinate, rooted corruptions are in the hearts of children, which parents must by all possible diligence root up! Oh how great and hard a work it is to speak to them of their sins and Savior, of their God, their souls, and the life to come, with that reverence, gravity, seriousness, and unwearied constancy, as the weight of the matter requires! and to suit all their actions and conduct to the same ends! Little do most that have children know what an abundance of care and labor God will require of them, for the sanctifying and saving of their children's souls. Consider your fitness for so great a work before you undertake it.

17. It is an abundance of affliction that is ordinarily to be expected in the misbehavior of children, when you have done your best, much more if you neglect your duty, as even godly parents too often do. After all your pains, and care, and labor, you must expect that the foolishness of some, and the obstinacy of others, and the unthankfulness of those that you have loved best, should even pierce your hearts. You must expect that many vices should spring up and trouble you; and be the more grievous by how much your children are the more dear. And oh what a grief it is to bring up a child to be a servant of the devil, and an enemy of God and godliness, and a persecutor of the church of God! and to think of his lying in hell forever! And alas! how great is the number of such!

18. And it is not a little care and trouble that servants will put you to; so difficult is it to get those that are good, much more to make them good; so great is your duty, in teaching them, and reminding them of the matters of their salvation; so frequent will be the displeasures about your work and worldly business, and every one of those displeasures will hinder them from receiving your instructions; that most families are houses of correction or affliction.

19. And these marriage hardships are not for a year, but for life; they deprive you of all hope of relief while you live together. There is no room for repentance, nor casting about for a way to escape them. Death only must be your relief. And therefore such a change of your condition should be seriously thought about beforehand, and all the troubles be foreseen and pondered.

20. And if love makes you dear to one another, your parting at death will be the more grievous. And when you first come together, you know that such a parting you must have; through all the course of your lives you may foresee it: one of you must see the body of your beloved turned into a cold and ghastly clod; you must follow it weeping to the grave, and leave it there in dust and darkness; there it must lie rotting as a loathsome lump, whose sight or smell you cannot endure; till you shortly follow it, and lie down yourself in the same condition. All these are the ordinary accompaniments and consequences of marriage; easily and quickly spoken, but long and hard to be endured! No fictions, but realities, and less than most have reason to expect. And should such a life be rashly ventured on in a pang of lust? or such a burden be undertaken without forethought?

*In the margin: Of ministers' marriage.*

But especially the ministers of the gospel should think what they do, and think again, before they enter upon a married life. Not that it is simply unlawful for them, or that they are to be tied from it by a law, as they are in the kingdom of Rome, for carnal ends and with odious effects. But so great a hindrance ordinarily is this troublesome state of life to the sacred ministration which they undertake, that a very clear call should be expected for their satisfaction. So that I am not tedious, consider well only these four things: 1. How well will a life of so much care and business agree with you, that have time little enough for the greater work which you have undertaken? Do you know what you have to do in public and private? in reading, meditating, praying, preaching, instructing personally, and from house to house? And do you know of how great importance it is? even for the saving of men's souls? And have you time to spare for so much worldly cares and business? Are you not charged, "Take pains with these things; be absorbed in them" (1 Timothy 4:15). "No one in military service gets entangled in matters of everyday life; otherwise he will not please the one who recruited him" (2 Timothy 2:4). Is this not plain? Soldiers are not accustomed to look after farms and servants. If you are faithful ministers, I dare confidently say, you will find all your time so little for your proper work that many a time you will groan and say, Oh how short and swift is time! and, Oh how great and slow is my work and duty! 2. Consider how well a life of so great diversions, interruptions, and distractions suits a mind devoted to God, that should be always free and ready for his service. Your studies are on such great and mysterious subjects that they require the whole mind, and all too little. To resolve the many difficulties that are before you, to prepare those suitable convincing words, which may pierce and persuade the hearers' hearts, to get within the bosom of a hypocrite, to follow on the word till it attains its effect, and to deal with poor souls according to their great necessity, and handle God's word according to its holiness and majesty, these are things that require a whole man, and are not employments for a divided or distracted mind. The talking of women, and the crying of children, and the cares and business of the world, are poor preparations or attendants on these studies. [5] 3. Consider well whether a life of so great disturbance is suitable for one whose affections should be taken up for God; and whose work must be all done, not formally and affectedly with the lips alone, but seriously with all the heart. If your heart and warm affections are at any time left behind, the life, and power, the beauty, and glory of your work are lost. How dead will your studies, and praying, and preaching, and conversation be! And can you keep those affections warm and vigorous for God, and taken up with heaven and heavenly things, which are disturbed with the cares and the hardships of the world, and taken up with carnal matters? 4. And consider also how well that needy life will agree with one that by charity and good works should second his doctrine, and win men's souls to the love of holiness. [6] If you do not feed the bodies of the poor, they will less relish the food of the soul. Indeed, if you do not abound above others in good works, the blind, malicious world will see nothing that is good in you; but will say, You have good words, but where are your good works? What an abundance have I known hardened against the gospel and religion, by a common report that these preachers are as greedy, and worldly, and uncharitable as any others! and it must be something extraordinary that must refute such a report. And what an abundance of success have I seen of the labors of those ministers who give all they have in works of charity! And though a rich and resolved man may do some good in a married state, yet commonly it is next to nothing, as to the ends now mentioned; wife, and children, and family necessities devour all, however much you have. And some provision must be made for them when you are dead: and the maintenance of the ministry is not so great as to suffice well for all this, much less for any eminent works of charity besides! Never reckon upon the doing of much good to the poor, if you have wives and children of your own! Such instances are rarities and wonders. All will be too little for yourselves. Whereas if all that were given to the poor which goes to the maintenance of your families, you little know how much it would reconcile the minds of the ungodly, and further the success of your ministerial work.

## Baxter's notes

[4] Are you discontented with your childless state? Remember that of all the Roman kings, not one of them left the crown to his son. Plutarch. de Tranq. Anim. (English: Plutarch, *On Tranquility of Mind*.)

[5] Non bene fit quod occupato animo fit. Hieron. Epist. 5. 3. ad Paulin. (English: What is done with a preoccupied mind is not done well. Jerome, Letter 5, section 3, to Paulinus.)

[6] A single life does well with churchmen: for charity will scarcely water the ground, where it must fill a pool. Lord Bacon, Essay 8. The greatest works and foundations have been from childless men, who have sought to express the image of their minds that have none of their body: so the care of posterity has been most in them that had no posterity. Lord Bacon, Essay 7. He that has a wife and children has given hostages to fortune. For they are impediments to great enterprises.—The best works, and of greatest merit, for the public, have proceeded from unmarried and childless men. Id. ibid. Essay 8. (English: Id. ibid., the same author, the same work.)

## Translator's notes

- **Where this file starts and stops:** it begins partway through Direction 2 ("To restrain your inordinate forwardness to" marriage), at point 12 of Baxter's numbered list of the burdens of married life, so there is no heading; points 1-11 and the Direction's heading are in the previous file. It ends partway through the section on ministers' marriage (after point 4 of his "four things"); the Direction continues in the next file. Not checked against the neighboring files.
- **Deuteronomy 6:6-7; 11:19 (flag):** Baxter adapts the King James verses into the third person ("unto their children ... when they sit in their houses"), so we kept his wording, modernized, in his quotation marks. The NET reads (11:19, and the same in 6:7) "Teach them to your children and speak of them as you sit in your house, as you walk along the road, as you lie down, and as you get up." Baxter's "teach ... diligently (or sharpen them)" rests on the King James "teach them diligently" and the Hebrew's sense of whetting or sharpening (his parenthesis); the NET has only "teach," so the diligence he leans on is not in the NET wording.
- **1 Timothy 4:15 (flag):** NET wording. Baxter's King James text is "Meditate upon these things; give thyself wholly to them." His argument leans on "wholly" (a minister's whole time belongs to his work) and on meditation as part of that work; the NET's "be absorbed in them" keeps the sense of total devotion, but "Take pains with these things" loses "meditate."
- **2 Timothy 2:4:** NET wording, the whole verse as Baxter quotes it. His King James text ("No man that warreth entangleth himself with the affairs of this life; that he may please him who hath chosen him to be a soldier") makes the same point; the NET's "everyday life" for "this life" does not change his argument. His "Are you not charged" question ends with the 1 Timothy quotation and no question mark, as in the source.
- **Point 15 and the ministers' section (dated views):** Baxter's account of women's lot in marriage ("their subjection to their husbands," a life of "lowly and troublesome tasks") and his remark that "the talking of women, and the crying of children" are poor companions to study are his views, translated as he states them.
- **"the kingdom of Rome ... for carnal ends and with odious effects":** Baxter's polemic against the Roman Catholic rule of clerical celibacy, kept as written. "Carnal" is kept (unspiritual, worldly).
- **"houses of correction"** (point 18): kept. In Baxter's day a house of correction was a workhouse-prison for vagrants and petty offenders; his point is that households become places of discipline and trouble.
- **"affections"** (point 3 of the ministers' section): kept, in its older sense of the emotions and desires.
- **"formally and affectedly":** kept; "formally" means as a mere outward form, "affectedly" with artificial show.
- **"convincing words":** kept; here it means words that persuade and convict the hearer.
- **"the maintenance of the ministry":** kept; it means ministers' pay and support.
- **"servants"** (point 18): household servants, who lived in the family in Baxter's day.
- **Word swaps:** "in order to their everlasting happiness" to "for the sake of their everlasting happiness"; "how good soever" to "however good"; "participate in" to "share in"; "pained" to "in pain"; "proveth" to "proves"; "exceeding great" to "exceedingly great"; "powerfulest" to "most powerful"; "by so much the greater" to "with so much the greater"; "what a heart-breaking would it be to converse so nearly with" to "what a heartbreak it would be to associate so closely with" (converse in its older sense, to have dealings with); "like to lie" to "likely to lie"; "ere this" to "before this"; "breeding" to "pregnancy"; "bringing forth" to "giving birth"; "low and troublesome businesses" to "lowly and troublesome tasks"; "uncessant" to "unceasing"; "carriage" to "conduct"; "miscarriages of children" to "misbehavior of children"; "you must look that" to "you must expect that" (twice); "breed up" to "bring up"; "minding them of" to "reminding them of"; "hinder them for receiving" to "hinder them from receiving"; "crosses" to "hardships" (see the glossary); "during life" to "for life"; "forethought on" to "thought about beforehand"; "concomitants and consequents" to "accompaniments and consequences"; "That I be not tedious" to "So that I am not tedious"; "consider well but of these four things" to "consider well only these four things"; "agree to you" to "agree with you" (and "agree to one" to "agree with one"); "use not to look to farms" to "are not accustomed to look after farms"; "avocations" to "interruptions" (things that call one away from one's work, not hobbies); "ill preparations" to "poor preparations"; "be agreeable to" to "is suitable for"; "conference" to "conversation" (see the glossary); "indigent" to "needy"; "Nay" to "Indeed"; "common fame" to "common report," and "confute such fame" to "refute such a report"; "covetous" to "greedy"; "if you have never so much" to "however much you have"; "hinderance" to "hindrance."
- **Kept words:** "displeasures" (point 18: annoyances and quarrels between master and servant); "ministration" (service in the ministry); "second his doctrine" (support his teaching); "eminent" (outstanding); "a pang of lust" (a sudden fit of desire).
- **Points 1-4 of the ministers' section** run on within one paragraph in the source; we keep them in one paragraph with his numbers.
- **Footnote [4]** is attached to point 16 (the duties of parents to children), though its subject is childlessness. "de Tranq. Anim." is the Latin short title of Plutarch's essay *On Tranquility of Mind*; we have not checked the passage.
- **Footnote [5]:** "Hieron." is Jerome; the letter number is Baxter's and we have not checked it against a modern edition of Jerome's letters.
- **Footnote [6]:** "Lord Bacon" is presumably Francis Bacon, and the essays named are probably "Of Marriage and Single Life" (8) and "Of Parents and Children" (7); we have not checked the wording or numbering. The source's "--" between "great enterprises." and "The best works" is kept as a dash; "hardly" is rendered "scarcely."
