# Part 2, Chapter 1: Direction 2, the common drawbacks of marriage (points 1-11)

**Direction 2. To restrain your excessive eagerness for marriage, keep the ordinary inconveniences of it in memory.**

Do not rush into a state of life, the inconveniences of which you never thought about. If you have a call to it, the knowledge of the difficulties and duties will be necessary to your preparation, and faithful undergoing of them; if you have no call, this knowledge is necessary to keep you away. I shall first name the inconveniences common to all, and then some that are particular to the ministers of the gospel, who have a greater reason to avoid a married life than other men have.

1. Marriage ordinarily plunges men into excess of worldly cares; it multiplies their business, and usually their needs. There are many things to attend to and do; there are many to provide for. And many persons you will have to deal with, who all of them have a selfish disposition and interest, and will judge you only according as you fit their ends. And among many persons and businesses, some things will frequently go wrong: you must look for many snags and disappointments. And your natures are not so strong, content, and patient, as to bear all these without disturbance.

2. Your needs in a married state are supplied with more difficulty than in a single life. You will need so many things which before you never needed, and have so many to provide for and satisfy, that all will seem little enough, however much you had. Then you will often be at your wit's end, worrying about the future, what you shall eat, and what you shall drink, and with what you and yours shall be clothed.

3. Your needs in a married state are far harder to bear than in a single state. It is far easier to bear personal needs ourselves than to see the needs of wife and children: affection will make their sufferings pinch you. And self-respect will make it a trouble to your mind to need the help of servants, and to lack what is fit for servants to expect. But especially the discontent and impatience of your family will more discontent you than all their needs. You cannot help your wife, and children, and servants to contented minds. Oh what a heart-cutting trial it is to hear them fretting, grumbling, and complaining! to hear them call for what you do not have for them, and grieve at their condition, and cry out against you, or against the providence of God, because they do not have it! And do not think that riches will free you from these discontents; for as the rich are only few, so those who have much have much to do with it. A great foot must have a great shoe. When poor men need some small supplies, rich men may need great sums, or larger provisions, which the poor can easily do without. And their condition, lifting them up to greater pride, torments them with greater discontents. How few in all the world who have families are content with their circumstances!

4. As a result, a married life contains far more temptations to worldliness or greed than a single state does. For when you think you need more, you will desire more: and when you find all too little to satisfy those whom you provide for, you will measure your means by their desires, and be apt to think that you never have enough. Birds and beasts that have young ones to provide for are most hungry and rapacious. You have so many now to scrape for that you will think you are always in need: it is not only until death that you must now store up; but you must provide for children who survive you. And while you take them to be as yourselves, you now have two generations to make provisions for: and most men are as greedy for their descendants as if it were for themselves.

5. And as a result, you are hindered from works of charity to others: wife and children are the devouring gulf that swallows all. If you had only yourselves to provide for, a little would serve; and you could deny your own desires for unnecessary things; and so might have plentiful provision for good works. But by the time wife and children are provided for, and all their insistent desires satisfied, there is nothing significant left for pious or charitable uses. Sorrowful experience proclaims this.

6. And by this it appears how much a married state ordinarily hinders men from honoring their profession. It is their vows of single life that have led the papists to do so many works of public charity, as are boasted of for the honor of their sect. For when they have no children to bequeath it to, and cannot keep it themselves, it is easy for them to leave it to such uses as will pacify their consciences most, and advance their names. And if it should prove as good a work, and as acceptable to God, to educate your own children piously for his service as to relieve the children of the poor, yet it is not so much regarded in the world, nor does it bring so much honor to religion. One hundred pounds given to the poor will more advance the reputation of your generosity and virtue than a thousand pounds given to your own children, though it is with as pious an end, to train them up for the service of the church. And though this is insignificant as far as your own honor is concerned in it, yet it is significant as far as the honor of religion and the good of souls are concerned in it.

7. And it is no small patience which the natural weakness of the female sex requires you to prepare. Except for a very few who are patient and manlike, women are commonly of powerful imaginations, and sensitive, emotional, impatient spirits, easily cast into anger, or jealousy, or discontent; and of weak understandings, and therefore unable to reform themselves. They are between a man and a child: some few have more of the man, and many have more of the child; but most are only in a middle state. Weakness naturally inclines persons to be peevish and hard to please; as we see in children, old people, and sick persons. They are like a sore, diseased body; you can scarcely touch them but you hurt them. With too many you can scarcely tell how to speak or look but you displease them. If you were very well versed in the art of pleasing, and set yourselves to it with all your care, as if you made it your very business and had little else to do, yet it would tax you hard to please some weak, impatient persons, if not quite surpass your ability and skill. And the more you love them, the more grievous it will be to see them always in discontent, weary of their condition, and to hear the clamorous expressions of their disquieted minds. Indeed, the very multitude of words that very many are addicted to makes some men's lives a continual burden to them. Note what the Scripture says: "It is better to live on a corner of the housetop than to share a house with a quarrelsome wife" (Proverbs 21:9). "It is better to live in the wilderness than with a quarrelsome and easily provoked woman" (verse 19). So chapter 25:24, and 27:15, "A continual dripping on a rainy day—a contentious wife makes herself like that." "I have found only one upright man among a thousand, but I have not found one upright woman among all of them" (Ecclesiastes 7:28).

8. And there is such a meeting of faults and imperfections on both sides that makes it much the harder to bear the infirmities of others rightly. If one party only were peevish and impatient, the steadfastness of the other might make it the more tolerable; but we are all sick, in some measure, of the same disease. And when weakness meets with weakness, and pride with pride, and passion with passion, it aggravates the disease and doubles the suffering. And our corruption is such that, though our intent is to help one another in our duties, yet we are far more apt to stir up one another's disorders.

9. The business, care, and trouble of a married life is a great temptation to call down our thoughts from God, and to divert them from the "one thing necessary" (Luke 10:42); and to distract the mind, and make it unfit for holy duty, and to serve God with a divided heart, as if we did not serve him. How hard it is to pray or meditate with any serious fervency, when you come out of a crowd of cares and business! Hear what Saint Paul says: "I wish that everyone was as I am. ... To the unmarried and widows I say that it is best for them to remain as I am" (1 Corinthians 7:7–8). "Because of the impending crisis I think it best for you to remain as you are. ... But those who marry will face difficult circumstances" (verses 26–28). "And I want you to be free from concern. An unmarried man is concerned about the things of the Lord, how to please the Lord. But a married man is concerned about the things of the world, how to please his wife" (verses 32–33). "An unmarried woman or a virgin is concerned about the things of the Lord, to be holy both in body and spirit. But a married woman is concerned about the things of the world, how to please her husband. I am saying this for your benefit, not to place a limitation on you, but so that without distraction you may give notable and constant service to the Lord" (verses 34–35). "But the man who is firm in his commitment, and is under no necessity but has control over his will, and has decided in his own mind to keep his own virgin, does well. So then, the one who marries his own virgin does well, but the one who does not, does better" (verses 37–38). And note Christ's own words: "The disciples said to him, 'If this is the case of a husband with a wife, it is better not to marry!' He said to them, 'Not everyone can accept this statement, except those to whom it has been given. ... The one who is able to accept this should accept it'" (Matthew 19:11).

10. The business of a married state commonly devours almost all your time, so that little is left for holy contemplations, or serious thoughts of the life to come. All God's service is shrunk and thrust into a corner, and done, as it were, on the side: the world will scarcely allow you time to meditate, or pray, or read the Scripture; you think yourselves (as Martha) under a greater necessity of dispatching your business than of sitting at Christ's feet to hear his word. Oh that single persons knew (for the most part) the preciousness of their leisure, and how free they are to attend the service of God, and learn his word, in comparison with the married!

11. There is so great a diversity of temperaments and degrees of understanding that there are scarcely any two persons in the world but there is some unsuitability between them. Like stones that have some unevenness, which makes them lie crooked in the building; some contrariness there will be, of opinion, or disposition, or interest, or will, by nature, or by custom and education, which will stir up frequent discontents.

## Translator's notes

- **Where this file starts and stops:** it begins with the opening of Direction 2 and ends after point 11 of the drawbacks "common to all"; the next file continues the Direction. The drawbacks Baxter says are particular to ministers of the gospel come later and are not in this range.
- **Direction 2:** the heading sentence is set in bold and the rest of the paragraph follows, as in the other files.
- **inconveniences:** kept. In Baxter's day the word was stronger than now: real disadvantages and troubles, not minor nuisances.
- **call** (Direction 2): a calling from God to the married state.
- **Point 7, on women (dated view):** Baxter's description of "the female sex" (weak by nature, of "weak understandings," "between a man and a child") is translated as he wrote it. It is his view and that of his time, not ours. "Imbecility" is rendered "weakness," its older sense (feebleness), not mental disability.
- **Point 6, the papists (the author's polemic):** "papists" is Baxter's word for Roman Catholics, and his account of why they give so much to public charity (to pacify their consciences and advance their names) is his own polemic, kept as written. "Profession" here means the profession of faith, Christianity as people profess it. "One hundred pounds ... a thousand pounds": English money of the time, kept.
- **Proverbs 21:9:** the King James has "a brawling woman in a wide house"; the NET has "share a house with a quarrelsome wife," so the NET names a wife where the King James has "woman" (which fits Baxter's point about marriage), and the "wide house" is gone.
- **Proverbs 21:19:** NET wording; the King James "contentious and angry woman" becomes "quarrelsome and easily provoked woman."
- **Proverbs 25:24; 27:15:** Baxter cites 25:24 (which repeats 21:9) without quoting it, and quotes 27:15. The NET of 27:15 is a different construction ("a contentious wife makes herself like that") from the King James "a contentious woman are alike"; the sense is the same.
- **Ecclesiastes 7:28 (flag):** the King James reads "one man among a thousand have I found; but a woman among all those have I not found." The NET adds "upright" ("one upright man ... one upright woman"), making explicit what the King James leaves open. Baxter's use (the rarity of a good woman to live with) fits the NET, but the word "upright" is the NET's interpretation, not in his quotation. The verse's first clause ("What I have continually sought, I have not found") is not quoted.
- **Luke 10:42:** "one thing necessary" is Baxter's own phrase from the King James ("one thing is needful"), kept in his quotation marks. The NET reads "but one thing is needed."
- **1 Corinthians 7:7–8:** NET wording. Baxter's "--" skip becomes "..."; it marks the second half of verse 7 ("But each has his own gift from God, one this way, another that"). The King James "It is good for them" is "it is best for them" in the NET.
- **1 Corinthians 7:26–28 (flag):** the King James "present distress" becomes the NET "impending crisis," and "trouble in the flesh" becomes "difficult circumstances." The NET "best for you to remain as you are" replaces "good for a man so to be." Baxter's skip ("--") covers verse 27 and the start of 28; the NET's "and I am trying to spare you such problems" at the end of 28 is also left out, since Baxter stops before it.
- **1 Corinthians 7:32–35 (flag):** the King James "without carefulness" (without anxious cares) becomes the NET "free from concern," which still fits Baxter's argument about the cares of marriage. Baxter's quotation for verses 34–35 begins at "The unmarried woman," leaving out the first words of verse 34; the NET's verse 34 begins "and he is divided," which is left out here too. The NET adds "or a virgin." In verse 35 the King James "for that which is comely, and that ye may attend upon the Lord without distraction" becomes the NET "so that without distraction you may give notable and constant service to the Lord": the King James "comely" (fitting) is gone, but the "without distraction" Baxter leans on remains. The NET's "he is divided" in verse 34 is close to Baxter's own "serve God with a divided heart" earlier in point 9; that may be an echo, but we have not confirmed it.
- **1 Corinthians 7:37–38 (flag):** Baxter's "he that marrieth doth well, but he that marrieth not doth better" is not the King James wording, which reads "he that giveth her in marriage ... he that giveth her not in marriage"; Baxter may be following another version or adapting it. The NET reads "the one who marries his own virgin does well, but the one who does not, does better," taking the passage as about a man and his betrothed. Baxter uses the verse as a general statement that marrying is good and not marrying better; the NET's "his own virgin" narrows it.
- **Matthew 19:11:** Baxter's reference is kept as printed, but his quotation runs from verse 10 (the disciples' words) through verse 12. His "--" skip becomes "..."; it covers most of verse 12 (the three kinds of eunuchs). In the NET the disciples' words are "it is better not to marry!" (King James "it is not good to marry").
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "what you shall eat, and what you shall drink, and with what you and yours shall be clothed" (Matthew 6:31; NET "What will we eat?' or 'What will we drink?' or 'What will we wear?"); Martha, and "sitting at Christ's feet to hear his word" (Luke 10:39–40).
- **"A great foot must have a great shoe":** a proverb, kept.
- **Point 11:** the sentence beginning "Like stones" has no main verb before the semicolon in the source; kept as printed.
- **Word swaps:** "inordinate forwardness to" to "excessive eagerness for"; "keep you off" to "keep you away"; "proper to" to "particular to"; "wants" (noun) to "needs" throughout, and "want" (verb) to "need" or "lack" by context (see the glossary); "mind" to "attend to"; "have to do with" to "deal with"; "judge of you but according as" to "judge you only according as"; "fall cross" to "go wrong"; "rubs" to "snags"; "molestation" to "disturbance"; "hardlier supplied" to "supplied with more difficulty," and "far hardlier borne" to "far harder to bear"; "content" (verb) to "satisfy"; "if you had never so much" to "however much you had"; "taking thought for" to "worrying about" (as in the King James "take no thought"); "wherewith shall you and yours be clothed" to "with what you and yours shall be clothed"; "ingenuity" to "self-respect" (its older sense, a free-born, honorable disposition that is ashamed to depend on others; our best guess at Baxter's meaning here); "repining" to "fretting"; "murmuring" to "grumbling"; "exclaim of" to "cry out against"; "estates" to "circumstances" (point 3) and "estate" to "means" (point 4); "Hereupon" to "As a result"; "covetousness/covetous" to "greed/greedy"; "lay up" to "store up"; "posterity" to "descendants"; "importunate" to "insistent"; "considerable/inconsiderable" to "significant/insignificant"; "Lamentable" to "Sorrowful"; "occasioned" to "led"; "liberality" to "generosity"; "imbecility" to "weakness"; "potent fantasies" to "powerful imaginations"; "tender, passionate" to "sensitive, emotional"; "betwixt" to "between"; "froward" to "peevish"; "distempered" to "diseased"; "put you hard to it" to "tax you hard"; "still in discontents" to "always in discontent"; "disquiet minds" to "disquieted minds"; "Nay" to "Indeed"; "Mark" to "Note"; "exasperateth" to "aggravates" (its older sense, make worse); "distempers" to "disorders"; "undisposed" to "unfit"; "contracted" to "shrunk"; "on the by" to "on the side"; "despatching" to "dispatching"; "unsuitableness" to "unsuitability"; "crossness" to "contrariness."
