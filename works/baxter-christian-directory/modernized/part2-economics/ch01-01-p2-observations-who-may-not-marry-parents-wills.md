# Part 2, Chapter 1: Observations on the reasons for marrying; who may not marry; parents' wills

*In the margin: Observations.*

Here it must be carefully observed, 1. That the two first reasons for marriage, (concupiscence and the will of parents,) or any suchlike, have their strength only in subordination to the third (the final cause, or interest of God and our salvation). And that this last reason (from the end) is of itself sufficient without any of the others, but none of the others are sufficient without this. If it is clear that in a married state you have better advantages for the service of God, and doing good to others, and saving your own souls, than you can have in a single state of life, then it is undoubtedly your duty to marry; for our obligation to seek our ultimate end is the most constant, indispensable obligation. Though parents do not command it, though you have no bodily necessity, yet it is a duty if it certainly makes most for your ultimate end. 2. But yet observe also, that no pretext of your ultimate end itself will warrant you to marry, when any other circumstance has first made it a thing unlawful, while that circumstance continues. For we must not do evil that good may come by it. Our salvation is not furthered by sin; and though we saw a probability that we might do more good to others, if we only committed such a sin to accomplish it, yet it is not to be done. For our lives and mercies being all in the hand of God, and the success and acceptance of all our endeavors depending wholly upon him, it can never be a rational way to attain them, by willfully offending him by our sin! It is a likely means to public good for able and good men to be magistrates and ministers; and yet he who would lie, or be perjured, or commit any known sin that he may be a magistrate, or that he may preach the gospel, might better expect a curse on himself and his endeavors, than God's acceptance, or his blessing and success; so he who would sin to change his state for the better, would find that he changed it for the worse: or if it does good to others, he may expect no good but ruin to himself, if repentance does not prevent it. 3. Observe also, that if the question is only which state of life it is (married or single) that best contributes to this ultimate end, then any one of the subordinate reasons will prove that we have a call, if there are not greater reasons on the contrary side. As in case you have no bodily necessity, the will of parents alone may oblige you, if there is no greater thing against it: or if parents do not oblige you, yet bodily necessity alone may do it: or if neither of these invites you, yet a clear probability of the attaining of such an estate or opportunity, as may make you more fit to relieve many others, or be useful to the church, or the blessing of children who may be devoted to God, may warrant your marriage, if no greater reasons lie against it; for when the scales are equal, any one of these may turn them.

*In the margin: Who may not marry.*

By this also you may perceive who they are that have no call to marry, and to whom it is a sin. As, 1. No man has a call to marry, who laying all the advantages and disadvantages together, may clearly discern that a married state is likely to be a greater hindrance of his salvation, or to his serving or honoring God in the world, and so to disadvantage him as to his ultimate end.

*Question.* But what if parents do command it? or will turn against me if I disobey?

*Answer.* Parents have no authority to command you anything against God or your salvation, or your ultimate end. Therefore here you owe them no formal obedience: but yet the will of parents, with all the consequences, must be put into the scales with all other considerations, and if they make the disadvantages of a single life become the greater, as to your end, then they may bring you under a duty or obligation to marry; not *necessitate præcepti*, as obedience to their command; but *necessitate medii*, as a means to your ultimate end, and in obedience to that general command of God, which requires you to "above all pursue" your ultimate end, even "his kingdom and righteousness" (Matthew 6:33).

*Question.* But what if I have a bodily necessity, and yet I can foresee that marriage will greatly disadvantage me as to the service of God and my salvation?

*Answer.* 1. You must understand that no bodily necessity is absolute: for there is no man so lustful but may possibly bridle his lust by other lawful means; by diet, labor, sober company, diverting business, solitude, watching the thoughts and senses, or at least by the physician's help; so that the necessity is only *secundum quid*, or an urgency rather than a simple necessity. And then, 2. This measure of necessity must itself be laid in the balance with the other circumstances; and if this necessity will turn the scales by making a single life more disadvantageous to your ultimate end, your lust being a greater impediment to you than all the inconveniences of marriage will be, then the case is resolved, "it is better to marry than to burn with sexual desire." But if the hindrances in a married state are likely to be greater than the hindrances of your concupiscence, then you must set yourself to the curbing and curing of that concupiscence; and in the use of God's means expect his blessing.

*In the margin: Of parents' wills.*

2. Children are not, ordinarily, called by God to marry, when their parents absolutely and peremptorily forbid it. For though parents' commands cannot make it a duty, when we are sure it would hinder the interest of God our ultimate end; yet parents' prohibitions may make it a sin, when there is a clear probability that it would most contribute to our ultimate end, were it not prohibited. Because, (1.) Affirmatives do not bind *semper et ad semper*, as negatives or prohibitions do. (2.) Because the sin of disobedience to parents will counter its tendency to good, and do more against our ultimate end than all the advantages of marriage can do for it. A duty is then to us no duty, when it cannot be performed without a chosen, willful sin. In many cases we are bound to refrain from what a governor forbids, when we are not bound to do the contrary if he commands it. It is easier to make a duty to be no duty, than to make a sin to be no sin. One bad ingredient may turn a duty into a sin, when one good ingredient will not turn a sin into a duty, or into no sin.

*Question.* But may not a governor's prohibition be outweighed by some great degrees of hardship? It is better to marry than to burn. 1. What if parents forbid children to marry absolutely until death, and so deprive them of the lawful remedy against lust? 2. And if they do not do so, yet if they forbid it to them when it is to them most seasonable and necessary, it seems little better. 3. Or if they forbid them to marry where their affections are so engaged that they cannot be taken off without their mutual ruin? May not children marry in such cases of necessity as these, without and against the will of their parents?

*Answer.* I cannot deny that some cases may be imagined or occur, in which it is lawful to do what a governor forbids, and to marry against the will of parents: for they have their power for edification, and not for destruction. As if a son is qualified with eminent gifts for the work of the ministry, in a time and place that needs much help; if a hostile parent, in hatred of that sacred office, should ever so peremptorily forbid him, yet the son may devote himself to the blessed work of saving souls: even as a son may not refrain from relieving the poor (with that which is his own) though his parents should forbid him; nor refrain from putting himself into a position to relieve them in the future; nor go without his own necessary food and clothing though he is forbidden: as Daniel would not refrain from praying openly in his house, when he was forbidden by the king and law. When any inseparable circumstance makes a thing, of itself indifferent, become a duty, a governor's prohibition will not discharge us from that duty, unless the circumstance is smaller than the circumstance of the ruler's prohibition, and then it may be outweighed by it; but to determine what circumstances are greater or less is a difficult task.

And as to the particular questions, to the first I answer, If parents forbid their children to marry while they live, it is fitting and safe to obey them until death, if no greater obligation to the contrary forbids it: but it is necessary to obey them during the time that the children live under the government of their parents, as in their houses, in their younger years (except in some few extraordinary cases). But when parents are dead, (though they leave commands in their wills,) or when age or former marriage has removed children from under their government, a smaller matter will serve to justify their disobedience here than when the children in minority are less fit to govern themselves. For though we owe parents a limited obedience always, yet at full age the child is more at his own disposal than he was before. Nature has given us a hint of her intention in the instinct of animals, who are all taught to protect, and lead, and provide for their young ones, while the young are insufficient for themselves; but when they are grown to self-sufficiency, they drive them away or neglect them. If a wise son who has a wife and many children, and great affairs to manage in the world, should be bound to as absolute obedience to his aged parents as he was in his childhood, it would ruin their affairs, and parents' government would pull down that in their old age which they built up in their middle age.

And to the second question I answer, that, 1. Children who claim unconquerable lust or love must do all they can to subdue such inordinate affections, and bring their lusts to stoop to reason and their parents' wills. And if they do their best, there are either none, or not one of many hundreds, but may maintain their chastity together with their obedience. 2. And if any say, I have done my best, and yet am under a necessity of marriage; and am I not then bound to marry though my parents forbid me? I answer, it is not to be believed: either you have not done your best, or else you are not under a necessity. And your urgency being your own fault, (seeing you should subdue it,) God still obliges you both to subdue your vice, and to obey your parents. 3. But if there should be any one who has such an (incredible) necessity of marriage, he is to get some others to solicit his parents for their consent, and if he cannot obtain it, some say it is his duty to marry without it: I should rather say that it is *minus malum*, the lesser evil: and that having cast himself into some necessity of sinning, it is still his duty to avoid both, and to choose neither; but it is the smaller sin to choose to disobey his parents, rather than to live in the flames of lust and the filth of unchastity. And some theologians say that in such a case a son should appeal to the magistrate, as a superior authority above the father. But others think, 1. That this leaves it as difficult to resolve what he shall do, if the magistrate also does not consent: and, 2. That it only resolves one difficulty by a greater; it being very doubtful whether in domestic cases the authority of the parent or the magistrate is the greater.

3. The same answer serves as to the third question, when parents forbid you to marry the persons that you are most fond of. For such fondness (whether you call it lust or love) as will not stoop to reason and your parents' wills is inordinate and sinful. And therefore the thing that God binds you to is by his appointed means to subdue it, and to obey: but if you cannot, the circumstances and probable consequences must tell you which is the lesser evil.

## Translator's notes

- **Where this file starts and stops:** it begins partway through Direction 1 (whether to marry), in the middle of Baxter's answer to the question how one may know whether God calls him to marry; the "Observations" follow his list of reasons for marrying in the previous file. It ends partway through his numbered list of those who have no call to marry (points 1 and 2 are here, with the questions under point 2); the next file continues it. No heading has been added.
- **Matthew 6:33:** Baxter (King James) quotes "seek first ... the kingdom of God, and his righteousness." The NET reads "above all pursue his kingdom and righteousness." The sense Baxter uses (that God's kingdom is to be sought before everything else) is kept, but the NET's "pursue" replaces "seek," and its "his kingdom" replaces "the kingdom of God"; the quotation marks follow the NET's word order, which makes the sentence read a little awkwardly.
- **"It is better to marry than to burn"** (1 Corinthians 7:9, not cited in the text): in the first answer Baxter puts it in quotation marks, so the NET wording is used: "it is better to marry than to burn with sexual desire." The NET makes explicit what the King James "burn" leaves implied; this matches Baxter's own reading (lust). The second use, in the third Question, is unmarked in the source and is kept in Baxter's wording.
- **Unmarked allusions** (references not in the text): "we must not do evil that good may come by it" (Romans 3:8); "they have their power for edification, and not for destruction" (2 Corinthians 10:8; 13:10, where the NET has "for building up, not for tearing down"); Daniel praying openly though forbidden by the king and law (Daniel 6).
- **Latin in the text** (draft translations): *necessitate præcepti* (by necessity of precept, that is, because it is commanded); *necessitate medii* (by necessity of means, that is, because it is a needed means to the end); *secundum quid* (in a certain respect, relatively, as opposed to absolutely); *semper et ad semper* (always and at every moment: the scholastic maxim that positive commands bind always but not at every moment, while prohibitions bind at every moment); *minus malum* (the lesser evil, which Baxter glosses himself).
- **Kept terms:** "concupiscence" (sexual desire, lust); "final cause" (the end or purpose for which a thing is done, a scholastic term); "affections" and "inordinate affections" (emotions and desires, here disordered ones); "indifferent" (morally neutral in itself, neither commanded nor forbidden); "call" (God's calling to a state of life).
- **Word swaps:** "accident(s)" to "circumstance(s)" (Baxter uses the philosophical sense: a circumstance attached to an act that is not part of its nature); "corporal necessity" to "bodily necessity"; "conduceth" to "contributes"; "commodities and discommodities" to "advantages and disadvantages"; "incommodity" to "hardship"; "set against me" to "turn against me"; "pretence" to "pretext," and "pretend to" to "claim"; "serviceable" to "useful"; "malignant parent" to "hostile parent" ("malignant" in Baxter's day was also a party label for those hostile to the godly; the sense here is hostility to the ministry); "brutes" to "animals"; "convenient" to "fitting"; "procure" to "get"; "capacity" to "position"; "forbear" to "refrain from" or "go without"; "raiment" to "clothing"; "cross the tendency of it unto good" to "counter its tendency to good"; "obedience still" to "obedience always" ("still" meaning continually).
- **Source oddity:** "should he bound to as absolute obedience" in the source is a misprint for "should be bound"; rendered "should be bound."
- **Dated views:** Baxter's view that hardly anyone who does his best is unable to remain chaste without marrying, and that a child's "unconquerable" love is his own fault, is translated as he states it.
