# Chapter 8: Directions 8–11 (sense ruled, but not denied)

**Direction 8. You may know whether sense, or faith and reason, is the chief in government, by knowing which of their objects is made your chief end, and accounted your best, and loved, and delighted in, and sought accordingly.**

If the objects of sense are thus taken for your best and end, then certainly sense is the chief in government; but if the objects of faith and reason, even God and life eternal, are taken for your best and end, then faith and reason are the ruling power. Though you should use ever so great understanding and shrewdness for physical things (as riches, and honor, and worldly greatness, or fleshly delights), this does not prove that reason is the ruling power; but proves the more strongly that sense is the conqueror, and that reason is depraved and captivated by it, and truckles under it, and serves it as a voluntary slave. And the greater is your learning, wit, and abilities, and the nobler your education, the greater is the victory and dominion of sense, that can subdue, and rule, and serve itself by abilities so noble.

*In the margin: Do not deny sense with the papists.*

**Direction 9. Though sense must be thus absolutely ruled, its proper power must neither be disabled, prohibited, nor denied.**

You must keep your horse strong and able for his work, though not headstrong and unruly; and you must not keep him from the use of his strength, though you do not grant him the government. Nor will you deny that he may be stronger than the rider, though the rider has the ruling power: he has more of the power called δυναμις, natural power, though the εξουσια is yours. So it is here: 1. No man must destroy his bodily sense; the quickest sense is the best servant to the soul, if it is not headstrong and too impetuous. The body must be struck so far as to be "kept under and brought into subjection" (1 Corinthians 9:27); but not be disabled from its service to the soul. 2. Nor must we forbid or refrain from the exercise of the senses, in subordination to the exercise of the inferior senses (Hebrews 4:14). It is indeed a smaller loss to part with a right hand or a right eye than with our salvation; but that does not prove that we are put to such straits as to be forced to either (unless persecution puts us to it). 3. Nor must we deny the certainty of the sensory apprehension, when it keeps its place; as the papists do, that affirm it necessary to salvation to believe that the sight, and taste, and smell, and feeling of all men in the world that take the sacrament are certainly deceived, in taking that to be bread and wine which is not so. For if all the senses of all men, though ever so sound and rational, are certainly deceived in this, we do not know when they are not deceived, and there can be no certainty of faith or knowledge: for if you say that the church tells us that sense is deceived in this, and only in this, I answer, If it is not first granted that sense (as so stated) is certain in its apprehension, there is no certainty then that there is a church, or a man, or a world, or what the church ever said, or any member of it. And if sense is so fallible, the church may be deceived, who by the means of sense comes to all her knowledge. To deny faith is the mark of an unbeliever; to deny reason is to deny humanity, and is fittest for a madman, or a beast (if without reason, reason could be denied); but to deny the certainty of sense itself, and of all the senses of all sound men, and that about the proper objects of sense, this shows that ambition can make a religion which shall bring man quite below the beasts, and make him a mushroom, so that Rome may have subjects capable of her government; and all this under pretense of honoring faith, and saving souls; making God the destroyer of nature in order to its perfection, and the deceiver of nature in order to its edification.

**Direction 10. Sense must not be made the judge of matters that are above it, as the proper objects of faith and reason; nor must we argue negatively from our senses in such cases, which God in nature never brought into their court.**

We cannot say that there is no God, no heaven, no hell, no angels, no souls of men, because we do not see them. We cannot say, I do not see the antipodes, nor other kingdoms of the world, and therefore there is no such place: so we say, as well as the papists, that sense is no judge whether the spiritual body of Christ is present in the sacrament, any more than whether an angel is here present. But sense with reason is the judge whether bread and wine are there present, or else human understanding can judge of nothing. Christ would have had Thomas believe without seeing and feeling, and blesses those that neither see him nor feel, and yet believe; but he never blesses men for believing contrary to the sight, and feeling, and taste, and all that have sound senses and understandings in the world. Their instance of the Virgin's conception of Christ is nothing contrary to this; for it does not belong to sense to judge whether a virgin may conceive. Nor will any wise man's reason judge that the Creator, who in making the world from nothing was the only cause, cannot supply the place of a partial second cause in generation: they might more plausibly argue with Aristotle against the creation itself, that *ex nihilo nihil fit*; but as it is beyond doubt that the infallibility of sense is not at all concerned in this, so it is sufficiently proved by Christians that God can create without any pre-existent matter. Reason can see much further than sense by the help of sense; and yet much further by the help of divine revelation by faith. To argue negatively against the conclusions of reason or divine revelation, from the mere negation of sensory apprehension, is to make a beast of man. We must not be so irrational or impious as to say that there is nothing but what we have seen, or felt, or tasted, etc. If we will believe others who have seen them, that there are other parts of the world, we have full reason to believe the sealed testimony of God himself, that there are such superior worlds and powers as he has told us. We have the use of sense in hearing, or seeing God's revelation; and we have no more in receiving man's report of those countries which we never saw.

If they will make it the question whether the sense may not be deceived; I answer, we do not doubt that by distance of the objects, or disorders, or disproportions of itself or the media, it may: but if the sense itself, and all the means and objects, have their natural soundness, aptitude, and disposition, it is a contradiction to say it is deceived; for that is to say it is not the sense which we suppose it is. If God deceives it thus, he makes it another thing. It is no longer the same, nor will admit the same definition. But, however, it is most evident that, the senses being the first entrance or inlet of knowledge, the first certainty must be there, which is presupposed to the certain judgment of the intellect; but if these err, all following certainty which supposes the certainty of the senses is destroyed; and this error in the first reception (like an error in the first digestion) is not rectified by the second. And if God should thus leave all men under a fallibility of sense, he would leave no certainty in the world; and I desire those that know the definition of a lie to consider whether this is not to imagine God to lie in the very frame of nature, and by constant lies to rule the world, when yet it is impossible for God to lie. And if this blasphemy were granted them, yet it would be man's duty still to judge by such senses as he has about the objects of sense; for if God has made them fallible, we cannot make them better; nor can we create a reason in ourselves which shall not presuppose the judgment of sense, or which shall supply its ordinary, natural defects. So that the Roman faith of transubstantiation, denying the reality of bread and wine, does not only unman the world, but bring man lower than a beast, and make sense to be no sense, and the world to be governed by natural deceit or lies, and banish all certainty of faith and reason from the earth. And after all (with such astonishing enmity to charity as makes man more like the devil than else could easily be believed), they sentence all to hell that do not believe this; and decree to burn them first on earth, and to depose temporal lords from their dominions that favor them, or that will not exterminate them from their lands, and so absolve their subjects from their allegiance, and give their dominions to others. All this you may read in the third canon of the Lateran general council under Innocent III.

**Direction 11. Do not look upon any object of sense with sense alone, nor stop in it, but let reason begin where sense ends, and always see by faith or reason the part which is invisible, as well as the sensible part by sense.**

By that which is seen, infer and rise up to that which is unseen. If God had given us an eye, or ear, or taste, or feeling, and not a mind, then we would have exercised no other faculty but what we had. But surely he that has given us the higher faculty requires that we use it as well as the lower. And remember that they are not mere coordinate faculties, but the sensory faculty is subordinate to the intellectual: and accordingly that which the sensible creature objectively reveals through the sense to the intellect is something to which things sensible are subordinate. Therefore if you stop in sensible things, and do not see the principle which animates them, the power which orders and rules them, and the end which they are made for, and must be used for, you play the beasts; you see nothing but a dead carcass without the soul, and nothing but a useless, senseless thing. You know nothing indeed to any purpose; no, not the creature itself; while you do not know the use and meaning of the creature, but separate it from its life, and guide, and end.

## Translator's notes

- **Range:** this file begins at the start of Direction 8 and ends at the close of Direction 11; the next file continues Part 1.
- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **1 Corinthians 9:27 (flag):** Baxter's "kept under and brought into subjection" is the King James ("I keep under my body, and bring it into subjection"), turned into the passive to fit his sentence. We kept his wording in his quotation marks. The NET reads "I subdue my body and make it my slave." The sense he uses (the body disciplined but not destroyed) survives, but the NET's "make it my slave" is stronger than "bring into subjection."
- **Hebrews 4:14 (probable misprint, kept):** Hebrews 4:14 is about the great high priest and has nothing on the senses. Baxter probably meant Hebrews 5:14, which in the King James speaks of those "who by reason of use have their senses exercised to discern both good and evil" (NET: "whose perceptions are trained by practice to discern both good and evil"). We are not certain, so we kept his reference. Note also that the NET's "perceptions" loses the word "senses," which is the point Baxter would be leaning on.
- **"in subordination to the exercise of the inferior senses":** printed so in the source. The phrase reads oddly (the senses subordinated to "the inferior senses"); it may be a misprint for something like "the superior faculties." We kept it as printed and have not checked another edition.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording, no reference in his text): "a right hand or a right eye" (Matthew 5:29–30); Thomas and the blessing on those who believe without seeing (John 20:24–29; NET 20:29: "Blessed are the people who have not seen and yet have believed"); "it is impossible for God to lie" (compare Hebrews 6:18 and Titus 1:2). The Hebrews wording happens to match the NET; we have not put it in quotation marks because Baxter did not.
- **Greek (Direction 9):** δυναμις (dynamis), power or strength, which Baxter glosses as "natural power"; εξουσια (exousia), authority or rightful power. Printed without accents in the source; kept as printed. The point: the horse may have more strength, but the rider has the authority.
- **ex nihilo nihil fit** (Direction 10): Latin, "nothing comes from nothing." Baxter sets it against Aristotle; the maxim is widely associated with ancient Greek natural philosophy generally. We make no further claim about its source.
- **papists; Rome; "the Roman faith of transubstantiation":** Baxter's terms and his polemic against the Roman Church, kept as he wrote them. "Papist" is his word for a Roman Catholic.
- **Lateran council:** Baxter means the general council held at the Lateran under Pope Innocent III, probably the Fourth Lateran Council (1215), though we have not confirmed this. The description of its contents is Baxter's; we have not checked it against the canon's text.
- **exterminate:** kept. In Baxter's day it could mean "drive out, banish" (from the Latin *exterminare*), which may be his sense here ("exterminate them from their lands").
- **mushroom:** kept. Baxter's image of man reduced to something lower than a beast, a growth without senses or reason. In his period "mushroom" could also mean an upstart or a worthless person; we do not know which shade he intends.
- **Word swaps (Baxter's word, then ours):** chiefest, chief; policy, shrewdness; sensual, physical; parts, abilities (twice); never so, ever so; deny but that, deny that; stricken, struck; forbear, refrain from; necessitated to, forced to; sensitive (apprehension, faculty), sensory; property (of an infidel), mark; infidel, unbeliever; that (Rome may have), so that; no more than, any more than; past doubt, beyond doubt; distempers, disorders; no more the same, no longer the same; concoction, digestion; feign, imagine; wonderful, astonishing; liker, more like; collect, infer; should have exercised, would have exercised.
- **Kept and explained:** *truckles under* (submits meekly to); *wit* (intelligence); *the media* (in the theory of perception of his day, what lies between the object and the sense organ, such as air or light); *the first concoction* (rendered "digestion": the older physiology counted several stages of digestion, and an error in the first was not fixed by the later ones; this is Baxter's analogy, not a medical claim of ours); *sensible* (perceptible by the senses, not "reasonable"); *creature* (any created thing, not only an animal); *objectively* (as an object presented to the mind); *coordinate* (of equal rank); *in generation* (in begetting a child); *a partial second cause* (a secondary, created cause, here the human father, as against God the first cause).
- **who** (of the church, Direction 9): kept as Baxter has it, since he personifies the church ("her knowledge").
