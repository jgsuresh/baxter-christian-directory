# Chapter 3: Grand Direction XIV (part 2 of 2)

*This section continues Grand Direction 14 from part 1 (on thankfulness for mercies). It begins at the start of Direction 7 and carries the numbered Directions through Direction 18.*

**Direction 7. Think much of those personal mercies which God has shown you from your youth up until now, by which he has manifested his care of you, and particular kindness to you.**

Though the common mercies of God's servants are the greatest, which all other Christians share in with each one; yet personal favors peculiar to ourselves are apt much to affect us, as being close to our awareness, and expressing a peculiar care and love of us. Therefore Christians should mark God's dealings with them, and write down the great and notable mercies of their lives (which are not unfit for others to know, if they should see it).

**Direction 8. Compare your proportion of mercies with the rest of the people's in the world. And you will find that it is not one of many thousands that has your proportion.**

It is so small a part of the world that are Christians, and of those so few that are orthodox, reformed Christians, and of those so few that are seriously godly, as devoted to God, and of those so few that do not fall into some perplexities, errors, scandals, or great afflictions and distress, that those few that are in none of these ranks have cause of wondrous thankfulness to God; yes, the most afflicted Christians in the world. Suppose God had divided his mercies equally to all men in the world, such as health, and wealth, and honor, and grace, and the gospel, etc.; how little of them would have come to your share in comparison of what you now possess! how many have less wealth or honor than you! how many thousands have less of gospel and of grace! In reason therefore your thankfulness should be proportionate and extraordinary.

**Direction 9. Compare the mercies which you lack with those which you possess, and observe how much greater your receivings are than your sufferings.**

You have many meals' plenty for one day of scarcity or pinching hunger; you have many days' health for one day's sickness: and if one part is ill, there are more that are not; if one cross befalls you, you escape many more that might befall you, and which you deserve.

**Direction 10. Consider how you would value your mercies, if you were deprived of them.**

The lack of them usually teaches us most effectively to esteem them. Think how you would value Christ and hope, if you were in despair! and how you would value the mercies of earth, if you were in hell! and the mercies of England, if you were among bloody inquisitors and persecutors, and wicked, cruel heathens or Muslims, or brutish, savage Americans! Think how good sleep would seem to you, if you could not sleep for pain! or how good your food, or drink, or clothes, or house, or means of support, or friends, would all seem to you, if they were taken from you! and how great a mercy health would seem, if you were under some tormenting sickness! and what a mercy time would seem, if death were at hand, and time were ending! and what a mercy your least sincere desires, or measure of grace, is, in comparison of the case of those who are the haters, despisers, and persecutors of holiness! These thoughts, if followed home, may shame you into thankfulness.

**Direction 11. Let heaven be ever in your eye, and still think of the endless joy which you will have with Christ.**

For that is the mercy of all mercies; and he that has not that in hope to be thankful for will never be thankful aright for anything; and he that has heaven in promise to be thankful for has still reason for the highest, joyful thanks, whatever worldly thing he lacks, or though he were sure never more to have comfort in any creature upon earth. He is unthankful indeed that will not be thankful for heaven; but that is a mercy which will constrain to thankfulness, so far as our title is discerned. The more believing and heavenly the mind is, the more thankful.

**Direction 12. Look on earthly and present mercies in connection with heaven, which is their end, and as sweetened by our interest in God who gives them.**

You leave out all the life and sweetness, which must cause your thankfulness, if you leave out God and overlook him. A dead carcass does not have the loveliness or usefulness of a living man. You mortify your mercies when you separate them from God and heaven, and then their beauty, and sweetness, and excellence are gone; and how can you be thankful for the husks and shells, when you foolishly neglect the kernel? Take every bit as from your Father's hands: remember that he feeds, and clothes, and protects you, as his child: it is to "Our Father in heaven" that we must go every day for our "daily bread." Taste his love in it, and you will say that it is sweet. Remember where all his mercies tend, and where they will leave you, even in the bosom of Eternal love. Think with yourself, how good is this with the love of God! this and heaven are full enough for me. Coarse fare, and coarse clothing, and coarse treatment in the world, and hard labor, and a poor home, with heaven after all, is mercy beyond all human estimation or conceiving. Nothing can be little which is a token of the love of God, and leads to eternal glory. The relation to heaven is the life and glory of every mercy.

**Direction 13. Think often how great a mercy it is that thankfulness for mercy is made so great a part of your duty.**

Is it not the sweetest employment in the world to be always thinking on so sweet a thing as the mercies of God, and to be mentioning them with glad and thankful hearts? Is not this a sweeter kind of work than to be abusing mercy, and casting it away upon fleshly lusts, and sinning it away, and turning it against us? Yes, is it not a sweeter work than to be groaning under sin and misery? If God had as much fixed your thoughts upon saddening, heart-breaking objects as he has (by his commands) upon reviving and delighting objects, you might have thought religion a melancholy life. But when sorrow is required only as preparatory to delight, and cheerful thanksgiving is made the life and sum of your religion, who but a monster will think it grievous to live in thankfulness to our great Benefactor? To think thus of the sweetness of it will do much to incline us to it, and make it easy to us.

**Direction 14. Make conscience ordinarily of allowing God's mercies as great a room in your thoughts and prayers as you allow to your sins, and wants, and troubles.**

In a day of humiliation, or after some notable fall into sin, or in some special cases of distress, I confess sin and danger may have the greater share. But ordinarily, mercy should take up more time in our remembrance and confession than our sins. Let the reasons of it first convince you, that this is your duty; and, when you are convinced, hold yourselves to the performance of it. If you cannot be so thankful as you desire, yet spend as much time in the confessing of God's mercy to you as in confessing your sins and mentioning your wants. Thanksgiving is an effectual petitioning for more: it shows that the soul is not drowned in selfishness, but would carry the fruit of all his mercies back to God. If you cannot think on mercy so thankfully as you would, yet see that it has a due proportion of your thoughts. This course (of allowing mercy its due time in our thoughts and prayers) would work the soul to greater thankfulness by degrees. Whereas, on the contrary, when men accustom themselves to have ten words or twenty of confession and petition for one of thanksgiving, and ten thoughts of sins, and wants, and troubles for one of mercies, this starves thankfulness and turns it out of doors. You can command your words and thoughts if you will; resolve, therefore, on this duty.

**Direction 15. Take heed of a proud, a greedy, a fleshly, or a discontented mind; for all these are enemies to thankfulness.**

A proud heart thinks itself the worthiest for more, and thinks diminutively of all. A greedy heart is always gaping after more, and never returning the fruit of what it has received. A fleshly mind is an insatiable gulf of bodily mercies; like a greedy dog that is gaping for another bone when he has devoured one, and sacrifices all to his belly, which is his god (Philippians 3:18). A discontented mind is always murmuring and never pleased, but finds something still to quarrel at; and takes more notice of the denying of its unjust desires than of the giving of many undeserved mercies. Thankfulness does not prosper where these vices prosper.

**Direction 16. Avoid as much as may be a melancholy and over-fearful temper; for that will not allow you to see or taste your greatest mercies, nor to be glad or thankful for anything you have, but is still representing all things to you in a terrible or lamentable shape.**

The grace of thankfulness may be habitually in a timorous, melancholy mind; and that appears in their valuation of the mercy. How glad and thankful would they be, if they were assured that the love of God is toward them! But it is next to impossible for them ordinarily to exercise thankfulness, because they cannot believe anything of themselves that is good and comforting. It is as natural for them to be always fearing, and despairing, and complaining, and troubling themselves, as for perverse children to be crying, or sick men to groan. Do not befriend, therefore, this miserable disease, but resist it by all due remedies.

**Direction 17. Take heed of unthankful doctrines, which teach you to deny or undervalue mercy.**

Such is, 1. The doctrines of the Pelagians, (whom Prosper calls the Ungrateful,) who denied faith and special grace to be any special gift of God; and who teach you that Peter is no more indebted to God than Judas for his differencing grace. 2. The doctrine which denies general grace, (which is presupposed to special,) and tells the world that Christ died only for the elect, and that all the mercy of the gospel is confined to them alone; and teaches all men to deny God any thanks for Christ or any gospel mercy until they know that they are elect and justified; and would teach the wicked, (on earth and in hell,) that they ought not to accuse themselves for sinning against any gospel mercy, or for rejecting a Christ who died for them. 3. All doctrine which makes God the physical, efficient predeterminer of every act of the creature considered in all its circumstances; and so tells you that saving grace is no more, nor any otherwise caused, by God than sin and every natural act is; and that our thanks which we owe him for keeping us from sin are only for his not irresistibly premoving us to it. Such doctrines cut the veins of thankfulness; and being not doctrines according to godliness, the life of grace and the spiritual sense of believers are against them.

**Direction 18. Do not put God off with verbal thanks, but give him yourself and all you have.**

Thankfulness causes the soul to ask, "How can I repay the Lord for all his acts of kindness to me?" (Psalm 116:12). And it is no less than yourself and all you have that you must render; that is, you must give God not only your tithes, and the sacrifice of Cain, but yourself to be entirely his servant, and all that you have to be at his command, and used in the order that he would have you use it. A thankful soul devotes itself to God; this is the "sacrifice—alive, holy, and pleasing to God" (Romans 12:1). It studies how to do him service, and how to do good with all his mercies. Thankfulness is a powerful spring of obedience, and makes men long to be fruitful and profitable, and glad of opportunities to be serviceable to God. Thus law and gospel, obedience and gratitude, concur. A thankful obedience and an obedient thankfulness are a Christian's life. "Present to God a thank offering. Repay your vows to the Most High. Pray to me when you are in trouble. I will deliver you, and you will honor me. ... Whoever presents a thank offering honors me. To whoever obeys my commands, I will reveal my power to deliver" (Psalm 50:14-15, 23).

I beseech you now who read these lines, be so true to God, be so honest, be so much a friend to the comfort of your soul, and so much love a life of pleasure, as to set yourself for the time to come to a more conscientious performance of this noble work; and steep your thoughts in the abundant mercies of your God, and express them more in all your speech to God and man. Say as David, "Yes, Lord! I am indeed your servant; ... You saved me from death. I will present a thank offering to you, and call on the name of the Lord" (Psalm 116:16-17). "I will praise you, O Lord, for you lifted me up and did not allow my enemies to gloat over me. O Lord my God, I cried out to you and you healed me. O Lord, you pulled me up from Sheol; you rescued me from among those descending into the grave. Sing to the Lord, you faithful followers of his; give thanks to his holy name. ... you removed my sackcloth and covered me with joy. So now my heart will sing to you and not be silent; O Lord my God, I will always give thanks to you" (Psalm 30:1-4, 11-12). "I will sing praises to God's name. I will magnify him as I give him thanks. That will please the Lord more than an ox ..." (Psalm 69:30-31). "It is fitting to thank the Lord, and to sing praises to your name, O Most High; to proclaim your loyal love in the morning and your faithfulness during the night" (Psalm 92:1-2). "In the middle of the night I arise to thank you for your just regulations" (Psalm 119:62). "Certainly the godly will give thanks to your name; the morally upright will live in your presence" (Psalm 140:13). Remember that you are commanded "in everything give thanks" (1 Thessalonians 5:18). When God is scant in mercy to you, then be scant in thankfulness to him; and not when the devil, and a forgetful, or unbelieving, or discontented heart, would hide his greatest mercies from you. It is just with God to give up that person to sadness of heart, and to comfortless, self-tormenting melancholy, who will not be persuaded by the greatness and multitude of mercies to be frequent in the sweet returns of thanks.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). All texts were fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **The last line (margin note):** the line "To glorify God" at the end of the range is a margin note that belongs to the paragraph or section that follows in the source (the next file). It is kept here so that every line of the range is accounted for. The row of asterisks before it is Baxter's section break.
- **Word swaps:** *christians* to Christians; *covetous* to greedy (Direction 15); *corporal* to bodily; *favours, honour* to American spelling; *near our apprehension* to "close to our awareness" (Direction 7); *want(est)* (lack) to "lack"; *bethink* to "consider"; *maintenance* to "means of support"; *ingenuous* to "honest"; *conscionable* to "conscientious"; *froward* to "perverse"; *beholden* to "indebted"; *uncomfortable* (melancholy) to "comfortless"; *usage* to "treatment"; *habitation* to "home"; *yea* to "yes."
- **Kept as Baxter's terms:** *diminutively* (Direction 15: "thinks little" of everything), *make conscience of* (Direction 14: make it a matter of conscience), *apt much to affect* (Direction 7), *room* (Direction 14: place), *melancholy*, *religion*, *mortify* (Direction 12: "You mortify your mercies" means you deaden them by cutting them off from God and heaven), *humiliation* (a day of public or private fasting and confession), *orthodox*, *perplexities*, *scandals* (offenses or public disgraces that cause others to stumble), *interest in God* (a share in God, as a believer's claim on him), *title* (Direction 11: our claim to heaven, "so far as our title is discerned"), *constrain*, *scant*, *gaping*.
- **"heathens or Muslims, or brutish, savage Americans"** (Direction 10): "Mahometans" is Baxter's word, rendered "Muslims." "Heathens" and "savage Americans" are his words, kept; they reflect his seventeenth-century view and his polemic, and we neither soften nor sharpen them. "Bloody inquisitors" is likewise his polemic against Rome.
- **"the mercies of England"** (Direction 10): Baxter's words; left as written.
- **Direction 17 (doctrines):** this is Baxter's own polemic and his own characterization of the views he opposes; we render it as he wrote it and do not assess whether the characterizations are fair to those who hold them. *The Pelagians:* followers of Pelagius, whom Baxter names; *Prosper calleth the Ungrateful:* Baxter attributes this name to Prosper (perhaps Prosper of Aquitaine; we have not confirmed who is meant or the reference). *Differencing grace:* grace that makes a difference between one person and another; kept. *General grace / special grace:* Baxter's terms for the mercy offered to all through the gospel and the saving grace given to believers. *Physical, efficient predeterminer:* "physical" here means a real causing of an act by God in the natural order, not "bodily"; "efficient" is the philosophical term for the producing cause; kept as Baxter's. *Premoving:* causing to act beforehand, kept. The sentence "our thanks that we owe him ... is but for not irresistible premoving us to it" is rendered as "are only for his not irresistibly premoving us to it" (adjusting the number of the verb and the adverb); the syntax in the source is a little loose.
- **Direction 16:** the source prints "may he habitually" where "may be habitually" is clearly meant; rendered "may be." *Valuation of the mercy:* kept. *Temper:* disposition.
- **Direction 12:** Baxter gives no reference for "Our Father which is in heaven" and "daily bread." The words are from the Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:9, 11, not in Baxter's text). NET reads "Our Father in heaven" and "Give us today our daily bread," and the text here uses the NET for "Our Father in heaven" while keeping Baxter's "daily bread." The sense is the same.
- **Philippians 3:18** (Direction 15): Baxter's reference is kept, but the words "sacrifices all to his belly, which is his god" paraphrase Philippians 3:19 ("their god is the belly" in the NET), which follows 3:18 immediately. This is Baxter's paraphrase, not a quotation.
- **Psalm 116:12** (Direction 18): NET "all his acts of kindness to me" for the King James "all his benefits toward me." The sense is not changed. Baxter's "What shall I render" becomes "How can I repay."
- **Romans 12:1** (Direction 18): Baxter's "living, acceptable sacrifice" is the King James sense (from "a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God"); NET reads "a sacrifice—alive, holy, and pleasing to God." We quote the NET with the extent Baxter quotes, and so the quotation is a little longer than his. The NET has "present your bodies" as the verb, which Baxter does not quote.
- **Psalm 50:14-15, 23** (Direction 18; the source prints "Psal. 1. 14, 15, 23," evidently a misprint for "l.," the Roman numeral 50): Baxter leans on "thanksgiving," "praise," and "ordereth his conversation aright." The NET changes each of these. In verse 14 the NET has "a thank offering" (King James: "thanksgiving"); in verse 15 "honor me" (King James: "glorify me"); in verse 23 the NET reads "Whoever presents a thank offering honors me" (King James: "Whoso offereth praise glorifieth me") and "To whoever obeys my commands, I will reveal my power to deliver" (King James: "to him that ordereth his conversation aright, I will show the salvation of God"). So the NET makes it a thank offering (a sacrifice) where Baxter has the more general "praise"; speaks of obeying God's commands where Baxter has "conduct ordered aright" (conversation = conduct); and has "my power to deliver" where Baxter has "the salvation of God." His point (that thankfulness and obedience go together) is not lost, but the verse is less pointed on "ordering one's conduct." The ellipsis between verses 15 and 23 marks the verses Baxter skipped (16-22 in the NET).
- **Psalm 116:16-17** (the paragraph beginning "I beseech you now"): NET reads "You saved me from death" where Baxter has the King James "thou hast loosed my bonds." The NET does not have the "bonds" image Baxter quotes. NET has "thank offering" for "sacrifice of thanksgiving." The ellipsis marks the words "I am your servant, the son of your female servant," which Baxter does not quote.
- **Psalm 30:1-4, 11-12** (the same paragraph): Baxter's quotation follows the King James and the NET changes it in several places. Verse 3: the King James has "thou hast kept me alive, that I should not go down to the pit"; the NET has "you rescued me from among those descending into the grave" (a different wording, not the same clause; we have not checked why the renderings differ). Verse 4: the King James "at the remembrance of his holiness" is "to his holy name" in the NET. Verse 12: the King James "my glory may sing praise" is "my heart will sing" in the NET (we have not confirmed the reason for the difference). Baxter's "girded me with gladness" is "covered me with joy." The NET's verses 5-10 are not quoted, and the NET's superscription in verse 1 ("A psalm, a song used at the dedication of the temple; by David") is left out as Baxter has it left out.
- **Psalm 69:30-31:** NET "That will please the Lord more than an ox ..." The NET continues "or a bull with horns and hooves"; Baxter stops at "an ox." The King James had "praise the name of God with a song" for the NET's "sing praises to God's name"; the sense is the same.
- **Psalm 92:1-2:** the King James "It is a good thing to give thanks" is "It is fitting to thank" in the NET; "loving-kindness" is "loyal love"; "every night" is "during the night." The NET's superscription ("A psalm; a song for the Sabbath day") is not quoted.
- **Psalm 119:62:** NET "just regulations" for the King James "righteous judgments." **Psalm 140:13:** the King James "Surely the righteous" is "Certainly the godly" in the NET, and "the upright shall dwell" is "the morally upright will live." The NET makes the "righteous" those who live godly lives, a small shift of emphasis from Baxter's.
- **1 Thessalonians 5:18:** NET "in everything give thanks" (the King James: "In every thing give thanks"). The sense is the same.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "the sacrifice of Cain" (Genesis 4:3-5, an offering God did not accept; Baxter uses it for gifts without the giver's heart); "tithes."
- **"a life of pleasure"** (last long paragraph): Baxter means a life of joy in God, which he uses throughout as a reason to be thankful; left as written. **"not when the devil ... would hide his greatest mercies from thee"**: kept, with "you."
- **"such is, 1. ... 2. ... 3."** (Direction 17): Baxter's numbered sub-points are kept as he has them.
- **Headings:** Baxter's "Direction" headings run into the first sentence of the paragraph with a double dash (`--`); they are printed as bold headings here.
- **Cross-references:** Direction 17's remarks on the "elect" and on "general grace" refer to Baxter's disputes with Calvinist and other doctrines on grace and the extent of the atonement; those views are developed in his other writings (not checked here).
