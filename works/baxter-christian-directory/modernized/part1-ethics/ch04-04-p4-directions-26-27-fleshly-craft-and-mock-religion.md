# Chapter 4: Directions against Hypocrisy, Directions 26 and 27

**Direction 26. Be careful of all that fleshly policy or craft, and worldly wisdom, which are contrary to the wisdom of the word of God, and would draw you from the plain and open-heartedness which godly sincerity requires.**

Let that which was Paul's rejoicing be yours, "that with pure motives and sincerity which are from God—not by human wisdom but by the grace of God—we conducted ourselves in the world" (2 Corinthians 1:12). Christianity does not renounce wisdom and honest self-preservation; but yet it makes men plain-hearted, and haters of crafty, fraudulent minds. What is the famous hypocritical religion superadded to Christianity and called popery, but that which Paul feared in his godly jealousy for the Corinthians, "just as the serpent deceived Eve by his treachery, your minds may be led astray from a sincere and pure devotion to Christ" (2 Corinthians 11:1–3). A forsaking of the Christian simplicity of doctrine, discipline, worship, and conduct, is the hypocrisy of religion, and of life. Equivocating and dishonest shifts and hiding, suit those that have an ill cause, or an ill conscience, or an ill master whom they dare not trust; and not those that have so good a cause and God as Christians have.

**Direction 27. Remember how much of sincerity consists in seriousness, and how much of hypocrisy consists in seeming, and dreaming, and trifling in the things of God and our salvation: see therefore that you keep your souls awake, in a sensible and serious frame.**

[159] Read over the fifty considerations, which, in the third part of my *Saints' Rest*, I have given to convince you of the necessity of being serious. See that there be as much in your faith as in your creed, and as much in your hearts and lives as in your belief. Remember that seeming and dreaming will not mortify deep-rooted sins, nor conquer strong and subtle enemies, nor make you acceptable to God, nor save your souls from his revenging justice. Remember what a mad kind of profaneness it is to jest and trifle about heaven and hell, and to dally with the great and dreadful God. "Since all these things are to melt away in this manner, what sort of people must you be, conducting your lives in holiness and godliness?" (2 Peter 3:11). You pray for an obedience answering the pattern of the heavenly society, when you say, "may your will be done on earth as it is in heaven;" and will you be such hypocrites as to pray, that you may imitate saints and angels in the purity and obedience of your hearts and lives, and when you have done, take up with shows, and seemings, and saying a few words, and a lifeless image of that holiness which you never had; yes, and perhaps deride and persecute in others the very thing which you daily pray for. O horrible abuse of the all-seeing God! Do you no more believe or fear his justice? When the apostle says (Galatians 6:7), "Do not be deceived. God will not be made a fool;" he hints, that hypocrites go about to put a scorn on God by a mock religion, though it is not he, but themselves, that will prove mocked in the end. They offer God a deaf nut, or an empty shell or cask, for a sacrifice. A hypocrite differs from a true Christian, as a fencer from a soldier; he plays his part very formally upon a stage with much applause; but you may perceive that he is not in earnest, by his trifling and formality, and never killing any of his sins. Would men show no more of the great, everlasting matters of their own professed belief, in any seriousness of affection or effort than most men do, if they were not hypocrites? Would they hate and scorn men for doing but that (and part of that) which they pray and profess to do themselves, if they were not hypocrites? Woe to the world, because of hypocrisy! Woe to the carnal members of the church! Woe to idol shepherds, and the seeming, nominal, lifeless Christians, of whatever sect! for God will not be mocked. They are Christians, but it is with a mock Christianity, while their souls are strangers to the true esteem and use of Christ. They are believers, but with a mock belief, described (James 2). They believe God should be loved above all, but they do not love him. They believe that holiness is better than all the pleasures of sin; yet they do not choose it, but hate it. [160] They are religious, with a seeming empty religion, which will not so much as humble them, nor bridle their tongues (James 1:26). They are wise, with a mock wisdom; they are wise enough to prove their sins to be all lawful, or but venial sins: and wise enough to cast away the medicine that would heal them; and to prove the physician wrong, and to answer the most learned preacher of them all, and to escape salvation, and to secure themselves a place in hell, and keep themselves ignorant of it till they are there. They are converted, but with a mock conversion; which leaves them as carnal, and proud, and worldly as before; being born of water but not of the Spirit, and being pleasure-loving still (John 3:5, 6; Jude 19). They repent, but with a mock repentance; they repent, but they will not leave their sin, nor confess and bewail it, but hate reproof, and excuse their sin. They are honest, but with a mock honesty; though they swear, and curse, and rail, and slander, and backbite, and scorn at piety itself, yet they mean well, and have honest hearts: though they do not receive the word with deep-rooting in their hearts, but are abominable and disobedient, and to every good work reprobate, they are honest for all that (Luke 8:15; Titus 1:16). They love God above all, though they do not love to think or speak of him seriously, but hate his holiness and justice, his word and holy ways and servants, and are such as the Scripture calls "haters of God;" and do not keep his commandments, nor live to his glory. [161] They love the servants of God, but they do not care if the world were rid of them all; and take them to be but a company of self-conceited, troublesome fellows, and as very hypocrites as themselves; and the poor Christians that are cruelly used by them, think they are neither in earnest nor in jest, when they profess to love the worshippers of God. They do not love their money, nor lands, nor lusts, with such a kind of love, I am sure. They have also always good desires; but they are such mock desires as those in James 2:15, that wished the poor were fed, and clothed, and warmed, but gave them nothing toward it: and such good desires as the sluggard has, that lies in bed and wishes that all his work were done (Proverbs 21:25). "What the sluggard desires will kill him, for his hands have refused to work." They pray, but with mock prayers; you would little think that they are speaking to the most holy God, for no less than the saving of their souls, when they are more serious in their very games and amusements. They pray for grace, but they cannot abide it; they pray for holiness, but they are resolved they will have none of it; they pray against their sin, but no entreaty can persuade them from it. They would have a mock ministry, a mock discipline, a mock church, a mock sacrament, as they make a mock profession, and give God but a mock obedience; as I might show you through all the particulars, but for being tedious. And all this, because they have but a mock faith: they do not believe that God is in earnest with them in his commands, and threatenings, and foretelling of his judgments; as Lot to his sons-in-law (Genesis 19:14), "He seems to them as one who mocked," and therefore they serve him as those that would mock him. O wretched hypocrites! is this agreeable to your holy profession? You call yourselves Christians, and profess to believe the doctrine of Christ: is this agreeable to Christianity, to your creed, to the ten commandments, to the Lord's prayer, and to the rest of the word of God? Had you none but the holy, jealous God to make a mock of? Had you nothing less than religion, and matters of salvation and damnation, to play with? Do you serve God as if he were a child, or an idol, or a man of straw; who either does not know your hearts, or is pleased with trifles, and compliments, and shows, and saying over certain words, or acting a part before him as on a stage? [162] Do you know what you offer, and to whom? His power is omnipotence; his glory is ten thousand-fold above that of the sun; his wisdom is infinite; millions of angels adore him continually; he is your King and Judge; he abhors hypocrites. If you did but see one glimpse of his glory, or of the lowest of his angels, the sight would awake you from your dreaming and dallying, and frighten you from your canting and trifling into a serious regard of God and your everlasting state. Malachi 1:8, "Indeed, try offering them to your governor! Will he be pleased with you or show you favor?" asks the Lord of Heaven's Armies. If your servants set before you upon your table the feathers instead of the fowl, and the hair and wool instead of the flesh, and the scales instead of the fish, would you not think they rather mocked than served you? How dearly some have paid even in this life for mocking God, let the case of Aaron's sons (Leviticus 10:1–3), and of Ananias and Sapphira (Acts 5) inform you: if with the fig tree (Matthew 21:19), you offer God leaves only instead of fruit, you are near to cursing, and your end is to be burned. Do you not read what he says to the church of Laodicea, "I wish you were either cold or hot! So because you are lukewarm, and neither hot nor cold, I am going to vomit you out of my mouth" (Revelation 3:15–16): that is, either be an open unbeliever, or a holy, downright, zealous Christian: but because you call yourself a Christian, and do not have the life or zeal of a Christian, but cover your wickedness and carnality with that holy name, I will cast you away as an abominable vomit. It would make the heart of a believer ache to think of the hypocrisy of most that usurp the name of Christians, and how cruelly they mock themselves. What a glory is offered them, and they lose it by their dallying! What a price is in their hands, what mercy is offered them, and they lose it by their dallying! What danger is before them, and they will fall into it by their dallying! Does not the weight of your salvation forbid this trifling? You might better set the town on fire, and make a jest of it, than jest your souls into the fire of hell. Then you will find that hell is no jesting matter. If you mock yourselves out of your salvation, where are you then? If you play with time, and means, and mercy till they are gone, you are undone forever. O do not dally till you are past remedy. Alas, poor dreaming trifling hypocrites! Is time so sweet, and life so short, and death so sure and near, and God so holy, just, and terrible, and heaven so glorious, and hell so hot, and both everlasting, and yet will you not be in earnest about your work? Up and be doing, as you are men! and as ever you care what becomes of you forever! Depart from iniquity, if you will name the name of Christ (2 Timothy 2:19). Do not let a cheating world delude you for a moment, and have the kernel, the heart, while God has but the empty shell. A mock religion will but keep up a mock hope, a mock peace, and a mock joy and comfort, till Satan has done his work, and is ready to unhood you and open your eyes. Job 8:13, "Such is the destiny of all who forget God; the hope of the godless perishes," Job 27:8–9, "For what hope does the godless have when he is cut off, when God takes away his life? Does God listen to his cry when distress overtakes him?" Job 20:4–7, "Surely you know that it has been from old, ever since humankind was placed on the earth, that the elation of the wicked is brief, the joy of the godless lasts but a moment. Even though his stature reaches to the heavens and his head touches the clouds, he will perish forever, like his own excrement; those who used to see him will say, 'Where is he?'" Away then with hypocritical formality and dalliance, and be serious and sincere for your soul, and with your God.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows the Latin in parentheses; the translation was prepared for this project and is not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check it before relying on fine points.

[159] The causes of superstition (and so of hypocrisy) are, pleasing and sensory rites and ceremonies, excess of outward and pharisaical holiness, over-great reverence of traditions, which cannot but load the church, the stratagems of prelates for their own ambition and lucre, the savoring too much good intentions, which opens a gate to notions and novelties. Lord Bacon's Essays. As P. Callimachus Exper. describes Attila, that he was a devourer of flesh and wine, etc. and yet Religione persuasionibusque de diis à gente sua susceptis, usque ad superstitionem addictus. Calli. p. (mihi) 339. (English, rendered loosely: yet devoted, even to the point of superstition, by the religion and the convictions about the gods received from his own people. Callimachus, p. 339; "mihi" ("to me") probably refers to Baxter's own copy, which we have not confirmed.)

[160] Psalm 78:37; 2 Corinthians 7:11.

[161] 1 John 1:6; 2:3–5, 15; 4:6–8, 20; 5:3; Matthew 10:37.

[162] The similitude of superstition to religion makes it the more deformed: and as wholesome meat corrupts into little worms, so good forms and orders corrupt into a number of petty observances. Lord Bacon's Essay of Superstition.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). Each quoted verse below was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Range:** the file begins at Direction 26 and covers Direction 27 to the end. The last line of the range is the printed heading "PART IV.", which belongs to the next section and is not rendered here. No section heading ("Directions against Hypocrisy.") falls in this range; it appears in the first file of the section.
- **Direction labels:** Baxter's "Direct. XXVI., XXVII." are rendered "Direction 26" and "Direction 27" The first sentence of each is in bold. In Direction 27 the footnote marker [159] falls after that sentence.
- **2 Corinthians 1:12 (flag):** Baxter quotes "in simplicity and godly sincerity, not in fleshly wisdom, you have had your conversation in the world." The NET reads "with pure motives and sincerity which are from God—not by human wisdom but by the grace of God—we conducted ourselves in the world." Three changes matter. "Fleshly wisdom" becomes "human wisdom," which loses the link to the "fleshly policy" and "worldly wisdom" with which the Direction opens. "Simplicity" (the key word of this Direction) becomes "pure motives." And the person changes from "you" to "we" (Paul speaking of himself).
- **2 Corinthians 11:3 (flag):** Baxter cites 11:1–3 and quotes "lest as the serpent beguiled Eve by his subtilty, so their minds should be corrupted from the simplicity that is in Christ." We begin the NET quotation at "just as the serpent deceived Eve by his treachery, your minds may be led astray from a sincere and pure devotion to Christ," leaving out the NET's "I am afraid that" (Baxter's "lest" is carried by "feared" in his own words). "Subtilty" (craftiness) becomes "treachery," and "the simplicity that is in Christ" becomes "a sincere and pure devotion to Christ," so again the word "simplicity," on which Baxter builds, is lost. Baxter's sentence has no question mark in the source; left as printed.
- **2 Peter 3:11:** NET wording. Baxter's "what manner of persons ought you to be in all holy conversation and godliness" becomes "what sort of people must you be, conducting your lives in holiness and godliness" (his "conversation" meaning conduct). The NET's closing comma is replaced by Baxter's question mark.
- **Lord's Prayer (unmarked):** "Thy will be done on earth as it is in heaven" has no reference in Baxter's text; NET Matthew 6:10 reads "may your will be done on earth as it is in heaven" (reference given here only, not in Baxter's text).
- **Galatians 6:7 (flag):** Baxter has "Be not deceived, God is not mocked." The NET has "Do not be deceived. God will not be made a fool." Baxter's whole argument turns on "mocked" and "mock," and the NET drops that word, though "made a fool" carries a similar sense of scorn. His semicolon after the quotation is kept, inside the quotation mark as he printed it, which joins the NET's second sentence to his "he hints."
- **Proverbs 21:25:** NET wording. Baxter's "The desire of the sluggard killeth him, because his hands refuse to labour" becomes "What the sluggard desires will kill him, for his hands have refused to work." The reference is placed where Baxter put it, before the quotation.
- **Genesis 19:14 (paraphrase):** Baxter's "He seemeth to them as one that mocked" is his own paraphrase of the story, not a verbatim quotation of the verse; we kept his wording (modernized) in his quotation marks. The NET reads "his sons-in-law thought he was ridiculing them." The word "mocked" is the point of his argument.
- **Malachi 1:8:** NET wording. Baxter's "Offer this now to thy governor: will he be pleased with thee, or accept thy person, saith the Lord of hosts?" becomes "Indeed, try offering them to your governor! Will he be pleased with you or show you favor?" asks the Lord of Heaven's Armies. In the NET "them" points back to the blind, lame, and sick animals of the preceding verse; Baxter's "this" does the same work. The NET title "the Lord of Heaven's Armies" renders the same title as the King James "Lord of hosts." The reference is at the head of the quotation, where Baxter put it.
- **Revelation 3:15-16:** NET wording. Baxter's "I would thou wert cold or hot" is "I wish you were either cold or hot!" and "spue thee out of my mouth" is "vomit you out of my mouth." The NET's closing exclamation mark is left out where Baxter's colon and his explanation ("that is...") follow. Baxter's later "abominable vomit" is his own phrase.
- **Job 8:13; 27:8-9; 20:4-7 (flag):** NET wording. Baxter's King James has "hypocrite" in all three ("the hypocrite's hope shall perish," "the hope of the hypocrite," "the joy of the hypocrite"); the NET has "godless" ("the hope of the godless perishes," "what hope does the godless have," "the joy of the godless"). The NET's word is wider than "hypocrite," so Baxter's application of these verses to hypocrites rests on the older rendering. In 27:8, Baxter's "though he hath gained" has no counterpart in the NET, which reads "when he is cut off." In 20:4, the NET adds "ever since humankind was placed on the earth." In 20:7, Baxter's "like his own dung" is "like his own excrement." In Job 8:13 the NET's closing comma is replaced by Baxter's period, as the sentence stops there in his text. The NET's "Where is he?" is in single quotation marks because it is quoted within the quotation.
- **Cited only (no quotation, kept in Baxter's wording):** James 2 (mock belief); James 1:26; John 3:5, 6; Jude 19; Luke 8:15; Titus 1:16; James 2:15; Leviticus 10:1–3; Acts 5; Matthew 21:19; 2 Timothy 2:19. Baxter's "Depart from iniquity, if you will name the name of Christ" is in his own words with the reference to 2 Timothy 2:19; the NET there reads "Everyone who confesses the name of the Lord must turn away from evil," and we have not substituted it since Baxter does not put his words in quotation marks.
- **"haters of God":** Baxter says "the Scripture calls" these without giving a reference; the NET of Romans 1:30 also has "haters of God" (reference given here only, not in Baxter's text).
- **Unmarked allusions (kept in Baxter's wording):** "idol shepherds" (compare Zechariah 11:17); "being born of water but not of the Spirit" (John 3:5, cited); "the sluggard that lies in bed" (compare Proverbs 26:14); "fifty considerations ... in the third part of my *Saints' Rest*" is Baxter's own work, not checked.
- **Word swaps (Baxter's word, then ours):** "take heed" is "be careful"; "conversation" is "conduct"; "convince" kept (persuade); "endeavour" is "effort"; "good sadness" (twice) is "earnest"; "sports" is "amusements"; "learnedst" is "most learned"; "scape" is "escape"; "confute" is "prove wrong" (in "to confute the physician"); "toys" is "trifles"; "meanest" is "lowest"; "omnipotency" is "omnipotence"; "nigh" is "near"; "dear" (in "how dear have some paid") is "how dearly"; "vain" (in "seeming vain religion") is "empty"; "sensual" (in "being sensual still") is "pleasure-loving" and in note [159] "sensory"; "idol shepherds," "carnal," "mortify," "venial" are kept; "intimateth" is "hints"; "beseem" is "suit"; "infidel" is "unbeliever"; "yea" is "yes."
- **sensible** (Direction 27, "a sensible and serious frame"): kept. Baxter means feeling, alert, aware, as opposed to dull or asleep. **frame:** kept (a settled state of mind).
- **deaf nut:** a nut with no kernel inside; kept. **cask:** kept (an empty barrel).
- **fencer … soldier:** Baxter means a stage or practice swordsman as against a real fighter. Kept.
- **man of straw:** a figure stuffed with straw, a mere dummy; kept.
- **canting:** hypocritical, formulaic religious talk; kept. **compliments:** empty courtesies; kept.
- **unhood:** to take off a hawk's hood; kept (here, to remove the blindfold of delusion). The explanation is ours, from the usual sense of the word.
- **reprobate** (to every good work): kept; it means rejected or failing the test (the sense of Titus 1:16). **honest:** kept (upright). **mock:** kept throughout as Baxter's recurring word.
- **popery, papist:** "popery" in Direction 26 is Baxter's word for the Roman Catholic religion; kept as his polemic. He claims that it is a hypocritical religion added to Christianity; that is his claim, not ours.
- **superadded:** kept (added on top). **superstition:** kept (as in note [159]).
- **Punctuation kept:** the sentence "What is the famous hypocritical religion ... but that which Paul feared ... in Christ" is a question but is printed in the source with a full stop; kept as printed. In "Do you serve God as if he were a child ... that either knoweth not your hearts," Baxter's "that" is rendered "who."
- **"as ever you care what becomes of you for ever":** an oath-like urging ("as you ever care"); kept in his order.
- **Note [159]:** "Lord Bacon's Essays" is Baxter's attribution; we have not checked it. "P. Callimachus Exper." is unexpanded; "Exper." may be an abbreviation of a title but we have not confirmed which work is meant. Attila is Baxter's example here. "&c." is "etc." The Latin is printed as in the source; "à" is Baxter's accent. "lucre" kept (gain, profit). "savouring" (to favor, to relish) is rendered "savoring." "conceits" is rendered "notions."

"similitude" and "deformed" are kept.
