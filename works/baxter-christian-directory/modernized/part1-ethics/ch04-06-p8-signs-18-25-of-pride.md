# Chapter 4: Signs of Pride (Signs 18-25): Titles, Praise, Censoriousness, Reproof, Talkativeness, Dependence, and Dress

*This file begins in the middle of Direction 2 ("Observe the motions and discoveries of pride"), partway through Baxter's list of signs, at Sign 18 of this list. It ends at Sign 25; the next file continues.*

**Sign 18.** A proud man loves honorable names and titles; as the Pharisees to be called Rabbi (Matthew 23). And yet they may have so much wit as to claim, that it is but to promote their service for the common good, and not that they are so weak to care for empty names; or else that they were forced to it, by somebody's kindness, without their seeking, and against their wills.

**Sign 19.** Pride tickles the heart of fools with content and pleasure to hear themselves applauded, or see themselves admired by the people, or to hear that they have got a great reputation in the world, or to be flocked after, and cried up, and have many followers. Herod loves to hear in commendation of his oration, "The voice of a god, and not of a man!" (Acts 12:22). It is a feast to the proud, to hear that men abroad do magnify him, or see that those about him do reverence, and love, and honor, and idolize him. Hence has the church been filled with busy sect-masters, even of those that seemed forwardest in religion; which was sadly prophesied of by Paul to the Ephesians, Acts 20:29–30. Two sorts of troublers, under the name of pastors, pride has in all ages thrust upon the church; devouring wolves, and dividing sect-masters. "I know that after I am gone fierce wolves will come in among you, not sparing the flock. Even from among your own group men will arise, teaching perversions of the truth to draw the disciples away after them." See also Romans 16:16–17.

**Sign 20.** Pride makes men censorious and uncharitable: they minimize other men's virtues and good works, and suspect groundlessly their sincerity. A little thing serves to make them think or call a man a hypocrite. Very few are honest, or sincere, or godly, or humble, or faithful, or able, or worthy in their eyes, even among them that are so indeed, or that they have cause to think so: a slight conjecture or report seems enough to allow them to condemn or defame another. They quickly see the speck in a brother's eye. Their pride and fancy can create a thousand heretics, or schismatics, or hypocrites, or ungodly ones, that never were such but in the court of their presumption. Especially if they take men for their adversaries, they can cast them into the most odious shape, and make them anything that the devil will desire them. But the humble are charitable to others, as conscious of much infirmity in themselves, which makes them need the tenderness of others. They judge the best till they know the worst, and censure not men until they have both evidence to prove it, and a call to meddle with them, having learned, Matthew 7:1–4, "Do not judge so that you will not be judged."

**Sign 21.** Pride causes men to hate reproof: the proud are forward in finding faults in others, but do not love a plain reprover of themselves. Though it is a duty which God himself commands, Leviticus 19:17, as an expression of love, and contrary to hatred, yet it will make a proud man to be your enemy. Proverbs 15:12, "The scorner will not love one who corrects him; he will not go to the wise." Proverbs 9:7–8, "Whoever corrects a mocker is asking for insult; whoever reproves a wicked person receives abuse. Do not reprove a mocker, or he will hate you; reprove a wise person, and he will love you." It galls their hearts, and they take themselves to be injured, and they will bear you a grudge for it, as if you were their enemy. If they valued or honored you before, you have lost them or angered them if you have told them of their faults. If they love to hear a preacher deal plainly with others, they hate him when he deals so with them. Herod will give away John's head, when he has first imprisoned him for telling him of his sin, though before he reverenced him and heard him gladly. They can easily endure to be evil, and do evil, but not to hear of it. As if a man that had the leprosy, loved the disease, and yet hated him that tells him that he has it, or would cure him of it. This pride is the thing that has made men so unprofitable to each other, by driving faithful reproof and admonition almost out of the world, because men are so proud that they will not hear it. Hence it is that others hear oftener of men's faults, than they do themselves; and that backbiting has grown the common fashion, because proud sinners drive away reprovers, by their impatience and displeasure. Husbands and wives, yes, servants with their masters, are so far out of love with just reproof, that they can hardly bear it. He must be exceedingly skillful in smoothing and oiling every word, and making it more like a commendation or flattery, than a reproof, that will escape their indignation.

**Sign 22.** When a proud man is justly reproved, he studies at once to deny or minimize his fault; to show you that he is more tender of his honor than of his honesty. It is a hard thing to bring him to free confession, and to thank you for your love and faithfulness, and to resolve upon more watchfulness for the time to come: when the humble soul is readier to believe that he is faulty than that he is innocent, and to say more against himself than you will say (if truly). This one sign may tell you how commonly pride reigns in the world. How few are they among many that are heartily thankful for a just and necessary reproof! Mark them, whether the first word they speak, in answer to you, is not either a denial or an excuse, or an upbraiding you with something that they think you faulty in, or else an emotional, proud repulse, bidding you meddle with yourselves?

**Sign 23.** Pride makes men talkative; and more desirous to speak than to hear, and to teach than to be taught: because such think highly of their own understandings, and think others have more need of their instructions, than they of other men's. [225] Not that humility is any enemy to communicative charity, or to zealous efforts for the converting and edifying of souls; but a teaching, talking disposition, where there is no need, and beyond the measure of your calling and abilities, when you have more need to learn yourselves, is the fruit of pride. When you take less heed what another says to you, than you expect he should take of what you say to him: when your talk is not so much by way of question as becomes a learner, but in the conversations and dictates of a teacher: when you are so full of anything that is your own, and so contemptuous of what is said by others, that you have not the patience to hear them silently till they come to the end; but unmannerly interrupt them, and set in yourselves; which is as much as to say, Hold your tongue, and let me speak that am more wise and worthy: when you strive to have the most words, and to be speaking; as horses in a race, strive which shall go foremost: this is because pride puffs you up, and moves your tongues, as a leaf is shaken by the wind; it fills your sails; and makes you like bagpipes, that are loudest when they are full of wind, and pressed. Ecclesiastes 10:14, "a fool keeps on babbling." Proverbs 10:19, "When words abound, transgression is inevitable, but the one who restrains his words is wise."

**Sign 24.** Pride makes men excessively loath to be beholden to others; so that some will starve or perish before they will stoop so far as to seek, or be obliged to thankfulness by any; especially if they are such as they have any quarrel with. And this they take for manlike gallantry, and a scorning to be base. I confess that, as Paul says to servants, if we can be free, we should rather choose it; and that no man should unnecessarily make himself a debtor to another, by being beholden to him: especially ministers, who should avoid all temptations of dependence upon man: and therefore should neither hang on great ones, lest they be tempted to unfaithful silence or flattery; nor needlessly live on the people's charity, lest they be hindered from the free exercise of their ministry. Therefore Paul labored with his hands where he thought it would hinder his work to be burdensome to the churches, or give occasion to the envious to reproach him; [226] and he would "rather die than any should make this his glorying void," 1 Corinthians 9:15. Innocency and independency, as Mr. Bolton was accustomed to say, steel the face, and help a minister to be bold and faithful. As Camerarius said, when he was invited to the court,

      Alterius ne sit, qui suus esse potest.

But yet man is a sociable creature; and we are made to be helpful to each other: we are like the wheels of a watch, that can none of them do their work alone, without the concurrence of the rest. And therefore a proud man that would live wholly on himself, and scorns to be beholden, would break himself off from the place that God has set him in, and separate himself from human society, and be either a world of himself, or a god to others. But God has caused all the members purposely to stand in need of one another, that none might be despised, and that all might still exercise love in communicating, and humility in accepting of each other's help.

**Sign 25.** Pride makes people desirous to equal their superiors, and exceed their equals, in apparel, or handsome dwellings, and provisions, and entertainments, and all appearances that tend to set them out, and make them seem considerable in the world: [227] for it excessively regards the eye of man. A fit respect to decency must be had (so we place no greater a necessity in it than we ought): but pride would gladly go with the highest, and have more curiosity than needs; and makes a greater matter of decency than the thing requires. I am not of their temper, that censure every man whose hair is not of their cut, and whose garments are not of their fashion, and who are bred in a way of more gentility and ceremony than myself. But yet the affectation of imitating fashion-mongers, and bearing a port above one's rank, and rather desiring the conversation and company of superiors than inferiors, and to live like those that are a step above us, than those that are a step below us, are signs as significant of pride, as the robes of a judge or a doctor are of their dignities and degrees. I am sure humility has learned this lesson, Romans 12:16, "do not be haughty but associate with the lowly. Do not be conceited." As for the ridiculous, effeminate fashions and deportment of some men, and the spots, and paintings, and nakedness, and other bizarre fashions of some women, and the many hours which they daily waste in dressings and adornings, and preparing themselves for the sight of others, they are the badges of so foolish, and worse than childish a sort of pride, that I will not trouble myself and the reader in rebuking them. Manly pride is ashamed of such toys. Let the patrons of them please their patients, by proving them lawful, while they have no wiser work to do; and when they have done, let them go on to prove that it is lawful for sober persons to wear such irons as they do in Bedlam; and that such chains as they in Newgate wear are no signs of a prisoner; and that it is lawful for an honest woman to wear a harlot's habit. If the proud have no more wit than to wear the badges of their childishness or distraction, and show their shame to all they meet, and make themselves as ridiculous as men that lay aside their breeches, and wear sidecoats again like children, I will leave them to themselves, and will not now trouble them with any longer contradiction.

Humilitatis septem gradus secundum Anselmum sunt. 1. Opinione: (1.) Se contemptibilem cognoscere. (2.) Hoc non dolere. 2. Manifestatione: (1.) Hoc confiteri: (2.) Hoc persuadere. (3.) Patienter sustinere hæc dici. 3. Voluntate: (1.) Pati contemptibiliter se tractari. (2.) Hoc idem amare. Anselm. lib. de similit. (English: Humility is: 1. Necessary: to submit oneself to a greater, and not to set oneself before an equal. 2. Abundant: to submit oneself to an equal, nor to set oneself before a lesser. 3. Perfect: to submit oneself to a lesser. Gloss on Matthew 3. There are seven degrees of humility according to Anselm. 1. In opinion: (1) to know oneself contemptible; (2) not to grieve at this. 2. In manifestation: (1) to confess this; (2) to persuade others of it; (3) to bear patiently that these things are said. 3. In will: (1) to suffer oneself to be treated contemptibly; (2) to love this same thing. Anselm, book on similitudes.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). All NET quotations below were fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Sign numbering:** Baxter's numerals here ("Sign XVIII" and so on) are kept as given. The earlier file for this section also has a Sign 18, 19, and 20 (a different set of signs), so the numbers repeat across files.
- **Matthew 23 (Sign 18):** Baxter gives no quotation marks, only the title "Rabbi." The reference is as he gave it (verse not named); the NET of Matthew 23:7 has people call them "Rabbi."
- **Acts 11:22 corrected to Acts 12:22 (Sign 19):** Baxter's printed reference is "Acts xi. 22," but the words are in Acts 12:22. NET: "The voice of a god, and not of a man!" Baxter's "It is" is not in the NET, which presents the crowd's shout as a bare cry. The meaning is unchanged.
- **Acts 20:29-30 (Sign 19):** NET wording. Baxter's King James has "grievous wolves" and "after my departure" and "perverse things"; the NET has "fierce wolves," "after I am gone," "perversions of the truth." The NET also lacks the opening "For" that Baxter includes. His point (wolves from outside, sect-masters from within) is unchanged.
- **Romans 16:16-17 (Sign 19):** Baxter's "xvi. 16, 17" is kept. Verse 16 is the greeting with a holy kiss; the warning against those who cause divisions is verse 17 (NET: "watch out for those who create dissensions and obstacles contrary to the teaching that you learned"). We have not changed his reference.
- **Matthew 7:1-4 (Sign 20):** the quotation is only verse 1; NET: "Do not judge so that you will not be judged." Baxter's "mote" (King James) is "speck" in the NET (Matthew 7:3), so "mote in a brother's eye" is given as "speck." This is an allusion to that passage, unmarked.
- **Leviticus 19:17 (Sign 21):** cited only.
- **Proverbs 15:12 (Sign 21):** NET wording; same sense as the King James except that the NET has "corrects" for "reproveth."
- **Proverbs 9:7-8 (Sign 21), flagged:** the King James has "getteth himself shame" and "getteth himself a blot," and "rebuke a wise man." The NET has "is asking for insult" and "receives abuse," and "reprove a wise person." The NET also reads "mocker" for "scorner" here, though it has "scorner" in 15:12. Baxter's argument (the proud man hates his reprover) is unaffected.
- **Ecclesiastes 10:14 (Sign 23), flagged:** the King James has "A fool also is full of words." The NET has "yet a fool keeps on babbling," part of a longer sentence ("yet" begins it). We quote only the NET phrase. "Full of words" and "keeps on babbling" are close in sense for Baxter's point, but the image of fullness (he goes on to compare the talker to a bagpipe full of wind) is lost.
- **Proverbs 10:19 (Sign 23):** NET wording. King James: "In the multitude of words there wanteth not sin: but he that refraineth his lips is wise." The sense is the same.
- **1 Corinthians 9:15 (Sign 24), paraphrase:** Baxter writes that Paul "would 'rather die than any should make this his glorying void'." This is his adaptation of the King James ("it were better for me to die, than that any man should make my glorying void"), put in the third person. We kept his words, modernized ("loath," "labored"), and did not replace them, because the NET is in the first person and breaks off in a dash: "it would be better for me to die than—no one will deprive me of my reason for boasting!" The sense is the same. "Glorying" is kept as his word for "boasting."
- **Romans 12:16 (Sign 25), flagged:** Baxter's King James: "Mind not high things; but condescend to men of low estate: be not wise in your own eyes." The NET: "do not be haughty but associate with the lowly. Do not be conceited." Baxter's "condescend" here means "stoop to associate with," which "associate with the lowly" matches. "Do not be conceited" is a different image from "be not wise in your own eyes," though the sense is near. His point (humility chooses lower company, not higher) is intact.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "if we can be free, we should rather choose it" (compare 1 Corinthians 7:21, Paul to servants); Herod giving away John's head after imprisoning him and hearing him gladly (Mark 6:17-28); Paul laboring with his hands (the passages in note 226); "the wheels of a watch" (compare 1 Corinthians 12:14-26 on the members).
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): pretend, claim; extenuate, minimize (Sign 20) and deny or minimize (Sign 22); ungroundedly, groundlessly; mote, speck; presently, at once; passionate, emotional (see GLOSSARY); discourses, conversations (Sign 23); endeavours, efforts; loth, loath; chargeable (to the churches), burdensome; was wont, was accustomed; antic, bizarre; reprehending, rebuking; humour, temper; converse, conversation (Sign 25).
- **Words kept:** *wit* (intelligence, cleverness); *beholden* (indebted, under obligation); *honesty* (Sign 22, "his honor than his honesty": integrity, good character, not only truthfulness); *curiosity* (Sign 25, "more curiosity than needs": excessive fussiness or care in appearance); *port* ("a port above one's rank": a style or bearing, a grand manner); *fashion-mongers* (people who make a business of following fashion); *sect-masters* (leaders who gather a following for their own party); *contradiction* (opposition, arguing against them); *spots* (probably beauty patches, small patches of cloth stuck on the face as cosmetics in his day; we have not confirmed this); *sidecoats* (long coats, such as young children wore; our reading); *toys* (trifles). *Bedlam* and *Newgate* are, probably, the London hospital for the insane and the London prison of those names (Baxter does not explain them); he mentions their irons and chains as signs of madness and imprisonment.
- **Sign 24, "Mr. Bolton":** probably Robert Bolton, the Puritan divine (we have not confirmed which Bolton Baxter means). The saying about "innocency and independency" is reported as Baxter gives it.
- **Sign 24, "Camerarius":** left as written; we have not identified which Camerarius Baxter means. The Latin line (kept as printed) means: "Let him not be another's who can be his own." 
- **Sign 24, ministers and "hang on great ones":** Baxter's own view of ministers' independence, kept as his. "Let the patrons of them please their patients" (Sign 25): left as written; it reads as sarcasm against those who defend such fashions, and "patients" is probably those they flatter (our reading).
- **Baxter's remarks on women's and men's fashions (Sign 25):** left as he wrote them; they reflect his own century's views of dress, and he says he will not rebuke them at length.
- **Note 225:** the Latin has "benedicti," which could mean "blessed" or be the genitive of the name Benedict. The content reads like a list of signs of humility in a monastic rule (probably the Rule of St. Benedict, though we have not confirmed this). Both readings are given. Baxter adds the caution at the end ("salvo amore veritatis, et animarum"), that these signs must always be understood without harming the love of truth and of souls.
- **Note 227:** the Latin has "preferre" (probably a misprint for "præferre") in the second item. "Gloss. sup. Matt. iii." is a medieval gloss on Matthew 3 (we have not identified which gloss). "Anselm. lib. de similit." probably means Anselm's "De similitudinibus" (we have not confirmed the passage). The Latin lists three kinds of humility by whom one submits to, then seven degrees in three groups (opinion, manifestation, will).
- **Not carried:** nothing in the range has been omitted.
