# Chapter 3: Grand Direction XI (part 3 of 8)

*This file begins partway through Grand Direction XI, at Direction 2 in its series of numbered Directions, and ends partway through Direction 4; the next file continues. No new Grand Direction heading is given here. Baxter's own heading for the Grand Direction falls in an earlier file.*

**Direction 2. Think of the perfect fitness of God to be the only object of your superlative love; and how easy and necessary it should seem to us to do a work so agreeable to right reason and uncorrupted nature; and abhor all temptations which would make God seem unsuitable to you.**

O sluggish and unnatural soul! Should not an object so admirably fit allure you? Should not such attractive goodness draw you? Should not perfect lovableness win you wholly to itself? Just know yourself and God, and then refrain from loving him if you can! Where should the fish live, but in the water? And where should birds fly, but in the air? God is your very element: you die and sink down to brutishness, if you forsake him or are taken from him. What should delight the smell, but odors? or the appetite, but its delicious food? or the eye, but light, and what it shows? and the ear, but harmony? and what should delight the soul, but God? If you know yourself, you know that the nature of your mind inclines to knowledge; and by the knowledge of effects, to rise up to the cause; and by the knowledge of lower and lesser matters, to ascend to the highest and greatest. And if you know God, you know that he is the cause of all things, the Maker, Preserver, and Orderer of all, the Being of beings, the most great, and wise, and good, and happy; so that to know him is to know all; to know the most excellent, independent, glorious Being, who will leave no darkness nor unsatisfied desire in your soul. And is he not then most suitable to your mind? If you know yourself, then you know that your will, as free as it is, has a natural, necessary inclination to goodness. You cannot love evil as evil; nor can you choose but love apprehended goodness, especially the chief good, if rightly apprehended. And if you know God, you know that he is infinitely good in himself, and the cause of all the good that is in the world, and the giver of all the good you have received, and the only fit and suitable good to satisfy your desires for the time to come. And yet, shall it be so hard for you to love, so agreeably to perfect nature, so perfect, and full, and suitable a good? even Goodness and Love itself, which has begun to love you? Is any of the creatures which you love so suitable to you? Are they good, and only good, and perfectly good, and unchangeably and eternally good? Are they the spring of comfort, and the satisfying happiness of your soul? Have you found them so? or do you look to find them best at last? Foolish soul! can you love the uneven, defective, troublesome creature, if for some one small, inferior use it seems suitable to you? and can you not love Him, who is all that rational love can possibly desire to enjoy? What though the creature be near you, and God be infinitely above you? He is nearer to you than they. And though in glory he is distant, you are passing to him in his glory, and will at once be there. Though the sun is distant from you, it communicates to you its light and heat, and is more suitable to you than the candle that is nearer you. What though God is most holy, and you too earthly and unclean? is he not the fitter to purify you, and make you holy? You would rather, if you are poor, have the company and favor of the rich who can relieve you, than of beggars who will only complain with you. And if you are unlearned or ignorant, you would want the company of the wise and learned who can teach you, and not of those who are as ignorant as yourself. Who is so suitable to your desires, as he who has all that you can wisely desire, and is willing and ready to satisfy you to the full? Who is more suitable to your love, than he who loves you most, and has done most for you, and must do all that ever will be done for you, and is himself most lovely in his infinite perfections? O poor, diseased, fallen soul! if sin had not corrupted, and disordered, and perverted you, you would have thought God as suitable to your love, as meat to your hunger, and drink to your thirst, and rest to your weariness, and as the earth and water, the air and sun, are to the inhabitants of the world! O where have you fallen? and how far, how long, have you wandered from your God, that you now draw back from him as a stranger to you, and look away from him as an unsuitable good?

**Direction 3. Do not imagine God to be far away from you, but think of him as always near you and with you, in whose present love and goodness you subsist.**

Nearness of objects excites the faculties: we hear no sound, nor smell any odor, nor taste any sweetness, nor see any colors, that are too distant from us. And the mind, being limited in its activity, neglects, or does not reach, things too distant, and requires some nearness of its object, as well as the sense does; especially for the excitation of affections and bodily action. A distant danger does not stir up such fears, nor a distant misery such grief, nor a distant benefit such pleasure, as that which is at hand. Death affects us more deeply when it seems very near, than when we think we have many years yet to live. So, carnal minds are so drowned in flesh, and captivated to sense, that they take little notice of what they do not see, and therefore think of God as absent, because they do not see him: they think of him as confined to heaven, as we think of a friend who is in the East Indies, or at the antipodes, who is, if not out of mind as well as out of sight, yet too distant for us delightfully to converse with.

Remember always, O my soul, that none is so near you as your God. A Seneca could say, of good men, that God is with us, and in us. Nature taught pagans that in him we live, and move, and have our being. Your friend may be absent, but God is never absent from you; he is with you when, as far as other people are concerned, you are alone. The sun is sufficient to illuminate only one part of the earth at once; and therefore must leave the rest in darkness. But God is with you night and day; and there is no night to the soul, so far as it enjoys him. Your life, your health, your love, and joy, are not nearer to you than your God: he is now before you, around you, within you, moving you to good, restraining you from evil, marking and accepting all that is well, disliking and opposing all that is ill. The light of the sun does not more certainly fill the room, and surround you, than God does with his goodness. He is as much at leisure to observe you, to converse with you, to hear and help you, as if you were his only creature: as the sun can as well illuminate every bird and fly, as if it shone on no other creature. Open the eye of faith and reason, and behold your God! Do not forget him, or unbelievingly deny him, and then say, He is not here. Do not say that the sun does not shine, because you shut your eyes. O do not quench your love to God, by feigning him to be out of reach, and occupied with other company! Do not turn to inferior delights, by thinking that he has turned you off to these: and do not love him as an absent friend; but as the friend who is always in your sight, in your bosom, and in your heart; the fuel that is nearest to the flames of love.

**Direction 4. All other graces must do their part in assisting love, and all be exercised in subservience to it, and with an intention, directly or remotely, to promote it.**

Fear and watchfulness must keep away the sin that would extinguish it, and preserve you from that guilt which would frighten away the soul from God. Repentance and mortification must keep away distracting and deceiving objects, which would steal away our love from God. Faith must show us God as present, in all his blessed attributes and perfections. Hope must depend on him, for nearer access and the promised happiness. Prudence must choose the fittest season, and means, and helps for our special approaches to him, and teach us how to avoid hindrances. And obedience must keep us in a fit capacity for communion with him. The mind that is turned loose to wander after worthless things the rest of the day is unfit, in an hour of prayer or meditation, to be occupied with the love of God. It must be the work of the day, and of our lives, to walk in a fitness for it, though we are not always in the immediate, lively exercise of it. To sin willfully one hour, and be occupied with the love of God the next, is as unlikely, as one hour to abuse our parents one hour, and provoke them to correct us, and the next to find the pleasure of their love; or one hour to fall and break one's bones, and the next to run and work as pleasantly as we did before.

And we must see that all other graces are exercised in a just subservience to love; and none of them degenerate into harmful extremes, to the hindrance of this, which is their proper end. When you set yourselves to repent and mourn for sin, it must be from love, and for love: that by sincere lamentation of the injuries you have done to a gracious God, you may be cleansed from the filth that displeases him, and being reconciled to him in Christ, may be fit to return to the exercises and delights of love. When you fear God, let it be with a filial fear, that comes from love, and is only a preservative or restorative for love. Avoid that slavish fear, as a sin, which tends to hatred, and would make you flee from God. Love casts out this tormenting fear, and frees the soul from the spirit of bondage. The devil tempts melancholy persons to live before God as one who is still among bears or lions that are ready to devour him; for he knows how much such a fear is an enemy to love. Satan would never promote such fears, if they were of God, and tended to our good. You never found him promoting your love or delight in God! But he does not care how much he plunges you into distracting terrors. If he can, he will frighten you out of your love, and out of your comforts, and out of your wits. A dull and sluggish sinner he will keep from fear, lest it should awaken him from his sin; but a poor, melancholy, penitent soul he would keep under perpetual terrors: it is so easy for such people to fear, that they may know it is a sinful, excessive fear; for gracious works are not so easy. And resist also all humiliation and grief that do not, immediately or remotely, tend to help your love. A religion that tends only to grief, and terminates in grief, and goes no further, has too much in it of the malice of the enemy to be of God. No tears are desirable, except those that tend to clear the eyes from the filth of sin, that they may see the better the loveliness of God.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture:** Baxter gives no chapter-and-verse reference anywhere in this range, so no NET quotation is placed in the text. The unmarked allusions below are kept in Baxter's wording; the references and NET texts given here are in the notes only and are not in Baxter's text.
- **"in him we live, and move, and have our being"** (Direction 3): probably Acts 17:28 (Baxter attributes it to what "nature taught pagans"; in Acts, Paul cites it from Greek poets). The NET reads: "For in him we live and move about and exist." The NET continues "as even some of your own poets have said, 'For we too are his offspring.'" The NET's "move about and exist" replaces the King James "move, and have our being." We kept Baxter's wording because he does not quote the verse by reference.
- **"Love casts out this tormenting fear"** (Direction 4): an allusion, probably to 1 John 4:18. The NET reads: "There is no fear in love, but perfect love drives out fear because fear has to do with punishment." The NET's "fear has to do with punishment" is close to Baxter's "tormenting," which comes from the King James. Not marked by Baxter.
- **"the spirit of bondage"** (Direction 4): probably Romans 8:15. The NET reads "the spirit of slavery leading again to fear." Not marked by Baxter; kept as his phrase.
- **A Seneca could say ... that God is with us, and in us** (Direction 3): Baxter's own claim about the Roman Stoic writer Seneca; we have not checked the passage he has in mind and add nothing.
- **Word swaps:** pagans (Baxter's "heathens"); happiness (his "felicity"); worthless things (his "vanity"); lovableness / loveliness (his "amiableness," "lovely"); at once (his "presently"); willfully (his "wilfully"); refrain (his "forbear"); flee (his "fly" from God); "fallen" for his "lapsed"; "disordered" for his "distempered"; "sincere" for his "ingenuous"; "hindrance(s)" for "hinderance" and "impediments"; "distracting" for "diverting"; "harmful" for "noxious"; "shut your eyes" for "wink"; "occupied with" for "taken up with" in two places (Directions 3 and 4); "excessive" for "inordinate"; "as far as other people are concerned" (his "as to men").
- **"helps from our special approaches to him"** (Direction 4): we read Baxter's "from" as meaning "for" and wrote "for." Flagged as a judgment of sense.
- **"the East Indies, or at the antipodes":** left as Baxter wrote.
- **"the Being of beings," "apprehended goodness," "superlative love":** Baxter's scholastic phrasing, kept. "Apprehended" means grasped or understood by the mind.
- **"the faculties"** (Direction 3), **"affections"** (emotions and desires), **"mortification"** (putting sinful desires to death), **"carnal"** (unspiritual, ruled by the flesh) and **"melancholy"** (a lasting low or fearful state, tied to the body in Baxter's day): kept as his technical terms; see GLOSSARY.md.
- **"religion"** (Direction 4: "A religion that tends only to grief"): kept; it means practiced piety and devotion.
- **More word swaps:** "chief good" (his "chiefest good"); "Just know yourself" (his "Do but know thyself"); "other company" (his "other converse," a noun meaning conversation or company); "Do not imagine" (his "Imagine not"); "surround" (his "compass ... about"). Where a modern form is close to his (subsist, excitation, feigning, capacity, terminates, allure) we kept his word.
- **"Direct. II," "Direct. III," "Direct. IV":** the numbers belong to the series of Directions within Grand Direction XI (on the love of God); we have kept Baxter's numbering.
- **Drafting note:** no Latin or Greek appears in this range.
