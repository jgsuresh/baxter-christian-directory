# Chapter 3: Grand Direction XI (part 1 of 8)

*This section opens Grand Direction 11, on loving God as our Benefactor, Father, and happiness. It gives Baxter's introduction and the first of his preparatory points, on the nature of love to God (points 1-7).*

**Grand Direction 11. Let it be most deeply engraved on your heart that God is infinitely good and lovable; your grand Benefactor and Father in Christ; the end of all that you are and have; and the everlasting rest and happiness of your soul: see therefore that your inflamed heart be entirely and absolutely offered up to him by the mediation of his Son, to love him, to trust him, to delight in him, to be thankful to him, to glorify him, and through faith to long for the heavenly glory, where all this will be perfectly done forever. And first let us speak of LOVE.**

I did in the first direction persuade you to lay a good foundation in faith and knowledge. In the second I directed you how to live upon Christ. In the third, how to believe practically in the Holy Spirit. In the fourth I directed you to the orderly and practical knowledge of all the attributes of God. In the fifth, how to know God practically in his first grand relation, as he is your Owner. In the sixth, how to know him practically in his second grand relation, as he is your King or Governor; and in subordination to his governing relation. In the seventh I directed you in your relation of disciples to Christ your Teacher. And in the eighth I directed you in your relation of patients to Christ your Physician, and the Holy Spirit as your Sanctifier. In the ninth I directed you in your relation of soldiers to Christ the Captain of your salvation. In the tenth I directed you in the relation of servants to Christ your Master. And now being past those subordinate relations (to the second), I proceed to direct you in your third grand relation to God as your Benefactor, Father, and Happiness. And because there are several great duties in this general, I shall first begin with this of love; and afterwards speak distinctly of the rest.

**Directions for loving God as our Father and Happiness.**

Here I shall first give you these general preparatives (and then give you directions for the exercise of holy love). 1. You must understand the nature of love to God. 2. You must understand the differences of this love. 3. You must understand the reasons for it. 4. And the contraries of it. 5. And the counterfeits of it.

*In the margin: God is not loved as a particular good, but as the universal good.*

I. For understanding its nature observe these things: 1. It is not the love of a particular good, but of the infinite, Universal Good. The creature is a particular good, and our love for it is a particular, limited love, confined as to a point. God is the Universal Good, and our love for him is not limited by the object, but by the narrowness and imperfection of our faculties themselves. As suppose you had a variety of candles in your room, and you had diamonds and other radiant things; you love each of these with a particular love, for their splendor and usefulness; and you more easily observe and feel the motion of this confined love. But light itself, as light, you love with a more universal love; which is greater, but not so noticeably observed. (Not as we speak of notional universals in logic, which have no existence but in particulars; but of the natural, transcendent, infinite good, eternally existent, and arbitrarily appearing in some created particles.) As the love of an infinite light would differ from the love of a candle, and the love of an infinite heat from the love of a fire, and the love of infinite wisdom itself from the love of a wise man, and the love of infinite goodness itself from the love of a good man; so does the love of God from the love of a particular, created good.

*In the margin: Whether God may be the object of emotional love.*

2. Our love for God is not ordinarily so emotional as our love for creatures; because the nearness and perceptibility of the creature promote such perceptible operations. But God is not seen, or felt, or heard, but believed in by faith, and known by reason. And the narrowness of the creature, making resistances, stops, and difficulties, occasions a turbulent emotionalism of love; when the infiniteness of God has no such occasion. Our love for creatures is like the running of a stream in a channel that is too narrow for it, where stops and banks make it go on with a roaring violence; but our love for God is like the brook that slides into the ocean, where it is imperceptibly devoured. Therefore our love for God must principally be perceived, not in violent emotions, but in, 1. A high esteem of him. 2. In the will's adhering to him. 3. And in the effects (to be mentioned soon). Yet when an emotional love is added to these, it may be the most excellent in what it signifies and in what it does. Some philosophers think that God cannot at all be loved with an emotional love, because he is a pure, immaterial Being, and therefore cannot be the object of a material act or motion, such as our emotions are; and, therefore, that it is some idol of the imagination that is so loved. But, 1. If they mean that his pure essence, in itself, is not the immediate object of an emotion, they may say the same of the will itself; for man (at least in the flesh) can have no other volition of God, but as he is apprehended by the intellect. And if by an idol they mean the image of God in the mind, gathered from the appearances of God in creatures, man in the flesh has no other knowledge of him; for here we know him but darkly, enigmatically, and as in a mirror, and have no formal, proper conception of him in his essence. So that the rational powers themselves do not otherwise know and will God's essence, but as represented to us in a mirror. 2. And thus we may also love him with emotion; it being God in his objective being as apprehended by the intellect that we both will and love with emotion. The motion of the soul in the flesh may raise emotions, by the instrumentality of the bodily spirits, toward an immaterial object; which is called the object of those emotions, not merely as emotions, but as the emotions of a rational agent; it being more nearly or primarily the object of the intellect and will, and then of the emotions, as first apprehended by these higher powers. A man may delight in God; or else, how is he our happiness? and yet, we know of no delight which is not an emotion. A man may love his own soul with an emotional love; and yet it is immaterial. When I love my friend with emotion, it is his immaterial soul, and his wisdom, and holiness, which I chiefly love.

*In the margin: What of God is the object of our love.*

3. It is not only for his excellencies and perfections in himself, nor only for his love and benefit to us, that grace causes a sinner to love God; but it is for both jointly; as he is good, and does good, especially to us, in the greatest things.

*In the margin: What is the motive of our first love to God.*

4. Our first special love to God is orderly and rationally to be raised, the belief of his goodness in himself, and his common love and mercy to sinners, manifested in his giving of his Son for the world, and giving men the conditional promise of pardon and salvation, and offering them Christ and eternal life, and all this to us as well as others: and not to be caused by the belief or persuasion of his special, particular, electing, redeeming, or saving love to us above others, who have the same invitations and offers. It is the knowledge of common love and mercy, and not of special love and mercy, as already possessed, that is appointed to be the motive of our first special love to God. (Yet there is in it an apprehension that he is our only possible happiness, and that he will give us a special interest in his favor, if we return by faith in Christ to him.) For, 1. Every man is bound to love God with a special love: but every man is not specially loved by him: and no man is bound to love God as one who specially loves him except those who indeed are so loved by him; for otherwise they were bound to believe a falsehood, and to love that which is not; and grace would be an error and deceit. The object is before the act. God's special love must in itself be before its revelations; and as revealed it must go before our belief of it; and as believed it must go before our loving it, or loving him as such, or for it. 2. The first saving faith is inseparably joined with special love; for Christ is believed in and willed, as the way or means to God as the end (otherwise it is no true faith). And the volition of the end (which is love) is in order of nature before the choice or use of the means as such: and if we must love God as one who specially loves us, in our first love, then we must believe in him as such by our first faith: and if so, it must be to us a revealed truth. But (as it is false to most of those who are bound to believe, so) it is not revealed to the elect themselves: for if it is, it is either by ordinary or extraordinary revelation. If by ordinary, either by Scripture directly, or by evidences in ourselves which Scripture makes the characters of his love. But neither of these; for Scripture does not promise salvation to named, but to described persons; and evidence of special love there is none, before faith, and repentance, and the first love for God. And extraordinary revelation from heaven, by inspiration or angel, is not the ordinary begetter of faith; for faith is the belief of God, speaking to us (now) by his written word. So that where there is no object of love, there can be no love; and where there is no revelation of it to the understanding, there is no object for the will; and until a man first believes and loves God, he has no revelation that God specially loves him. Search as long as you will, you will find no other. 3. If the wicked were condemned for not loving a false or feigned object, it would quiet their consciences in hell when they had detected the deceit, and seen the natural impossibility and contradiction. 4. The first love for God is more a love of desire than of possession; and therefore it may suffice to raise it, that we see a possibility of being forever happy in God, and enjoying him in special love, though yet we do not know that we possess any such love. The nature of the thing proclaims it most rational and due, that we love the infinite Good, who has done so much by the death of his Son, to remove the impediments of our salvation; and is so far reconciled to the world in his death, as by a message of reconciliation, to entreat them to accept of Christ, and pardon, and salvation freely offered them (2 Corinthians 5:19-20); and is himself the offered happiness of the soul. He who dares say that this much does not have an objective sufficiency to engage the soul in special love is a blind undervaluer of wonderful mercy. 5. The first special grace brings no new object for faith or love, but causes a new act upon the formerly revealed object.

5. But our love for God is greatly increased and advantaged afterward by the assurance or persuasion of his particular, special love to us. And therefore all Christians should greatly value such assurance, as the appointed means of advancing them to greater love for God.

6. As we know God here in the mirror of his Son, and word, and creatures, so we most feelingly love him here, as his goodness appears in his works, and graces, and his word, and Son.

7. The nearer we come to perfection, the more we shall love God for himself and his infinite natural goodness and perfections, not casting away the regard for his goodness and love as to ourselves, but most highly regarding himself for himself, as carried to him above ourselves.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). The NET text of 2 Corinthians 5:19-20 was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **2 Corinthians 5:19-20:** Baxter gives this as a reference supporting his sentence, not as a quotation, so no NET words are placed in the text. His wording ("a message of reconciliation, to entreat them to accept of Christ") draws on the verses; the NET has "the message of reconciliation" (v. 19) and "We plead with you on Christ’s behalf, ‘Be reconciled to God!’" (v. 20). His "entreat" is kept as his word. The NET does not change the sense he relies on: God offers reconciliation to the world through the preached message. Baxter reads this as God's common mercy, which grounds the sinner's first love before any assurance of being specially loved.
- **Unmarked allusion** (kept in Baxter's wording): "darkly, enigmatically, and as in a glass" (point 2) echoes 1 Corinthians 13:12 (not cited by Baxter; no reference is added in the text). The King James has "through a glass, darkly"; the NET has "in a mirror indirectly." Baxter's "glass" is rendered "mirror" throughout, which agrees with the NET's noun.
- **Heading and structure:** Baxter's "Grand Direct. XI." is rendered "Grand Direction 11." The opening heading sentence and the review of the ten earlier Grand Directions are kept as written; "the first direction," "the second," etc. in that review probably mean the earlier Grand Directions (unconfirmed).
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): engraven, engraved; amiable, lovable; felicity, happiness; divers, several; refulgent, radiant; splendour, splendor; passionate (love), emotional (love); passions, emotions; sensible(ness) and sensibly, perceptible / perceptibility / noticeably or feelingly (by context; he means what is noticed or felt, not "reasonable"); insensibly, imperceptibly; anon, soon; peculiar, particular or special (by context); conjunct(ly), joined / jointly; highliest, most highly; glass, mirror (the old sense of a looking glass); Christians (capitalized).
- **"significatively and effectively"** (point 2): rendered "in what it signifies and in what it does." He means that an emotional love is a better sign of love and a stronger working cause of obedience than a calm love alone.
- **"arbitrarily appearing in some created particles"** (point 1): left as Baxter wrote; he means that the one infinite good shows itself, as God chooses, in particular created things. We have not tried to give a sharper sense.
- **"corporeal spirits"** (point 2): rendered "bodily spirits." This probably refers to the "spirits" of older physiology, thought to carry movements between soul and body; we have not confirmed his exact usage and keep his idea as stated.
- **"objective being" and "volition":** scholastic terms kept as written. "Objective being" means the way God exists as the object known by the mind; "volition" is an act of the will.
- **"The object is before the act":** kept; it means that something must exist to be loved before the act of loving it.
- **Point 4 syntax:** the first sentence ("is orderly and rationally to be raised, the belief of his goodness...") reads as if a word such as "by" has dropped out before "the belief." We have not repaired it and keep Baxter's construction in modern spelling; the sense is that the first special love is to be raised by the belief of God's goodness and common mercy.
- **Numbering:** Baxter's "I." opens the first preparative (the nature of love to God). Under it his points run 1-4, then 5, 6, 7. Inside point 4 he has an inner list "1." to "5."; the point-5 paragraph that follows the inner list is the one printed on its own line. Both sets are kept as printed, so "5." appears twice in a row.
- **"the elect," "electing, redeeming":** Baxter's theological terms, kept; his argument is that assurance of special love comes after faith, not before it.
- **"undervaluer":** kept as Baxter's word for one who undervalues.
- **"notional universals":** universals that exist only as ideas in logic; kept.
- **"the first direction ... the tenth":** the ten earlier directions are recapped (probably the earlier Grand Directions of this work; we have not confirmed); Baxter's own cross-references have not been checked against other files.
