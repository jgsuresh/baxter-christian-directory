# Chapter 10: Against idleness and sloth, Direction 1: the greatness of the sin

**Direction 1. The first help against sloth is to be well acquainted with the greatness of the sin.**

For no wonder if it is committed by them that think it small. First, therefore, I shall tell you what it is.

1. God himself reckons it with heinous sins. "Pride, fullness of bread, and abundance of idleness" (Ezekiel 16:49) (the very character of the debauched part of the gentry) is said to have been Sodom's sin, that was consumed with fire from heaven. And the Thessalonians were forbidden to keep company with such as lived disorderly and did not work. [574]

2. Idleness is a temporary destruction (as to their use) of all the faculties of mind and body which should be exercised. It is contrary to nature; for nature made our faculties for use. You bury yourselves alive. If it is a sin to hide God's lesser talents, what is it to bury ourselves and all our powers? If it is a pity to see a dead man, because he is useless to the world; is it not a pity and shame to see one voluntarily dead, that makes himself useless by his sloth? Should not the churchyard be the dwelling of the slothful, that he may be nearest them in place that he is nearest to in quality?

3. Idleness and sloth are consumers of all the mercies of God. You are the barren ground where he sows his seed, and none comes up. You return him but a crop of thorns and briers, and such ground is "about to be cursed" (the final curse); "its fate is to be burned" (Hebrews 6:8). Does God daily feed, and clothe, and keep you, and protect and support you, and teach and warn you, and all for nothing? Is idleness that for which he hired you? Will you accuse your Maker of so great imprudence, and your Redeemer of more, as if he created and redeemed you to do nothing, or that which is as bad or worse than nothing? He calls to you, "Why are you standing here all day without work?" (Matthew 20:3, 6). And it is a terrible sentence that such shall receive, "Evil and lazy slave! ... And throw that worthless slave into the outer darkness," etc. (Matthew 25:26).

4. Idleness is a robbing God, who is the Lord of us and all our faculties, and all our service is his due. You rob him of the honor and service that you might have done him by your diligence.

5. And it is a robbing yourselves of all the good to soul or body which by your labor and industry you might have got. The slothful man lies wishing till he perishes (Proverbs 21:25). [575]

6. And it is a robbing of the commonwealth, and of all those to whom your labors, or the fruit of them, were due. You are burdens to the commonwealth, like drones in the hive (Colossians 3:22).

7. Slothfulness is a great consumer of time (as was shown, chapter 5). You lose not only all the time when duty is omitted, but much of the time in which you perform it, while you get through no work, and do it as if you did it not. He that goes but a mile an hour loses his time, though he is still going, even as much as he that goes two miles one hour and sits still the next. Oh what abundance of their lives do idle persons lose! When time is gone, they will better understand the greatness of their sin and loss, that now make light of it.

8. Idleness is not a single sin, but a continued course of sinning: an idle person is sinning all the while he is idle; and that is with some a great part of their lives: and therefore it is the greater, because the continuance shows that it is not effectually repented of.

9. Idleness is a destroyer of grace, and gifts, and natural abilities; they will rust for want of use. "The one who is slack in his work is a brother to one who destroys" (Proverbs 18:9). Weeds will grow up and choke the fruit.

10. Idleness and sloth is a fruit of flesh-pleasing; and so comes from the most pernicious vice. It is but to please the flesh that one is drunk, and another gluttonous, and another a fornicator, and another greedy; and your idleness and sloth is but pleasing the same flesh in another way, which is forbidden as well as those. "For if you live according to the flesh, you will die" (Romans 8:13).

11. It is a strengthening the flesh against the Spirit, by indulging it in its ease and sloth; and makes it not only unruly and unserviceable, but masterful and earnest for its own desires.

12. Idleness is the mother and nurse of many heinous sins. 1. It cherishes lust, and draws people to fornication, which hard labor would have much prevented. 2. It is the time for foolish amusements, and empty pursuits, and wantonness, and excess of debauchery, and all the mischiefs which usually follow it. 3. It is the time for idle talk, and meddling with other folks' matters: and therefore Paul rebukes the idle as busybodies, or meddlers with matters that do not concern them, and chatterers, and talebearers (2 Thessalonians 3:11; 1 Timothy 5:13; 1 Thessalonians 4:11). [576] They that do not do what they should, will be doing what they should not. 4. It is the time for gluttony, and drunkenness, and gambling, and all other indulgence in physical pleasure. 5. Indeed, it is the time for seditions and rebellions; as in armies it is the time for mutinies.

13. Idleness is the season of temptation: it is Satan's seedtime. It is then that he has opportunity to tempt men to malice, revenge, and all other villainy that is committed.

14. Idleness is "living an undisciplined life" (2 Thessalonians 3:10–11); out of the way that God has appointed us to eat our bread in, and receive his blessing in. The large description of a virtuous woman, Proverbs 31:10 to the end, is worthy to be studied by the slothful. "She sought out wool and flax, then worked happily with her hands. She was like the merchant ships; she would bring in her food from afar. Then she rose while it was still night, and provided food for her household and a portion to her female servants. She considered a field and bought it; from her own income she planted a vineyard. She clothed herself in might, and she strengthened her arms. She perceived that her merchandise was good. Her lamp would not go out in the night. She extended her hands to the spool, and her hands grasped the spindle. She opened her hand to the poor, and extended her hands to the needy. She would not fear for her household in winter, because all her household were clothed with scarlet. ... Watching over the ways of her household, she would not eat the bread of idleness." [577] I desire our ladies and gentlewomen that take this pattern to be beneath them to remember that it was not a plowman, but a king, and that the greatest that ever Israel had, that gave this counsel as received from his mother: who concludes, verses 30, 31, "Charm is deceitful and beauty is fleeting. A woman who fears the Lord—she makes herself praiseworthy. Give her credit for what she has accomplished, and let her works praise her in the city gates." But if our gallants should have no food or clothing but what were the fruit of their hands, it would make a foul change in their garb and diet! And if their own works must be the matter of their praise, instead of the names of their ancestors, arms, lands, and titles, it would also make a foul change in their honors! [578]

15. Idleness usually brings poverty; and it is a just and merciful chastisement of God to cure the sin: but such can have little comfort in their needs; nor expect that others should pity them, as they would do the diligent. Indeed, many, when by idleness they are brought to poverty, by poverty are brought to murmuring and stealing, to the ruin both of soul and body, and family and reputation.

16. Idleness is a murderer of the body. Gluttony and idleness kill most of the world before their time: no two sins more constantly bring this curse along with them.

17. Idleness makes you the shame of the creation. Do you not see how all the world is in action? how the sun runs his course for you, the waters flow, the ground brings forth, your cattle labor for you; and all things that are most excellent are most active; and all things that are most inactive are most vile, and dead, and drossy. The Scripture sends the slothful even to the ant to learn to labor (Proverbs 6:6). And shall the ant, the bee, and every creature be witnesses against you to condemn your sloth?

18. Lastly, idleness disables you from doing good to others: you should "labor, doing good with his own hands, so that he will have something to share with the one who has need" (Ephesians 4:28). Or if you give out of your surplus that which cost you no labor, it is not so much to your honor or comfort as if you were purposely thrifty and hardworking to do good; he that pleases his flesh with ease and fullness, and gives his leavings (however much) to the poor, will never have that comfort and evidence of God's acceptance and grace in it, as he that does not pamper his flesh by his abundance, but gives that to the poor which he gets with his diligence, and which he denies to his inordinate desires.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows the Latin in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[574] 1 Thessalonians 5:12–13; Proverbs 18:9; 21:25; 2 Thessalonians 3; Proverbs 12:24; 12:15; Ecclesiastes 10:18.

[575] Proverbs 10:26; 18:9.

[576] Proverbs 26:16; 24:30.

[577] See Psalm 128:2, "You will eat what you worked so hard to grow." Proverbs 14:23; 13:11.

[578] Cleanthes coactum aliquando stipem in medium familiarium intulit, dicens, Cleanthes alium Cleanthem posset nutrire si vellet. (Cleanthes once brought into the midst of his acquaintances the money he had gathered, saying, Cleanthes could feed another Cleanthes if he wished.) And when he was questioned in court how he lived, Adeo robustus, et tam boni habitûs (so robust, and of so good a bodily condition), the gardener that he worked for, and the woman that baked his meal, were the witnesses that acquitted him. Hard labor and hard fare enabled him for hard study. Laertius, on Cleanthes.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). Every NET quotation here was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Where this file sits:** this continues Chapter 10, Part 1, **Title 2** ("Directions against idleness and sloth"). "Title" (Baxter's "_Tit._") is simply his word for a heading or division within the Part. The range begins at the start of Direction 1 and ends with the last of its eighteen numbered points; the Direction may continue in the next file.
- **Ezekiel 16:49 (important):** Baxter quotes the King James, "Pride, fulness of bread, and abundance of idleness," and his whole point is the word "idleness." The NET reads "She and her daughters had majesty, abundance of food, and enjoyed carefree ease": it has neither "pride" nor "idleness." Because his quoted words are not in the NET verse, we kept Baxter's wording (modernized) in his quotation marks and give the NET here. Flagged.
- **Hebrews 6:8:** NET wording, "about to be cursed ... its fate is to be burned," for the King James "nigh unto cursing; whose end is to be burned." Baxter's parenthesis "(the final curse)" is his gloss, kept.
- **Matthew 20:3, 6:** Baxter quotes "Why stand ye idle?" (King James 20:6). The NET reads "Why are you standing here all day without work?" The word "idle," which Baxter leans on, is gone, though the sense (not working) remains. Flagged. Verse 3 in the NET ("standing around in the marketplace without work") has no question; the quotation is from verse 6.
- **Matthew 25:26 (and 30):** Baxter cites only 25:26 but his quotation joins verse 26 ("Thou wicked and slothful servant") with verse 30 ("cast ye the unprofitable servant into outer darkness"). We give the NET wording of both, with "..." where he skips, and keep his single reference. The NET has "Evil and lazy slave!" and "worthless slave" for "wicked and slothful servant" and "unprofitable servant." "Unprofitable" fit Baxter's theme of uselessness; "worthless" keeps it roughly.
- **Proverbs 21:25:** Baxter gives this without quotation marks, as a paraphrase ("The slothful man lieth wishing till he perish"); we kept his wording, modernized. The NET reads "What the sluggard desires will kill him, for his hands have refused to work."
- **Proverbs 18:9:** NET wording. The King James "The slothful is brother to the great waster" becomes "The one who is slack in his work is a brother to one who destroys." Baxter uses it for "rust for want of use"; the NET's "destroys" still fits, but "waster" (squandering) is softened.
- **Romans 8:13:** NET wording. Baxter's quotation begins "And if ye live after the flesh"; the NET verse begins "(for if you live according to the flesh, you will die)". We used the NET's "For."
- **2 Thessalonians 3:10–11:** Baxter's "a disorderly walking" is the King James ("walk ... disorderly"). The NET reads "living an undisciplined life" (verse 11). The NET keeps the sense of not keeping to one's work. In item 1, "the Thessalonians were forbidden to keep company with such as lived disorderly and did not work" is Baxter's unquoted summary of 2 Thessalonians 3:6–14 (our reference, not in his text).
- **2 Thessalonians 3:11; 1 Timothy 5:13; 1 Thessalonians 4:11** (item 12): cited, not quoted. Baxter's "busy-bodies ... twattlers, and tale-carriers" echo the King James of 1 Timothy 5:13 ("tattlers also and busybodies"). "Twattlers" (idle chatterers) is rendered **chatterers**; "tale-carriers" is **talebearers**.
- **Proverbs 31:13–21, 27:** NET wording. Baxter says the description runs from verse 10 to the end, but his quotation begins at verse 13 and runs to verse 21 (ending "clothed with scarlet"), then skips (his "--", our "...") to verse 27. The NET uses past tense throughout ("She sought out wool"), where the King James has present tense ("She seeketh"). Other differences: "snow" becomes "winter"; "spindle ... distaff" becomes "spool ... spindle"; "with the fruit of her hands she planteth a vineyard" becomes "from her own income she planted a vineyard"; "worketh willingly" becomes "worked happily." The NET verse 21 continues into verse 22 with a comma; we end the quotation at "scarlet" with a period, as Baxter does.
- **Proverbs 31:30–31 (important):** NET wording. Baxter's next sentence leans on the King James "Give her of the fruit of her hands" ("if our gallants should have no meat or clothing but what were the fruit of their hands"). The NET reads "Give her credit for what she has accomplished," which drops "the fruit of her hands," so Baxter's follow-on reads less directly from the verse. The NET also has "Charm is deceitful and beauty is fleeting" for "Favour is deceitful, and beauty is vain," and "she makes herself praiseworthy" for "she shall be praised." "Let her works praise her in the city gates" keeps the phrase his last sentence builds on. Flagged.
- **"a king ... the greatest that ever Israel had ... from his mother":** Proverbs 31:1 attributes the chapter to "King Lemuel," taught by his mother. Baxter identifies the king as the greatest king of Israel (presumably Solomon, a traditional identification of Lemuel). Kept as Baxter's view.
- **Colossians 3:22 (item 6):** this verse (NET: "Slaves, obey your earthly masters in every respect...") says nothing about drones or idleness, so the reference may be a misprint in the source. We have not found the intended verse and keep Baxter's reference.
- **Proverbs 6:6:** cited, not quoted ("Go to the ant, you sluggard").
- **Ephesians 4:28:** Baxter adapts the verse to the second person ("work with your own hands, that you may have to give to him that needeth"). The NET is third person: "he must labor, doing good with his own hands, so that he will have something to share with the one who has need." We quote the NET from "labor"; the shift from "you" to "his/he" inside the quotation is the NET's person, not an error.
- **Psalm 128:2 (note 577):** NET wording. Baxter quotes the King James "Thou shalt eat the labour of thy hands"; the NET reads "You will eat what you worked so hard to grow," which loses "of thy hands," the phrase that ties the verse to Proverbs 31.
- **Proverbs 12:15 (note 574):** printed "xii. 15" (on the fool who is right in his own eyes); it may be a misprint, but we have not confirmed one and keep it.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): hiding "God's lesser talents" (Matthew 25:25); "thorns and briers" (Hebrews 6:8, Genesis 3:18); "excess of riot" (1 Peter 4:4, King James); "Weeds will grow up and choke the fruit" (compare Matthew 13:7); "the flesh against the Spirit" (Galatians 5:17); Sodom "consumed with fire from heaven" (Genesis 19:24).
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): unuseful, *useless*; rid (no work), *get through* (no work); natural parts, *natural abilities*; covetous, *greedy*; masterly (domineering, bent on mastery), *masterful*; sports, *amusements*; vanity, *empty pursuits*; excess of riot (dissolute excess, not civil disorder), *excess of debauchery*; which use to follow, *which usually follow*; reprehendeth, *rebukes*; gaming, *gambling*; sensuality, *indulgence in physical pleasure*; yea, *indeed*; meat (food in general), *food*; below them, *beneath them*; wants, *needs*; unactive, *inactive*; superfluity, *surplus*; laborious, *hardworking*; how much soever, *however much*; questioned in judgment (note 578), *questioned in court*; acquit (old past tense), *acquitted*.
- **Kept words:** *gentry* (the landed class below the nobility); *gallants* (fashionable young men and women of means); *garb* (dress); *arms* (coats of arms, the family insignia); *wantonness* (lewdness, unruly self-indulgence); *murmuring* (grumbling complaint, a biblical word); *drossy* (like dross, the waste skimmed off molten metal: worthless); *cattle* (in 1673 any livestock, not only cows); *talents* (the coins of the parable, and so God-given abilities); *commonwealth* (the community or state); *chastisement*; *leavings* (leftovers); *inordinate* (excessive, beyond due measure); *evidence* (proof, here of God's grace in a person).
- **Person shift:** Baxter speaks to "you" through most of the list and to "thee" in item 17; both are rendered "you."
- **chapter 5** (item 7): Baxter's cross-reference to his earlier chapter on redeeming time; we have not checked the exact place.
- **Cleanthes (note 578):** the Stoic philosopher who succeeded Zeno; as we understand Laertius's account, he did manual work (such as drawing water for a gardener) to support his study, but we have not checked the passage. Baxter cites "Laert. in Cleanth.," that is, Diogenes Laertius's life of Cleanthes. The English sentence in Baxter's note is somewhat broken (the Latin phrase "so robust, and of so good a bodily condition" stands loose in the middle); we kept its shape. "Meal" is ground grain, not a repast.
- **Baxter's social polemic:** his remarks on "the debauched part of the gentry," "our ladies and gentlewomen," and "our gallants" are his own sharp words against the idle upper classes of his day, kept as written.
- **"Idleness and sloth is"** (item 10) and **"its own desires"**: Baxter's grammar, kept.
