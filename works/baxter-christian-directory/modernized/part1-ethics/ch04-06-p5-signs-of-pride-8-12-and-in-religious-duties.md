# Chapter 4: Signs of Pride, Signs 8-12, and the Signs of Pride in Religious Duties, Signs 1-5

*This range begins partway through Baxter's Direction 2 (Observe the motions and discoveries of pride), in its list of signs, at Sign 8; the heading of the Direction and of the section (Directions against Pride, and for Humility) are in earlier files. It ends partway through the second list of signs (Sign 5 of the signs of pride in religious duties); Baxter's list continues in the next file.*

**Sign 8.** A proud man is a great undervaluer of all mercies, and unthankful for them; but especially for spiritual mercy. He receives it customarily, as if it were his due; and customarily gives God thanks. But though he may rejoice in the prosperity of his flesh, yet he is a stranger to holy thankfulness to God; and thinks slightingly of mercy; indeed, he is discontented, and murmurs if God does not give him as much as he desires. Whereas the humble confess themselves unworthy of the least, Genesis 32:10; 2 Chronicles 32:24-26. Hezekiah's lifting up and unthankfulness go together. A poor man will be very thankful for a penny or a piece of bread, which the rich would reject as a great indignity.

**Sign 9.** Proud men are always impatient in their afflictions. If they have a toughness or stupidity, yet they do not have Christian patience: they take it as if God treated them harshly, or did them wrong. But the humble know that they deserve much worse, and that the mercy that is left them is contrary to their deserving; and therefore say with the humbled church, "I must endure the Lord's fury, for I have sinned against him" (Micah 7:9). "It is because his compassions do not fail, that we are not consumed" (Lamentations 3:22).

**Sign 10.** Proud men are fearless of temptations, and confident of their strength and the goodness of their hearts. They dare live among snares, in pomp and pleasure, faring luxuriously every day; among plays, and gaming, and lascivious company and talk, and fear no hurt; their pride making them unaware of their danger, and what tinder and gunpowder there is in their natures, for every spark of temptations to catch fire in. But the humble are always suspicious of themselves, and know their danger, and avoid the snare. "A wise person is cautious and turns from evil, but a fool throws off restraint and is overconfident" (Proverbs 14:16). "A shrewd person saw danger and hid himself, but the naive passed on by and paid for it" (Proverbs 22:3).

**Sign 11.** Pride makes men murmur if the work of God is never so well done, if they did not have the doing of it; and sometimes by contending to have the honor of doing it, they destroy the work. If they are officers of Christ, they look more at the power than their obligation; at the dignity than at the duty; and at what the people owe to them, than what they owe to God and to the people. They are like dogs that snarl at any other that would partake with them, or come into the house. They do not say as Moses, "I wish that all the Lord's people were prophets." Indeed, the peace and unity of church and state is often sacrificed to this cursed pride.

**Sign 12.** Pride makes men ashamed of the service of God, in a time and place where it is disgraced by the world; and if it has dominion, Christ and holiness will be denied or forsaken by them, rather than their honor with men will be forsaken. If they come to Jesus, it is as Nicodemus did, by night: they are ashamed to own a reproached truth, or scorned cause, or servant of Christ. If men will but mock them with the nicknames or calumnies hatched in hell, they will do as others, or refrain from their duty: a scorn will do more to make them refrain from praying in their families to God, than the lion's den would do with Daniel, or the fiery furnace with the three confessors, Daniel 3 and 6. Especially if they are persons of honor and greatness in the world, then God must be merciful to them while they bow down in the house of Rimmon. As the rich man, when he heard Christ's terms, "he became very sad, for he was extremely wealthy" (Luke 18:23); so these, because their honors and dignities are so great, do think them too good to let go for the sake of Christ. Had they but the share of the obscure common people to lay down, they could forsake it; but they cannot forsake so fair a portion, nor endure the reproach of so honorable a name. But oh what contemptible things are these to a humble soul! He marvels what dreaming worldlings find, in the doting thoughts and breath of fools, which men call honor, that they should prefer it to the honor of God, and their real honor; when Christ has told them that "whoever shall be ashamed of him and his words, in an adulterous and sinful generation, of him also will the Son of Man be ashamed, when he comes in the glory of his Father with his holy angels" (Mark 8:38). I now proceed to the signs of pride in particular duties.

**The Signs of Pride in and about Religious Duties.**

**Sign 1.** A proud person is most solicitous in and about that part of duty which is visible to man, and tends to advance him in men's esteem: and therefore he is more regardful of the outside, than of the inside; of the words, than of the heart. [214] He takes much pains, if he is a preacher, to cast his sermon into such a form as tends to set forth his parts, according to the rank of those he would please. If he lives where wit is valued above grace, or pedantic jingling above a solid, clear, judicious, masculine speech, he bends himself to the humor of his listeners, and acts his part as a stage-player for applause. If he lives where serious, earnest exhortations are in greater demand, he studies to put an affected fervency into his style, which may make the hearers believe that he believes himself, and to seem to be what indeed he is not, and to feel what he does not feel: but all this while about his heart he is little solicitous; and takes small pains to affect it with the reverence of God, and with a due estimation of his truth, and a due compassion for men's souls, and indeed to believe and feel what he would seem to believe and feel. So also in prayer and conversation, his chief study is to speak so as may best procure applause; and it is seldom that he is so cunning as to hide this design of his from the observation of judicious men that know him: they may usually perceive that he is the image of a preacher or Christian, by affectation forcing himself to that which he is not truly serious in. He is sounding brass, a tinkling cymbal, a bladder full of wind, a skin full of words; wise and devout in public on the stage, but at home and with his companions in his ordinary conversation, he is but common, if not unclean. He is the admiration of fools, and the compassion of the wise; an oracle at the first meeting to those that do not know him, and the pity of those that have seen him at home, and without his mask: he is like proud gentlewomen that spend a great part of the morning in cleansing and adorning themselves when they are to be seen, and go abroad, but at home are very plain. And usually the proud, being hypocrites, are secret haters of the most serious, and judicious Christians; because these are more quick-sighted than others, to see through the cloak of their hypocrisy; unless as their charity, constraining them to conceal their fears and jealousies, may reconcile the hypocrite to them.

**Sign 2.** Proud men are apt to push themselves forward to any public duty which may tend to magnify them or set out their parts, and think themselves fitter to be preferred before others, and employed, than indeed they are. [215] They are forward to speak in preaching or praying among others, or in ordinary talk; a little knowledge makes them think that they are fit to be preachers: whereas the humble say with Moses, "Who am I that I should go to Pharaoh?" etc. (Exodus 3:11). "I am not an eloquent man ... I am slow of speech ... O my Lord, please send anyone else whom you wish to send!" (Exodus 4:13). Or, as Isaiah, chapter 6:5, "Woe to me! I am destroyed, for my lips are contaminated by sin" etc.; or as Paul, "Who is adequate for these things?" (2 Corinthians 2:16). How many a sermon has pride both studied and preached! And how many a prayer has it formed! And how well are they likely to be heard by God!

**Sign 3.** The proud are loath to be clouded by the greater abilities of others: they are content that weaker men pray or preach with them, that will not obscure but set off their parts, that they may have the pre-eminence; as a dwarf, that makes another seem a proper man. They are less troubled that God and the gospel is dishonored by the infirmities, insufficiency, and faults of others, than that their glory is obscured by worthier men, though God is honored and his work promoted. [216] Whereas the humbled person wishes from the bottom of his heart, that all the Lord's people were prophets, that all men could preach, and pray, and talk, and live much better than he does himself, though he would also be as good as they. He is glad when he hears any speak more judiciously, powerfully, and convincingly than he, rejoicing that God's work is done, whoever does it; for he loves wisdom and holiness, truth and duty, not only because it is his own, but for itself, and for God, and for the souls of others. A proud man envies both the parts, and work, and honor of others; and is like the devil, complaining at the gifts of God; and the better and wiser any one is the more he envies him: he is an enemy to the fruits of God's beneficence; as if he would have God less good and bountiful to the world, or to any but himself, and such as will serve his party, and interest, and honor with their gifts: his eye is evil because God is good. If others are better spoken of than himself, as more learned, able, wise, or holy, it kindles in his breast a secret hatred of them, unless they are such whose honor is his honor, or contributes thereto; whereas the holy, humble soul, is sorry that he lacks what others have, but glad that others have what he lacks. He loves God's gifts wherever he sees them; indeed, though it were in one that hates him. He would not have the world to be shut up in a perpetual night, because he may not be the sun; but would have them receive that by another which he cannot give them, and is glad that they have a sun though it is not he. Though some preached Christ of envy and strife, of contention, and not sincerely, to add afflictions to his bonds, yet Paul rejoiced, and would rejoice, that Christ was preached, Philippians 1:15-18.

**Sign 4.** When the proud man is praying or preaching, his eye is principally upon the hearers, and from them it is that his work is animated, and from them that he fetches principally the fire or motives for his zeal. He is thinking principally of their case, and all the while fishing for their love, and approval, and applause; and where he cannot have it, the fire of his zeal goes out. Whereas, though the humble in a secondary way look at men, and would do all to edification, yet it is not to be loved by them, so much as to exercise love upon them; nor to seek for honor and esteem from them, so much as to convert and save them: and it is God that he chiefly eyes and regards; and from him that he fetches his most powerful motives; and it is his approval that he expects: his eye and heart is so upon the listeners as to be more upon God; he would feed the sheep, but would please the Lord and Owner of them.

**Sign 5.** A proud man after his duty is more inquisitive how he was liked by men, and what they think or say of him, than whether God and conscience give him their approval. He has his scouts to tell him whether he is honored or dishonored: this is the return of prayer that he looks after; this is the fruit of preaching which he seeks to reap. But these are unimportant things to a serious, humble soul; he has God to please, his work to do, and does not set much by human judgment.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[214] His ergo qui loquendi arta cæteris hominibus excellere videntur, sedulo monendi sunt ut humilitate induti christiana discant non contemnere quos cognoverint morum vitia quam verborum amplius devitare, Aug. de Cat. rudib. c. 9. (English, rendered loosely: Those, therefore, who seem to excel other men in the art of speaking are to be earnestly warned that, clothed in Christian humility, they learn not to despise those whom they know to avoid faults of conduct more than faults of words. Augustine, *De catechizandis rudibus*, ch. 9.)

[215] Non potest non indoctus esse, qui se doctum credit. Hermar. Barbarus. (English: He cannot fail to be unlearned who believes himself learned. Hermar. Barbarus; see the translator's notes.)

[216] Pliny says, In commending another you do yourself right; for he whom you commend is either superior or inferior to you; if he is inferior, if he is to be commended, then you much more; if he is superior, if he is not to be commended, then you much less. Lord Bacon, Essay 54. p. 299.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). Every quoted verse below was fetched from labs.bible.org, not quoted from memory.
- **Where this begins:** Baxter heads each item "Sign" with a Roman numeral. We render them "Sign 8." and so on, as bold labels. The second list (Sign 1 onward) is a fresh numbering under the heading "The Signs of Pride in and about Religious Duties."
- **Micah 7:9 (flagged):** Baxter's "I will bear the indignation of the Lord" is the King James. The NET reads "I must endure the Lord's fury," which stresses that it has to be borne; "indignation" is milder than "fury." Baxter quotes only the first half of the verse; we do the same.
- **Lamentations 3:22 (flagged):** Baxter's words, "It is because his compassions fail not, that we are not consumed," follow the King James. The NET reads "The Lord's loyal kindness never ceases; his compassions never end," with no "not consumed" clause. Because Baxter's wording is not in the NET verse, we keep his wording, modernized, and do not give it as NET. The "not consumed" sense is what suits his point about the humble.
- **Proverbs 14:16 (flagged):** NET wording. The King James has "feareth," which Baxter's argument (the humble are fearful and suspicious of themselves) leans on; the NET has "is cautious." Likewise "rageth" is "throws off restraint."
- **Proverbs 22:3 (flagged):** NET wording. Baxter's "A prudent man foreseeth the evil" becomes "A shrewd person saw danger," which loses the sense of foresight; "the simple ... are punished" becomes "the naive ... paid for it."
- **Moses' words (Sign 11):** Baxter writes "Would all the Lord's people were prophets" in quotation marks, with no reference. This matches Numbers 11:29; the NET has "I wish that all the Lord's people were prophets, that the Lord would put his Spirit on them!" We quote the NET to the extent Baxter did. The reference is not in Baxter's text. In Sign 3 he repeats the thought without quotation marks, and we left it in his wording.
- **Luke 18:23:** NET wording ("he became very sad, for he was extremely wealthy"). Baxter writes "was very sorrowful, for he was very rich." He builds a parallel between the rich man's wealth and the honors of the proud; the NET keeps that.
- **Mark 8:38 (corrected reference; paraphrase):** Baxter prints "Mark viii. 31," but 8:31 is about the Son of Man's coming suffering. The words he quotes match 8:38. His quotation is also adapted: "ashamed of him and his words" (third person) and "with his holy angels," where the King James has "of me and of my words" and "with the holy angels." Because it is his paraphrase, we keep his wording, modernized. The NET reads: "For if anyone is ashamed of me and my words in this adulterous and sinful generation, the Son of Man will also be ashamed of him when he comes in the glory of his Father with the holy angels."
- **Exodus 3:11 and 4:13 (Sign 2):** NET wording. Baxter's second quotation joins two verses: "I am not eloquent, but slow of speech" is from 4:10, and "O my Lord, send I pray thee by the hand of him whom thou wilt send" is from 4:13. He cites only 4:13. In the NET, 4:10 reads "I am not an eloquent man ... I am slow of speech and slow of tongue" and 4:13 reads "O my Lord, please send anyone else whom you wish to send!" We use the NET with "..." between them and keep Baxter's one reference. The King James phrase "by the hand of him whom thou wilt send" is lost in the NET.
- **Isaiah 6:5 (corrected reference; flagged):** Baxter prints "chap. xvi. 5" (Isaiah 16:5 is about a king established on a throne). The words are Isaiah 6:5. The NET reads "Woe to me! I am destroyed, for my lips are contaminated by sin, and I live among people whose lips are contaminated by sin." Baxter's "undone" becomes "destroyed," and "a man of unclean lips" becomes "my lips are contaminated by sin," so the words "unclean" and "man" are gone. We quote the NET as far as Baxter's extent.
- **2 Corinthians 2:16:** NET "Who is adequate for these things?" Baxter has "sufficient." (The NET verse begins "And who ..."; we quote from "Who.")
- **Genesis 32:10 (corrected reference):** Baxter prints "Gen. xxx. 10." That verse (NET: "Soon Leah's servant Zilpah gave Jacob a son") has nothing to do with mercies. Jacob's prayer in Genesis 32:10 (King James: "not worthy of the least of all the mercies") fits Baxter's point, "unworthy of the least." This correction is our judgment, not confirmed from another source. The NET reads "I am not worthy of all the faithful love you have shown your servant," which has no "least."
- **2 Chronicles 32:24-26:** cited only. For reference, the NET says Hezekiah "was ungrateful; he had a proud attitude" (v. 25) and later "humbled" himself (v. 26).
- **Philippians 1:15-18; Daniel 3 and 6:** cited only.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): Nicodemus coming "by night" (John 3:2); "the house of Rimmon" (2 Kings 5:18); "sounding brass, a tinkling cymbal" (1 Corinthians 13:1); "his eye is evil because God is good" (compare Matthew 20:15); "faring deliciously every day" (compare Luke 16:19, King James "fared sumptuously every day"); "feed the sheep" (compare John 21:15-17).
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): diminutively to slightingly; faring deliciously to faring luxuriously; stoutness to toughness (he means a hardened or defiant endurance, not size); used them hardly to treated them harshly; contrary to their desert to contrary to their deserving; insensible to unaware; discourse to talk, conversation, or speech (by context; in Sign 1, "masculine discourse" is "masculine speech," meaning vigorous and manly speech); auditors to listeners; approbation to approval; subordinately to in a secondary way; inconsiderable to unimportant; congress ("an oracle at the first congress") to meeting; mundifying to cleansing; homely to plain (not ugly; unadorned); gingling to jingling; repining at to complaining at; wants (lacks) to lacks; forbear to refrain from; vulgar to common people; proportion to share; quality (of those he would please) to rank; "put on themselves to" to "push themselves forward to"; "put off their parts" to "set off their parts" (display them to advantage); like to be to likely to be; "heard of God" to "heard by God"; yea to indeed; nick-names to nicknames.
- **Kept words:** *parts* (talents or abilities); *solicitous* (anxious, concerned); *humor* (disposition or mood); *studies* (takes pains, applies himself); *jealousies* (suspicions); *edification* (building up in faith); *confessors* (those who confess the faith under persecution; here Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego); *proper man* (a fine, well-built man); *worldlings*; *stage-player* (actor); *pedantic*; *judicious*. "Wit is valued above grace" means cleverness above godliness.
- **"never so well done":** Baxter's idiom for "however well done." Left as written.
- **Hermar. Barbarus (note 215):** the abbreviation is probably meant for a humanist writer named Barbarus (Ermolao Barbaro, also called Hermolaus Barbarus, is a likely candidate); we have not confirmed it.
- **Latin in note 214:** "arta" is printed so in the source; the expected Latin is probably "arte" (the art, by ablative). We left it as printed. The draft translation is loose because of the awkward construction of "quos cognoverint ... devitare."
- **Pliny; Lord Bacon (note 216):** Baxter's English note attributes the thought to Pliny and cites Bacon's Essay 54. We have not checked the essay or the page number.
- **Baxter's polemic:** none in this range.
