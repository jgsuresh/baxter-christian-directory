# Chapter 1, Part 1: Direction 6

**Direction 6. Do not allow the devil, by company, pleasure, or worldly business, to divert or hinder you from these serious considerations.**

The devil has but two ways to procure your damnation. The one is by keeping you from any sober remembrance of spiritual and eternal things; and the other is, if you will insist on thinking of them, to deceive you into false, mistaken thoughts. To bring to pass the first of these (which is the most common, powerful means), his ordinary way is by diversion; [24] finding you always something else to do; putting some other thoughts into your mind, and some other work into your hand; so that you can never have leisure for any sober thoughts of God: whenever the Spirit of God knocks at your door, you are so taken up with other company, or other business, that you cannot hear, or will not open to him. Many a time he has been ready to teach you, but you were not at leisure to hear and learn. Many a time he secretly jogged your conscience, and checked you in your sin, and called you aside to consider soberly about your spiritual and everlasting state, when the noise of foolish mirth and pleasures, or the bustle of encumbering cares and business, has caused you to stop your ears, and put him off, and refuse the prompting. And if the abused Spirit of God departs, and leaves you to your beloved mirth and business, and to yourself, it is but just; and then you will never have a serious, effective thought of heaven, perhaps, until you have lost it; nor a sober thought of hell until you are in it; unless it be some despairing, or some dull, ineffective thought.

O therefore, as you love your soul, do not love your pleasure or business so well as to refuse to confer with the Spirit of God, who comes to offer you greater pleasures, and to engage you in a more important business. O set aside all, to hear awhile what God and conscience have to say to you. They have greater business with you than any others that you keep company with. They have better offers and proposals to make to you than you shall hear from any of your old companions. If the devil can but keep you busy a while, with one pleasure one day, and another business another day, and keep you from the work that you came into the world for, until time is gone, and you have slipped unawares into damnation, then he has his desire, and has the end he aimed at, and has won the day, and you are lost forever.

It is likely you set some limits to your folly, and purpose to do this but a little while: but when one pleasure withers, the devil will provide a fresh one for you; and when one business is over, which causes you to claim necessity, another, and another, and another will succeed, and you will think you have such necessity still, until time is gone, and you see, too late, how grossly you were deceived. Resolve, therefore, that whatever company, or pleasure, or business would divert you, you will not be fooled out of your salvation, nor taken off from minding the one thing necessary. If company claims an interest in you, ask them whether they are better company than the Spirit of God and your conscience; if pleasure would detain you, inquire whether it is purer and more lasting pleasure than you may have in heaven, by listening to grace; if business still claims necessity, inquire whether it is a greater business than to prepare your soul and your accounts for judgment, and of greater necessity than your salvation. If not, do not let it have priority: if you are wise, do that first that must necessarily be done; and let that stand by that may best be spared. What will it profit you to win all the world, and lose your soul? At least, if you dared say that your pleasure and business are better than heaven, yet they might sometimes be set aside while you seriously think of your salvation.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced exactly as in the source. A draft English translation follows the Latin passage in parentheses; it was prepared for this project and is not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check it before relying on fine points. Baxter's English in the note is modernized.

[24] Even learning and honest studies may be used as a diversion from more necessary things. Petrarch says, in Vita Sua (in his own *Life*), Ingenio sui ad omne bonum et salubre studium apto; sed ad moralem præcipue philosophiam, et ad poeticam prono. Quam ipsam processu temporis neglexi, sacris literis delectatus, in quibus sensi dulcedinem abditam, quam aliquando contempseram; poeticis literis non nisi ad ornamentum reservatis. (A mind suited to every good and wholesome study, but especially inclined to moral philosophy and to poetry. In time I neglected the poetry itself, delighting in sacred letters, in which I sensed a hidden sweetness that I had once despised; poetic letters being kept only for ornament. The Latin mixes third and first person; rendered loosely.)

## Translator's notes

- **No Scripture is quoted in quotation marks in this section.** Baxter alludes to three passages without naming them. We kept his wording, modernized, and did not substitute NET text for these: "the Spirit of God knocks at your door" (Revelation 3:20, where Christ knocks); "what will it profit you to win all the world, and lose your soul?" (Matthew 16:26; the NET reads "what will it benefit a person if he gains the whole world but forfeits his soul?"); "the one thing necessary" (Luke 10:42). Scripture quotations elsewhere are from the NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **allow** (heading): Baxter's "suffer." **claim:** Baxter's "pretend." **it is likely:** Baxter's "it is like."
- **confer with:** Baxter's "treat with," meaning to enter into discussion or negotiation. **proposals:** his "motions." **prompting:** his "the motion" ("refuse the motion" means refuse the inward nudge).
- **diversion** (paragraph 1): kept as Baxter's word for what keeps people from serious thought; the note [24] shows he includes even good studies.
- **encumbering:** left as Baxter's word, meaning burdensome. **effective:** Baxter's "effectual."
- **"let that stand by that may best be spared"** (paragraph 3): put aside what can best be done without. Left in Baxter's wording.
- **"the Petrarch note"** (note 24): the passage seems to be from Petrarch's autobiographical letter to posterity. Baxter's point is that even honest study can be a way of avoiding the serious questions.
