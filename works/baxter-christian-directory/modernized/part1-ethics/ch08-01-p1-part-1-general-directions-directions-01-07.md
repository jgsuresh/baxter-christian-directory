# Chapter 8: Introduction and Part 1, Directions 1-7 (general directions for the government of the senses)

**Chapter 8. Directions for the Government of the Senses.**

**Part 1.**

**General directions for the government of the senses (by a life of faith).**

The most wise and gracious God, having been pleased to constitute us of soul and body, that our nobler part, in its preparation and passage to a nobler state, might have a companion and instrument suited to the lower place and employment through which it is to pass, has appointed our senses not only for the exercise and helps of life, and the management of our lower actions, and the communication of his lower mercies, but also to be the common passage to the imagination, and so to the mind, and to be serviceable to our rational powers, and help in our service of our Maker, and communion with him in his higher gifts. To these ends all our senses should be used; as being capable of being sanctified and serviceable to God. But sin made its entrance by them, and by sin they are now corrupted and impaired with the body, and have grown inordinate, violent, and unruly in their appetite; and the rational powers, having lost and forsaken God, their proper end and chief object, have hired themselves out or made themselves captive to the sensory appetite, to serve its ends. And so the sensory appetite has become the ruling faculty in the unsanctified, and the senses the common entrance of sin, and instruments of Satan: and though the work of grace is primarily in the rational powers, yet secondarily the lower powers themselves also are sanctified, and brought under the government of a renewed mind and will, and so restored to their proper use. And though I cannot say that grace directly makes any alteration in the senses, yet indirectly it does, by altering the mind, and so the will, and then the imagination, and so the sensory appetite, and so in exercise the sense itself. We see that temperance and chastity do not only restrain, but bring down the appetite from the rage and violence which it had before: not the natural appetite, but the sensory, so far as it is sinful.

The sanctifying and government of the senses and their appetite lies in two parts: first, in guarding them against the entrance of sin: and secondly, in using them to be the entrance of good into the soul. But this latter is so high a work that too few are skilled in it; and few can well perform the other.

**Direction 1. The principal part of the work is about the superior faculties, to get a well-informed judgment, and a holy and confirmed will; and not about the sense itself.**

Reason is dethroned by sin; and the will is left unguided and unguarded to the ravages of physical violence. Reason must be restored before sense will be well governed; for what else must be their immediate governor? It is no sin in brutes to live by sense, because they do not have reason to rule it: and in man it is ruled more or less, as reason is more or less restored. When reason is only cleared about things temporal (as in men of worldly wisdom), there sense will be mastered and ruled as to such temporal ends, as far as they require it. But where reason is sanctified, there sense is ruled to the ends of sanctification, according to the measure of grace.

**Direction 2. It is only the high, eternal things of God and our salvation, objectively settled in the mind and will, and become as it were natural to them, and made our ruling end and interest, that can suffice for a true and holy government of the senses.**

Lower things may muzzle them, and make men seem temperate and sober as far as their honor, and wealth, and health, and life require it: but this is only stopping a gap while most of the hedge lies open, and engaging the sense to serve the flesh, the world, and the devil, in a handsome, calm, and less dishonored way, and not so filthily and furiously as others.

**Direction 3. The main part of this government in the exercise is in taking special care that no sensory good be made the ultimate end of our desire, nor sought for itself, nor rested in, nor delighted in too much; but to see that the soul (having first habitually fixed on its proper higher end and happiness) directs all the actions of every sense (so far as it falls under deliberation and choice) to serve it remotely to those holy ends.**

For the sense is not sanctified if it is not used to a holy end; and its object is not sanctified to us if it is not made serviceable to more holy objects. A mere negative restraint of sense for common ends is only such as those ends are for which it is done. When the eyes, and ears, and taste, and feeling are all taught by reason to serve God to his glory and our salvation, then, and never till then, they are well governed.

**Direction 4. To this end the constant use of a lively belief of the word of God and the things unseen of the other world must be the first and principal means by which our reason must govern every sense, both as to their restraint and their right employment.**

And therefore living by sight and living by faith are opposed in Scripture, 2 Corinthians 5:7. For "we live by faith, not by sight"; that is, sight and sense are not our principal guiding faculty, but subservient to faith; nor are the objects of sight the things which we principally or ultimately seek or value, but the objects of faith; as it is explained before, 2 Corinthians 4:18, "we are not looking at what can be seen but at what cannot be seen. For what can be seen is temporary, but what cannot be seen is eternal." Therefore "faith" is described as "being sure of what we hope for," and "being convinced of what we do not see" (Hebrews 11:1). Believing is to a Christian instead of seeing; because he knows by God's testimony that the things believed are true, though they are unseen. And you know that the objects of sense are all only trifles compared to the great astonishing objects of faith. Therefore if faith is lively, it must necessarily prevail and overrule the senses, because its objects utterly cloud and make nothing of the transitory objects of sense. Therefore the apostle John says, 1 John 5:4, "everyone who has been fathered by God conquers the world. This is the conquering power that has conquered the world: our faith." And "Moses, by seeing him who is invisible," overcame the desires of Egypt's treasures, and the "fear of the wrath of the king, having respect to the recompense of reward" (Hebrews 11:26-27). Stephen easily bore his cruel death when "he saw heaven opened, and Christ standing at the right hand of God" (Acts 7:56). I dare appeal to the man who is most given to physical pleasure, and says, I am not able to deny my appetite, or rule my senses, whether he would not be able if he only saw at the same time what is done in the other world? If he saw heaven, and hell, the glorified and the damned, and saw the majesty of that God who commands him to refrain, would he not then be able to let alone the cup, the dish, the harlot, the amusement, which is now so powerful with him? I would not thank the most beastly pleasure-seeker among you for living as temperately (as to the act) as the strictest saint alive, if he only saw the worlds which departed souls now see. It is not possible but that it would overpower his physical desires; yes, and call off those senses to serve him in some inquiry what he should do to be saved. Therefore if believing the unseen world is instead of seeing it with our eyes, it is most certain that the means to overcome indulgence in physical pleasure is faith, and lively belief must rule our senses.

**Direction 5. The more this belief of God and glory kindles love to them, the more effective it will be in the government of the senses.**

Our common proverb says, Where the love is, there is the eye. How readily it follows the heart! Love will not alter the sense itself, but it commands the use of all the senses. It will not clear a dim, decayed sight; but it will command it what to look upon. As the stronger love of one dish, or one amusement, or one company, will carry you from another which you love more faintly; so the love of God, and heaven, and holiness, will carry you from the captivity of all physical things.

**Direction 6. It must be well considered how powerful and dangerous things perceived by the senses are, and how high and hard a work it is in this our depraved, earthly state to live by faith upon things unseen, and to rule the sense and be carried above it: so that the soul may be awakened to a sufficient fear and watchfulness, and may fly to Christ for assistance to its faith.**

It is no small thing for a man in flesh to live above flesh. The way of the soul's reception and operation is so much by the senses here, that it is apt to grow too familiar with things perceived by the senses, and to be a stranger to things which it never saw. It is a great work to make a man in flesh deny the pleasures which he sees, and tastes, and feels, for such pleasures as he only hears of; and hears of as never to be enjoyed till after death, in a world which sense has no acquaintance with. Oh what a glory it is to faith that it can perform such a work as this! How hard is it for a weak believer! And the strongest find it work enough. Consider this, that it may awaken you to set about this work with that care that the greatness of it requires, and you may live by faith above a life of sight and sense; for it is this that your happiness or misery lies on.

**Direction 7. Sense must not only be kept out of the throne, but from any participation in the government; and we must be careful not to receive it into our counsels, or treat with it, or hear it plead its cause; and we must see that it gets nothing by striving, insistence, or violence, but that it is governed despotically and absolutely, as the horse is governed by the rider.**

For if the government is once halved between sense and reason, your lives will be half bestial: and when reason does not rule, faith and grace do not rule; for faith is to reason as sight to the eye. There are no such beasts in human shape who lay aside all the use of reason, and are governed by sense alone (unless it is idiots or madmen). But sense should have no part of the government at all. And where it is chief in power, the devil is there the unseen governor. You cannot here excuse yourselves by any plea of necessity or constraint: for though the sense is violent as well as enticing, yet God has made the reason and will the absolute governors under him; and by all its rebellion and violence, sense cannot depose them, nor force them to one sin, but does all the mischief by procuring their consent. This is done sometimes by affecting the imagination and passions too deeply with the pleasure and alluring sweetness of their objects, so that the higher faculties may be drawn into consent; and sometimes by wearing out the resisting mind and will, and causing them to slacken their opposition, and relax the reins, and by a sinful absence of restraint to permit the sense to take its course. A headstrong horse is not so easily ruled as one with a tender mouth that has been well ridden; and therefore, though it is in the power of the rider to rule him, yet sometimes for his own ease he will loosen the reins: and a horse that is used this way by a lazy or unskillful rider, to have his will whenever he strives, will strive whenever he is crossed in his will, and so will be the master. As ill-bred children that are used to having everything given them which they cry for will be sure to cry before they will be crossed in their desire. So it is with our sensory appetite: if you are in the habit of satisfying it when it is eager or insistent, you shall be mastered by its eagerness and insistence; and if you are in the habit of merely regarding it too much, and delaying your commands till sense is heard and taken into counsel, it is two to one that it will prevail, or at least will be very troublesome to you, and prove a traitor in your bosom, and its temptations keep you in continual danger. Therefore be sure that you never loosen the reins; but keep sense under a constant government, if you love either your safety or your ease.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). Verse texts were fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Headings:** the source heads the chapter "CHAPTER VIII. DIRECTIONS FOR THE GOVERNMENT OF THE SENSES," and this first Part "PART I. *General Directions for the Government of the Senses (by a Life of Faith).*" Baxter's numbered "*Direct.*" items are rendered as **Direction 1**, etc.; the first sentence of each is set in bold as its heading, and the rest of his paragraph follows unchanged.
- **This file ends** with Direction 7; Part 1's Directions continue in the next file.
- **2 Corinthians 5:7:** NET "we live by faith, not by sight." The King James reads "we walk by faith, not by sight." The NET's "live" fits Baxter's own phrasing ("living by sight and living by faith") even more directly.
- **2 Corinthians 4:18:** NET wording. Baxter quotes the King James, which begins "While we look not at the things which are seen"; the NET begins the verse with "because," which we leave out, starting the quotation at "we are not looking." The sense he uses (the seen is temporary, the unseen eternal) is unchanged. Baxter's "as it is before expounded" refers to this verse coming just before 5:7 in the letter; rendered "as it is explained before."
- **Hebrews 11:1 (NET flag):** Baxter quotes the King James, faith as "the substance of things hoped for" and "the evidence of things not seen." The NET reads "being sure of what we hope for" and "being convinced of what we do not see." Baxter leans on the King James words: "substance" and "evidence" suggest that faith gives the unseen things a kind of reality and proof, which supports his next sentence, "Believing is to a Christian instead of seeing." The NET puts the stress on the believer's assurance and conviction. His point still holds, but less strongly than with the King James words. Because the NET wording is not a noun phrase, "is described to be" is rendered "is described as."
- **1 John 5:4 (NET flag):** NET wording. The King James reads "Whatsoever is born of God overcometh the world: and this is the victory that overcometh the world, even our faith." The NET has "everyone who has been fathered by God" (a person, where the King James has the neuter "whatsoever") and "the conquering power" where the King James has "the victory." Baxter's point (faith overcomes the world) is the same in both.
- **Hebrews 11:26-27:** Baxter does not quote these verses straight; he blends King James phrases ("seeing him who is invisible," "the wrath of the king," "he had respect unto the recompence of the reward") into his own sentence, so his wording is kept, modernized, in his quotation marks. "Having respect to the recompense of reward" means looking ahead to the reward. NET: "He regarded abuse suffered for Christ to be greater wealth than the treasures of Egypt, for his eyes were fixed on the reward. By faith he left Egypt without fearing the king's anger, for he persevered as though he could see the one who is invisible." Note that the NET has "as though he could see," where Baxter's "by seeing him that is invisible" makes the seeing more direct; his argument (that seeing the unseen by faith overpowers the senses) leans on that.
- **Acts 7:56:** Baxter's paraphrase, in the third person and with "Christ" for the verse's "the Son of Man"; kept in his wording. NET: "'Look!' he said. 'I see the heavens opened, and the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God!'"
- **Unmarked allusion:** "what he should do to be saved" (Direction 4) echoes the jailer's question in Acts 16:30 (reference not in Baxter's text).
- **imagination:** Baxter's "fantasy" (opening paragraph and Direction 7), his word for the faculty that forms images from what the senses take in, standing between the senses and the mind. It does not mean daydreaming or make-believe. In the opening paragraph he also uses "imagination" for the same faculty.
- **sensory appetite:** Baxter's "sensitive appetite," the appetite of the senses (as distinct from the rational will). **physical, pleasure-seeker, indulgence in physical pleasure:** for Baxter's "sensual," "sensualist," and "sensuality," per the project's standing choices; "most sensual" in Direction 4 is rendered "most given to physical pleasure."
- **ravages of physical violence** (Direction 1): Baxter's "rapes of sensual violence." "Rape" here has its older sense of a seizing or carrying off by force; we chose "ravages" so that a modern reader does not take it as a sexual term.
- **lower:** Baxter's "inferior" (actions, mercies). **impaired:** his "vitiated." **hired themselves out or made themselves captive:** his "hired or captivated themselves." **directly / indirectly:** his "immediately / mediately." **bring down:** his "take down." **inordinate:** kept, meaning beyond due measure, disordered.
- **natural** (Direction 2): Baxter's "connatural," meaning become part of their nature, second nature.
- **objectively settled** (Direction 2): kept; Baxter means settled in the mind as its object, not "objective" in the modern sense of impartial.
- **value:** Baxter's "set by" (Direction 4). **must necessarily:** his "must needs." **refrain:** his "forbear." **amusement:** his "sport."
- **"I would not thank ... for living as temperately"** (Direction 4): an old idiom meaning it would be no great credit to him; even the worst pleasure-seeker would live like a saint if he saw the other world.
- **things perceived by the senses:** Baxter's "things sensible" (Direction 6).
- **insistence, insistent:** Baxter's "importunity," "importunate" (Direction 7): pressing, nagging demand. **slacken:** his "remit." **absence of restraint:** his "privation of restraint." **lazy:** his "slothful." **in the habit of:** his "use to" (do habitually).
- **its faith** (Direction 6): the source reads "assistance to his faith"; "his" here refers to the soul (Baxter's older use of "his" for "its"), so it is rendered "its."
- **Direction 7, "As ill-bred children...":** the source sentence ("As ill-bred children ... will be sure to cry before they will be crossed of their desire.") is a comparison left without its main clause; the next sentence ("So it is with our sensory appetite") completes it. We kept Baxter's sentence boundary as printed.
- **compared to** (Direction 4): Baxter's "all but trifles, to the great astonishing objects of faith," where "to" means "in comparison with."
- **despotically and absolutely** (Direction 7): kept; Baxter means sense must be ruled with unshared authority, as a rider rules a horse.
- **idiots or madmen** (Direction 7): Baxter's terms for people without the use of reason, kept as written.
