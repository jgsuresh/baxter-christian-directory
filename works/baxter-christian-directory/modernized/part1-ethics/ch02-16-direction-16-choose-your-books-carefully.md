# Chapter 2: Direction 16

**Direction 16. Make careful choice of the books which you read. Let the holy Scriptures always have the pre-eminence; and next to them, the solid, lively, heavenly treatises, which best expound and apply the Scriptures; and next to those, the credible histories, especially of the church, and short treatises upon lesser sciences and arts: but be careful of the poison of the writings of false teachers, which would corrupt your understandings; and of worthless romances, play-books, and false stories, which may bewitch your imaginations, and corrupt your hearts.**

As there is a more excellent appearance of the Spirit of God in the holy Scriptures than in any other book whatever, so it has more power and fitness to convey the Spirit, and make us spiritual, by imprinting itself upon our hearts. As there is more of God in it, so it will acquaint us more with God, and bring us nearer him, and make the reader more reverent, serious, and divine. Let Scripture be first and most in your hearts and hands, and other books be used as subservient to it. The efforts of the devil and papists to keep it from you show that it is most necessary and desirable to you. And when they tell you that all heretics plead the Scriptures, they only tell you that it is the common rule or law of Christians, which therefore all are obliged to claim; as all lawyers and quarrelers plead the law of the land, be their cause never so bad, and yet the laws must not be therefore concealed or cast aside: and they only tell you that in their concealing or dishonoring the Scriptures, they are worse than any of those heretics. When they tell you that the Scriptures are misunderstood, and abused, and perverted to maintain people's errors, they might also desire that the sun might be obscured, because the nearly blind mistake, and murderers and robbers do wickedly by its light; and that the earth might be overturned, because it bears all evil-doers; and highways stopped up, because people travel on them to do evil; and food prohibited, because it nourishes people's diseases. And when they have told you truly of a law or rule (whether made by pope or council) which bad people cannot misunderstand, or break, or abuse and misapply, then listen to them, and prefer that law, as that which spares the need of any judgment.

The writings of divines are nothing else but a preaching of the gospel to the eye, as the voice preaches it to the ear. Vocal preaching has the pre-eminence in moving the affections, and in being diversified according to the state of the congregations which attend it: this way the milk comes warmest from the breast. But books have the advantage in many other respects: you may read an able preacher, when you have only a poor one to hear. Every congregation cannot hear the most judicious or powerful preachers; but every single person may read the books of the most powerful and judicious. Preachers may be silenced or banished, when books may be at hand: books may be kept at a smaller cost than preachers: we may choose books which treat of that very subject which we desire to hear about; but we cannot choose what subject the preacher shall treat. Books we may have at hand every day and hour; when we can have sermons but seldom, and at set times. If sermons are forgotten, they are gone. But a book we may read over and over till we remember it; and if we forget it, may again peruse it at our pleasure, or at our leisure. So that good books are a very great mercy to the world. The Holy Spirit chose the way of writing, to preserve his doctrine and laws to the church, as knowing how easy and sure a way it is of keeping it safe to all generations, in comparison with mere verbal tradition, which might have made as many controversies about the very terms, as there are memories or persons to be the preservers and reporters.

Books are (if well chosen) household, present, constant, judicious, pertinent, yes, and powerful sermons; and always of very great use to your salvation; but especially when vocal preaching fails, and preachers are ignorant, ungodly, or dull, or when they are persecuted, and forbidden to preach.

You need a judicious teacher at hand, to direct you what books to use or to refuse. For among good books there are some very good that are sound and lively: and some are good, but poor, and weak, and somewhat dull: and some are very good in part, but have mixtures of error, or else of incautious, injudicious expressions, fitter to puzzle than to edify the weak. I am reluctant to name any of these latter sorts (of which abundance have come out of late); but to the young beginner in religion, I may be bold to recommend (next to a sound catechism) Mr. Rutherford's Letters, Mr. Robert Bolton's Works, Mr. Perkins's, Mr. Whateley's, Mr. Ball, of Faith, Dr. Preston's, Dr. Sibbs's, Mr. Hildersham's, Mr. Pink's Sermons, Mr. Joseph Rogers's, Mr. Rich. Rogers, Mr. Richard Allen's, Mr. Gurnall's, Mr. Swinnock's, Mr. Joseph Simonds's. And to establish you against popery, Dr. Challoner's Credo Eccles. Cathol., Dr. Field, of the Church, Dr. White's Way to the Church, with the Defence, Bishop Usher's Answer to the Jesuit, and Chillingworth, with Drelincourt's Summary. And for right principles about redemption, &c. Mr. Truman's Great Propitiation, and of Natural and Moral Impotency; and Mr. William Fenner, of Willful Impenitency, Mr. Hotchkis, of Forgiveness of Sin. To pass by many other excellent ones, that I may not name too many.

To a very judicious, able reader, who is fit to judge all he reads, there is no great danger in reading the books of any seducers: it only shows him how little and thin a cloak is used, to cover a bad cause. But, alas! young soldiers, not used to such wars, are startled at a mere sophism, or at a terrible threatening of damnation to dissenters, (which every fault-finding sect can use,) or at every confident, triumphant boast, or at everything that has a fair show of truth or godliness. Injudicious persons can answer almost no deceiver whom they hear: and when they cannot answer them, they think they must yield, as if the fault were not in them, but in the case; and as if Christ had no wiser followers, or better defenders of his truth, than they. Do not meddle therefore with poison, till you better know how to use it, and may do it with less danger, as long as you have no need.

As for play-books, and romances, and idle tales, I have already shown in my *Book of Self-Denial* how pernicious they are, especially to youth, and to frothy, empty, idle wits, that do not know what a man is, nor what he has to do in the world. They are powerful baits of the devil, to keep more necessary things out of their minds, and better books out of their hands, and to poison the mind so much the more dangerously, as they are read with more delight and pleasure: and to fill the minds of pleasure-loving people with such idle fumes, and intoxicating fancies, as may divert them from the serious thoughts of their salvation: and (which is no small loss) to rob them of abundance of that precious time, which was given them for more important business; and which they will wish and wish again at last, that they had spent more wisely. I know the fanciful will say that these things are innocent, and may teach people much good (like him that must go to a whorehouse to learn to hate uncleanness, and him that would go out with robbers to learn to hate thievery): but I shall now only ask them as in the presence of God, 1. Whether they could spend that time no better? 2. Whether better books and practices would not edify them more? 3. Whether the greatest lovers of romances and plays, are the greatest lovers of the book of God, and of a holy life? 4. Whether they feel in themselves that the love of these empty things does increase their love to the word of God, and kill their sin, and prepare them for the life to come? or the clean contrary? And I would desire people not to babble against their own experience and reason, nor to dispute themselves into damnable impenitency, nor to make fools of their souls by a few silly words, which anyone but a pleasure-seeker may perceive to be mere deceit and falsehood. If this will not serve, they shall be shortly proved wrong and answered in another manner.

## Translator's notes

- **No Scripture is quoted** in this section, so there is no NET text to fetch and no NET flag to raise. Baxter speaks of "the Scriptures" and of the Holy Spirit's choice of writing, but cites no verse. Scripture translation elsewhere in the project: NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **Footnotes and margin notes:** no footnote marker and no margin summary falls in lines 7842-7971.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): "vain" romances, "vanities" to *worthless*, *empty things*; "fantasies" to *imaginations*; "tractates" to *short treatises*; "inferior" sciences and arts to *lesser*; "fain to pretend" to *obliged to claim*; "wranglers" to *quarrelers*; "purblind" to *nearly blind*; "subverted" to *overturned*; "hearken" to *listen*; "mean" (a poor preacher; books that are "good, but mean") to *poor*; "charge" to *cost*; "domestic" (books as sermons) to *household*; "incautelous" to *incautious*; "loth" to *reluctant*; "censure" to *judge*; "censorious" to *fault-finding*; "pretence" (of truth) to *show*; "at a very sophism" to *at a mere sophism* ("very" in the sense of "mere"); "frothy" and "wits" kept in sense (frivolous, shallow people); "fantastics" to *the fanciful*; "sensual people" to *pleasure-loving people*; "sensualist" to *pleasure-seeker*; "prate" to *babble*; "befool" to *make fools of*; "Wilful" (in a book title) to *Willful*; "Holy Ghost" to *Holy Spirit*; "whore-house" to *whorehouse*, with Baxter's blunt word kept.
- **prevents the need of any judgment** ("preventeth"): Baxter's *prevent* is "go before," here "do away with beforehand." We render it *spares the need of*; the sense is a rule so clear that no one would need to judge it.
- **convinced** ("convinced and answered," last sentence): in Baxter's day, "proved wrong" or refuted; we render it so.
- **papists:** Baxter's word for Roman Catholics; his polemic against Rome (here, that Rome keeps Scripture from the people) is his own and is kept as written. **popery** likewise.
- **plead the Scriptures / plead the law of the land:** "plead" is the legal sense, *appeal to*; kept.
- **heretics, seducers, dissenters:** left as Baxter's words. "Dissenters" here seems to mean those who differ from a sect's own teaching (our reading); Baxter says that any fault-finding sect can threaten them with damnation.
- **play-books:** Baxter's word, kept; apparently plays in book form (our reading, not stated by Baxter). **divines:** clergy and theologians, kept.
- **affections:** kept as Baxter's term for emotions and desires. **pre-eminence:** kept.
- **young soldiers:** a figure for inexperienced Christians; "such wars" are controversies with deceivers.
- **"Book of Self-Denial":** Baxter's own earlier work, here put in italics as *Book of Self-Denial*. We have not checked which passage of it he means.
- **Authors and titles in the reading list** are kept exactly as Baxter printed them, including his abbreviations ("Credo Eccles. Cathol.," "&c.," "Rich."). We have not identified or checked the individual works, so we add no bibliographic detail. Whether "Mr. Pink's Sermons" and similar names are spelled as in other sources is unconfirmed; we left them as printed.
- **"To pass by many other excellent ones, that I may not name too many."** is a sentence fragment in the source and is kept as written.
- **Unmarked allusions:** none that we could identify with confidence.
- **Source oddities:** the numbered questions "1. ... 4." sit inside one sentence-run in the source and are kept in line, as in Baxter. The list "Mr. Rutherford's Letters, ..." has no verbs and is kept as a list.
- **Grammar:** in "The endeavours of the devil and papists ... doth show," the source has a singular verb with a plural subject; we use "show."
