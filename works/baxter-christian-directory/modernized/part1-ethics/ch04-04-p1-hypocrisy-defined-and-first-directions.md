# Chapter 4: Directions against Hypocrisy, the nature of hypocrisy and Directions 1-6

**Directions against Hypocrisy.**

Hypocrisy is the acting the part of a religious person, as upon a stage, by one who is not religious indeed; [147] a seeming in religion to be what you are not, or to do what you do not; or a dissembling or counterfeiting of that piety which you do not have. To counterfeit a state of godliness is the sin only of the unregenerate, who at present are in a state of misery: to counterfeit some particular act of godliness, or some higher degree, is an odious sin, but one that a regenerate person may be tempted into. This act of hypocrisy does not name the person a hypocrite; but the state of hypocrisy does. Every hypocrite therefore is an ungodly person, seeming godly; or one who indeed is no true Christian, professing himself a Christian. Of hypocrites there are two sorts: some desire to deceive others, but not themselves, but know themselves to be only dissemblers; and these are commonly called gross hypocrites: and some deceive both themselves and others, and think they are no hypocrites, but are as confident of their honesty and sincerity, as if they were no dissemblers at all: but yet they are as truly hypocrites as the former, because they seem to be religious and sincere, when indeed they are not, though they think they are; and profess themselves to be true Christians, when they are nothing less. These are called close hypocrites, because they do not know themselves to be hypocrites; though they might know it if they would. This is the commonest sort of hypocrites.

There are also two degrees of hypocrites: some of them have only a general profession of Christianity and godliness, which is the professed religion of the country where they live; and these are hypocrites because they profess to be what they are not: and others make a greater and extraordinary profession of special strictness in their religion, when they are not sincere; and these are eminently called hypocrites: such as the Pharisees were among the Jews, and many friars, and Jesuits, and nuns among the papists, who by their separating vows, and orders, and habits, profess extraordinarily an extraordinary measure of devotion, while they lack the life of godliness.

In all hypocrisy there are to be considered, 1. The thing claimed; 2. The claim, or means of seeming, or the cloak of their deceit. 1. The thing claimed by common hypocrites is to be true Christians, and servants of God, and heirs of heaven, though not to be so zealous in it as some of a higher degree. The thing claimed by eminent hypocrites is to be zealous, eminent Christians, or at least to be sincere in a special manner, while they discern the common hypocrite not to be sincere. 2. The cloak of seeming or claim by which they would be thought to be what they are not, is anything in general that has an appearance of godliness, and is apt to make others think them godly. And thus there are various sorts of hypocrites, according to the variety of their cloaks or ways of dissimulation; though hypocrisy itself is in all of them the same thing. As among the very Mahometans, and heathens, there often arise some notable hypocrites, who by claimed revelations and austerity of life, profess themselves (as Mahomet did) to be holy persons, who had some extraordinary familiarity with God or angels. So among the papists there are, besides the common ones, as many sorts of hypocrites as they have self-devised orders. And everywhere the cloak of the common hypocrite is so thin and transparent, that it shows his nakedness to the more intelligent sort: and this puts the eminent hypocrite upon some more praiseworthy claim, that is not so transparent. As for instance, the hypocrisy of common papists, whose cloak is made up of penances and ceremonies, of saying over Latin words, or numbering words and beads for prayers, with all the rest of their trumpery before named, (chapter 3, gr. direct. 15, direct. 11) is so thin a cloak that it will not satisfy some among themselves, but they withdraw into distinct societies and orders, (the church and the profession of Christianity being not enough for them,) that they may be religious, as if they saw that the rest are not religious. And then the common sort of ungodly Protestants have so much wit, as to see through the cloak of all the popish hypocrisy; and therefore they take up a fitter one for themselves; and that is, the name of a Protestant reformed religion and church, joined to the common profession of Christianity. The name and profession of a Christian and a Protestant, with going to church, and a heartless lip-service or saying their prayers, is the cloak of all ungodly Protestants. Others, discerning the thinness of this cloak, do think to make themselves a better one; and they take up the strictest opinions in religion, and own those which they account the strictest party, and own that which they esteem the purest and most spiritual worship: the cloak of these men is their opinions, party, and way of worship, while their carnal lives detect their hypocrisy. Some who see through all these claims, do take up the most excellent cloak of all, and that is, an appearance of serious spirituality in religion, with a due observation of all the outward parts and means, and a reformation of life, in works of piety, justice, and charity; I say, an appearance of all these, which if they had indeed, they were sincere, and should be saved; in which the godly Christian goes beyond them all.

By this it is plain, that among us in England all men who are not saints are hypocrites, because all (except here or there a Jew or unbeliever) profess themselves to be Christians; and every true Christian is a saint. They know that none but saints or godly persons shall be saved; and there are few of them who will renounce their hopes of heaven; and therefore they must claim to be all godly. And is it not most cursed, horrid hypocrisy, for a man to claim religion as the only way to his salvation, and confidently call himself a Christian, while he hates and mocks the power and practice of that very religion which he does profess? Of this see my treatise *The Vain Religion of the Formal Hypocrite*.

The hypocrite's ends in his claims and dissemblings are not all the same: one intends the pleasing of parents, or some friends on whom he depends, who will otherwise be displeased with him, and think ill of him. Another intends the pleasing of the higher powers, when it happens that they are friends to godliness. Another intends the preserving of his esteem with religious persons, that they may not judge him wicked and profane. Another intends the hiding of some particular villainy, or the success of some ambitious enterprise. But the most common end is to quiet and comfort their guilty souls, with an image of that holiness which they are without, and to steal some peace to their consciences by a lie: and so because they will not be religious indeed, they will take up some show or image of religion, to make themselves as well as others believe that they are religious. [148]

**Direction 1. To escape hypocrisy, understand well wherein the life and power of godliness consists, and wherein it differs from the lifeless image or corpse of godliness.**

The life of godliness is expressed in the seventeen grand directions in chapter 3. It principally consists in such a faith in Christ, as causes us to love God above all, and obey him before all, and prefer his favor and the hopes of heaven before all the pleasures, or profits, or honors of the world; and to worship him in spirit and truth, according to the direction of his word. The images of religion I showed you before, page 176. Take heed of such a lifeless image.

**Direction 2. See that your chief study is about the heart, that there God's image may be planted, and his interest advanced, and the interest of the world and flesh subdued, and the love of every sin cast out, and the love of holiness succeed; and that you do not content yourselves with seeming to do good in outward acts, when you are bad yourselves, and strangers to the great internal duties.**

The first and great work of a Christian is about his heart. There it is that God dwells by his Spirit, in his saints; and there it is that sin and Satan reign, in the ungodly. The great duties and the great sins are those of the heart. There is the root of good and evil: the tongue and life are only the fruits and expressions of that which dwells within. [149] The inward habit of sin is a second nature: and a sinful nature is worse than a sinful act. "Guard your heart with all vigilance, for from it are the sources of life" (Proverbs 4:23). Make the tree good, and the fruit will be good: but the "Offspring of vipers! How are you able to say anything good, since you are evil? For the mouth speaks from what fills the heart" (Matthew 12:33–34). Until the Spirit has regenerated the soul, all outward religion will be only a dead and pitiful thing: though there is something which God has appointed an unregenerate man to do, in order to his own conversion, yet no such antecedent act will prove that the person is justified or reconciled to God, until he is converted. To make up a religion of doing or saying something that is good, while the heart is void of the Spirit of Christ, and sanctifying grace, is the hypocrite's religion (Romans 8:9).

**Direction 3. Be conscientious about the sins of the thoughts, and the desire and other affections or passions of the mind, as well as about the sins of tongue or hand.**

A lustful thought, a malicious thought, a proud, ambitious, or greedy thought, especially if it proceeds to a wish, or scheming, or consent, is a sin the more dangerous by how much the more inward and near the heart; as Christ has showed you (Matthew 5 and 6). The hypocrite who most regards the eye of man, lives as if his thoughts were free.

**Direction 4. Be conscientious about secret sins, which are committed out of the sight of men, and may be concealed from them, as well as about open and notorious sins.**

If he can do it in the dark and secure his reputation, the hypocrite is bold: but a sincere believer bears a reverence to his conscience, and much more to the all-seeing God.

**Direction 5. Be faithful in secret duties, which have no witness but God and conscience: as meditation, and self-examination, and secret prayer; and do not be religious only in the sight of men.**

**Direction 6. In all public worship be more diligent with the heart, than with the tongue or knee: and see that your tongue does not overrun your heart, and leave it behind.**

Do not neglect the due composure of your words, and due behavior of your bodies: but take much more pains for the exercise of holy desires from a believing, loving, fervent soul.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). Each quoted verse below was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Section heading:** "Directions against Hypocrisy" is Baxter's heading for this section of Chapter 4. The range begins at it and ends partway through the section (after Direction 6); the next file continues.
- **Direction labels:** Baxter's "Direct. I.-VI." are rendered "Direction 1-6." The first sentence of each Direction is in bold with its label.
- **Proverbs 4:23:** NET wording. Baxter's "from thence are the issues of life" (King James) becomes "from it are the sources of life." The sense Baxter draws (the heart as the root of conduct) is kept.
- **Matthew 12:33-34 (flag):** Baxter's "Make the tree good, and the fruit will be good" is outside his quotation marks, and is kept in his wording (modernized). His quoted words, "viperous generation that are evil, cannot speak good; for out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh," are replaced by the NET: "Offspring of vipers! ... say anything good, since you are evil? For the mouth speaks from what fills the heart." The NET has no "abundance," and puts the point as a question ("How are you able ...?") rather than "cannot speak good." Baxter's sense (a bad heart cannot yield good speech) survives in the NET, but the King James word "abundance" and its flat "cannot" do not.
- **Romans 8:9:** cited only. **Matthew 5 and 6:** Baxter's "Matt. v. and vi.", cited only.
- **pretended, pretence (claim):** Baxter's "the thing pretended," "the pretence," "pretended revelations," "more laudable pretence," "pretend to religion" are rendered "claimed," "the claim," "claimed revelations," "more praiseworthy claim," "claim religion," following the project's decision on "pretend." In "the claim, or means of seeming, or the cloak of their deceit" the older sense of "pretence" (outward show) may be closer than "claim"; left as the convention directs.
- **denominate:** Baxter's "does not denominate the person a hypocrite" is rendered "does not name the person a hypocrite" (it does not give the person that name).
- **there is considerable:** Baxter's "In all hypocrisy there is considerable" is rendered "there are to be considered" (that is, to be taken into account).
- **Make conscience of:** Baxter's "Make conscience of" (Directions 3 and 4) is rendered "Be conscientious about," meaning to treat as a matter of conscience.
- **contrivance:** "scheming" (Direction 3). **laborious:** "diligent" (Direction 6). **villany:** "villainy." **divers:** "various." **wit:** kept (here, intelligence).
- **close hypocrites:** Baxter's term for hypocrites who hide from themselves that they are such; he explains it in the same sentence. **gross hypocrites:** his term for those who know they are dissemblers. Both kept.
- **dissimulation, dissembling:** kept; these mean concealing what one really is.
- **habits:** in "vows, and orders, and habits," Baxter means the distinctive clothing of religious orders. Kept as written. **habit** in "the inward habit of sin" is the technical sense of a settled disposition; kept.
- **trumpery:** worthless showy things; kept. **papists, Jesuits, friars, nuns:** Baxter's polemic against Rome, kept as he wrote it. **Mahometans, Mahomet:** Baxter's words for Muslims and for Muhammad; kept, and what he says of them is his claim, not ours.
- **infidel:** "unbeliever." **covetous:** "greedy." **carnal:** kept. **affections or passions of the mind:** kept as Baxter's terms for emotions and desires. **christian/protestant:** capitalized.
- **"chap. iii. gr. direct. xv. direct. xi.":** rendered "chapter 3, gr. direct. 15, direct. 11." We have not confirmed what "gr." stands for (perhaps "great"), nor checked the cross-reference. **"the seventeen grand directions in chap. iii.":** cross-reference not checked. **"page 176":** a page number of Baxter's original edition, not of this project; not checked.
- **my Treatise ... *The Vain Religion of the Formal Hypocrite*:** Baxter's own title (his quotation marks replaced by italics). We have not checked the work.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "the Pharisees ... among the Jews" (compare Matthew 23); "worship him in spirit and truth" (compare John 4:24); "the seventeen grand directions" and "the life of godliness" are Baxter's own.
- **Latin and Greek (notes 147-149):** the Greek words φιλειν and καταφιλειν are printed as in the source (without accents). The Latin has some oddities kept as printed: "observateque" (perhaps "observate-que"), "ficionem" (for "fictionem"), "tam vel," "janitores conscientia nostra--supposuit." Baxter's own English translation of the Twelve Tables line is carried as he gave it. We make no claim here about whether that line is genuinely from the Twelve Tables.
- **Note 148:** "Petrarch, in vita sua": we have not identified which work Baxter means.
- **Source oddity:** the source prints "gr. direct." and "direct. xi." with no further explanation.
- **Take heed (Direction 1):** kept as Baxter's idiom. **Baxter's notes:** "Laert." and "Scalig." are expanded above only as unconfirmed guesses; the Latin is as printed ("observateque," "ficionem," "tam vel" are as in the source and may be misprints).
- **Direction 2 (Matthew 12:34):** "viperous generation" was Baxter's own wording; the NET replaces it as flagged above.
