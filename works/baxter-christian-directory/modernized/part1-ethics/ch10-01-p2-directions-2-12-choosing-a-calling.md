# Chapter 10: Directions 2–12 on choosing a calling

**Direction 2. As labor is thus necessary, so understand how needful a stated calling is, for the right performance of your labors.**

A calling is a stated ordinary course of labor. This is very needful for these reasons: 1. Outside a calling a man's labors are only occasional, or inconstant, and so more time is spent in idleness than in labor. 2. A man is best skilled in that which he is used to. 3. And he will be best provided for it with tools and necessaries. 4. Therefore he does it better than he could do another work, and so does not wrong others, but attains more the ends of his labor. 5. And he does it more easily; when a man unused, and unskilled, and unequipped, toils himself much in doing little. 6. And he will do his work more orderly, when another is in continual confusion, and his business does not know its time and place, but one part contradicts another. Therefore some certain calling or trade of life is best for every man.

*Question 1.* May not a man have a calling consisting of occasional, uncertain works? *Answer.* He that can have no better may do thus; provided they are consistent works which he is fit for: as a footman may go on various errands, and a day laborer may do many sorts of works; but great variety will be a great inconvenience to him.

*Question 2.* May a man have several trades or callings at once? *Answer.* Yes, no doubt, if it is for the common good, or for his own, and no injury to any other; nor so inconsistent that one will make him unfaithful in the other; then God does not forbid it.

The question whether a man may change his calling I answered before, Chapter 3, Direction 10.

**Direction 3. Do not think that a calling can be lawful when the work of it is sin; nor that you, or your labor, or your gain, in an unlawful calling, shall be blessed.**

An unlawful act is bad enough; but an unlawful calling is a life of sin. To make sin a man's trade, and work, and living, is a most horrid, desperate course of life. As mercenary soldiers, that for their pay will fight against authority, right, or innocence, and murder men for half a crown a day: and those that live by cheating, stealing, oppressing, whoring, or by harboring such; or upon the sin of such: or of drunkards, gamblers, or other vices of physical indulgence, which they knowingly and willingly maintain.

**Direction 4. Do not think that because a work is lawful, that therefore it is lawful to make a calling of it.**

It is lawful to jest in time and measure, but not lawful to be a jester as a trade of life. If in some cases it should prove lawful to act a comedy or tragedy, it will not follow that therefore it is lawful to be by trade a stage-player: if a game of cards or dice may be in some cases lawful, it does not follow that it is lawful to be a gambler by trade. The like I may say of many others.

**Direction 5. It is not enough that the work of your calling is lawful, nor that it is necessary, but you must take special care also that it is safe, and not very dangerous to your souls.**

The calling of a wine-seller and ale-seller is lawful and needful; and yet it is so very dangerous that (unless it is in an extraordinary place or case) a man that loves his soul should be loath to meddle with it, if he can have a safer one to get his bread by. They get so little by sober people, and their gain depends so much upon men's sin, that it is a constant temptation to them to be the maintainers of it. And frail man, that can so hardly stand on firm ground, should be loath for a little money to walk continually upon the ice, and to venture his soul in a life of such temptations; for it is twenty to one that they will prevail.

**Direction 6. The first and principal thing to be aimed at in the choice of a trade or calling for yourselves or children is the service of God, and the public good; and therefore *(cæteris paribus)* that calling which most contributes to the public good is to be preferred.**

The callings most useful to the public good are the magistrates, the pastors, and teachers of the church, schoolmasters, physicians, lawyers, etc., farmers (plowmen, graziers, and shepherds); and next to them are mariners, clothiers, booksellers, tailors, and such others that are employed about things most necessary to mankind; and some callings are employed about matters of so little use (as tobacco-sellers, lace-sellers, feather-makers, wig-makers, and many more such) that he that may choose better should be loath to settle for one of these, though possibly in itself it may be lawful. It is a great satisfaction to an honest mind to spend his life in doing the greatest good he can; and a prison and constant calamity to be tied to spend one's life in doing little good at all to others, though he should grow rich by it himself.

**Direction 7. When two callings equally contribute to the public good, and one of them has the advantage of riches, and the other is more advantageous to your souls, the latter must be preferred; and next to the public good, the soul's advantage must guide your choice:**

as, suppose that a lawyer were as profitable to the public good as a minister, and that it is the way to far more wealth and honor; yet the sacred calling is much more desirable for the benefit of your souls; because it is an exceedingly great help to be engaged by our callings to have the word and doctrine of Christ continually before us, and in our minds and mouths; when others must be glad to be now and then exercised in it, when their hearts are cooled by the frequent and long diversions of their worldly business; so that our calling and work is to an honest heart a continual recreation, and preserving, and edifying help to grace. So a schoolmaster's calling is usually only poor and very laborious, requiring much close attendance; but yet it is of so great use to the common good, and allows the mind so much leisure and advantage to improve itself in honest studies, that it is fitter to be chosen and delighted in by a well-tempered mind than richer and more honored employments. It is sweet to be all day doing so much good.

**Direction 8. If it is possible, choose a calling which so exercises the body as not to overwhelm you with cares and labor, and deprive you of all leisure for the holy and noble employments of the mind; and which so exercises your mind as to allow you some exercise for the body also.**

1. That calling which so takes up body and mind as neither to allow you intermingled thoughts of greater things, nor convenient breaks for them, is a constant snare and prison to the soul; which is the case of many who plunge themselves into more and greater business than they can otherwise dispatch; and yet are contented to be thus continually alienated in their minds from God and heaven, to get more of the world. Many poor laborers (as clothiers, tailors, and other such) can work with their hands, and meditate or talk of heavenly things without any hindrance of their work; when many men of richer callings have scarcely room for a thought or word of God or heaven all day. 2. On the contrary, if the body does not also have its labor as well as the mind, it will ruin your health, and body and mind will both grow useless.

**Direction 9. It is lawful and fitting to look at the profit of your calling in the third place (that is, after the public good, and after your personal good of soul and bodily health).**

Though it is said, "Do not wear yourself out to become rich" (Proverbs 23:4); the meaning is that you do not make riches your chief end: riches for our fleshly ends must not ultimately be intended or sought. But in subordination to higher things they may; that is, you may labor in that manner that tends most to your success and lawful gain: you are bound to improve all your Master's talents; but then your end must be that you may be the better provided to do God service, and may do the more good with what you have. If God shows you a way in which you may lawfully get more than in another way (without wrong to your soul, or to any other), if you refuse this, and choose the less gainful way, you cross one of the ends of your calling, and you refuse to be God's steward, and to accept his gifts, and use them for him when he requires it; you may labor to be rich for God, though not for the flesh and sin.

**Direction 10. It is not enough that you consider what calling and labor is most desirable, but you must also consider what you or your children are fittest for, both in mind and body.**

For that calling may be one man's blessing which would be another's misery and undoing. A weak body cannot undergo those labors that require strength; and a dull and heavy mind and wit cannot do the works which require great judgment and ingenuity. [571] It has been the calamity of the church, and undoing of many ministers themselves, that well-meaning parents, out of love for the sacred work of God, have set their children to be ministers that were unfit for it; and many self-conceited persons themselves are ready to thrust themselves into that holy office, when they have some inconsiderable smattering of knowledge, and some poor measure of gifts, overvalued by themselves, that do not know what is required for so great a work. Be sure that you first look to the natural ingenuity of your children (or yourselves), and then to their grace and piety; and see that none be devoted to the ministry that do not naturally have a quickness of understanding, and a freedom of expression, unless you would have him live upon the ruin of souls, and wrong of the church and work of God; and turn an enemy to the best of his flock, when he sees that they value him only as he deserves: and let none be so unwise as to become a preacher of that faith, and love, and holiness, which he never had himself. And even to the calling of a physician none should be destined that do not have a special ingenuity, and sagacity, and natural quickness of apprehension; unless he should make a trade of killing men; for it is a calling that requires a quick and strong conjecturing ability, which no study will bring a man that does not have a natural acuteness and aptitude for it. Thus also as to all other callings, you must consider not only the will of the child or parents, but their natural fitness of body and mind.

**Direction 11. Choose no calling (especially if it is of public consequence) without the advice of some judicious, faithful persons of that calling.**

For they are best able to judge in their own profession. Never resolve on the sacred ministry without the advice of able ministers: do not resolve to be a physician except by the counsel of physicians; and so of the rest: for a great many persons ignorantly imagine themselves sufficient, that are utterly insufficient; and so live all their days as wrongs and burdens to others, and in sin and misery to themselves.

**Direction 12. If you are called to the poorest laborious calling, do not carnally murmur at it because it is wearisome to the flesh, nor imagine that God accepts the less of your work and you: but cheerfully follow it, and make it the matter of your pleasure and joy that you are always in your heavenly Master's service, though it is about the lowest things: and that he who knows what is best for you has chosen this for your good, and tries and values your obedience to him the more, by how much the humbler work you stoop to at his command.**

But see that you do it all in obedience to God, and not merely for your own necessity; thus every servant must serve the Lord in serving their masters, and from God expect their chief reward (Colossians 3:22–24; Ephesians 6:6–7).

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source. A draft English translation follows the Latin in parentheses; the translation was prepared for this project and is not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check it before relying on fine points.

[571] Omnes qui sunt, quique erunt, aut fuerunt, virtutibus aut doctrinis clari, non possunt unum ingenium accendere, nisi aliquæ intus in animo scintillæ sint, quæ preceptoris spiritu excitatæ et adjutæ, generosum disciplinæ fomitem arripiant. Petrarch. dial. 41. li. 2. (All who are, and who will be, or have been, famous for virtues or learning cannot kindle a single mind, unless there are some sparks within in the soul which, stirred up and helped by the teacher's spirit, may catch hold of the noble kindling of instruction. Petrarch, dialogue 41, book 2.)

## Translator's notes

- **Where this file begins:** the range opens at the start of Direction 2 (Chapter 10, Part 1, Title 1, on the right choice of a calling). Direction 1, the Chapter 10 heading, "Part 1," and the "Title 1" heading are in the preceding file. ("Title," Baxter's "Tit.," is his name for a heading or division of a Part.)
- **calling:** Baxter's word for one's vocation, occupation, or trade, understood as a place God assigns. Kept throughout. **a stated ordinary course of labor:** a settled, regular line of work.
- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **Proverbs 23:4 (flagged):** Baxter quotes the King James, "Labour not to be rich," and argues that the meaning is not to make riches one's chief end, while one may still "labour" in a way that tends to lawful gain. The NET reads "Do not wear yourself out to become rich; be wise enough to restrain yourself." The NET's "wear yourself out" is narrower than "labour," so the tension Baxter resolves (labor is commanded, yet "labour not to be rich") is less visible in the NET wording; his explanation still fits it. Baxter put the reference before the quotation; we moved it into parentheses after it, per our convention.
- **Colossians 3:22–24; Ephesians 6:6–7** (cited, not quoted): where the King James has "servants," the NET has "slaves." Baxter applies the passages to every servant and laborer in his calling.
- **Unmarked allusion:** "improve all your Master's talents" (Direction 9) alludes to the parable of the talents (Matthew 25:14–30); the reference is not in Baxter's text.
- **Cross-reference:** "Chapter 3, Direction 10" is Baxter's "chap. iii. direct. x." We have not checked that passage in this project.
- **Direction 7 and the bold headings:** Baxter's first sentence of Direction 7 runs on through several clauses; the bold heading ends at his colon and the same sentence continues in the next paragraph. No words were moved.
- **Word swaps (Baxter's word, then ours):** "Out of a calling," outside a calling; "but occasional," only occasional (also "usually but poor," usually only poor; "value him but as he deserves," value him only as he deserves); "unconstant," inconstant; "instruments," tools; "unfurnished," unequipped; "so be it," provided; "able for," fit for; "divers," several; "innocency," innocence; "resetting," harboring (the word could also mean receiving stolen goods; Baxter seems to mean harboring or protecting such people); "gamesters," gamblers; "sensual vices," vices of physical indulgence; "vintner," wine-seller; "still" (continually), continually or always; "twenty to one but," twenty to one that; "intended" (Direction 6), aimed at; "conduceth/conduce," contributes/contribute; "husbandmen," farmers; "periwig-makers," wig-makers; "take up with," settle for; "a divine," a minister; "exceeding great," exceedingly great; "painful," laborious; "commixed," intermingled; "intermissions," breaks; "discourse," talk; "commodity," profit; "meet," fitting; "love to," love for; "smattering knowledge," smattering of knowledge; "designed" (Direction 10), destined; "thereto," for it; "abundance of persons," a great many persons; "conceit themselves," imagine themselves; "but by," except by; "meaner," humbler.
- **when ... (Directions 2, 7, 8):** several times "when" means "whereas" ("when a man unused ... toils himself much in doing little"; "when others must be glad ..."; "when many men of richer callings ..."). Left as written.
- **footman:** a servant who ran errands and attended on foot. **day laborer:** one hired by the day. **half a crown:** two shillings and sixpence, Baxter's figure for a soldier's daily pay. **graziers:** those who raise and pasture cattle. **clothiers:** makers or sellers of cloth.
- ***cæteris paribus*** (Direction 6): Latin, "other things being equal." Kept as Baxter wrote it.
- **in time and measure** (Direction 4): at the proper time and in moderation. **stage-player:** an actor. Baxter's view of acting and gambling as trades is his own, kept as written.
- **recreation** (Direction 7): refreshment or renewal, not leisure activity in the modern sense. **well-tempered mind:** a mind of balanced disposition.
- **ingenuity** (Direction 10): natural ability, cleverness. Kept. **conjecturing ability:** the physician's skill of judging a case from signs; Baxter's word, kept.
- **carnally murmur** (Direction 12): grumble in an unspiritual way. "Carnal" is kept as Baxter's word (see the earlier notes).
- **Source oddity (Direction 4):** the 1846 text prints "it it will not follow"; we read one "it."
- **Note 571:** "preceptoris" is printed for the usual "praeceptoris" (teacher's); kept as printed. The passage is credited to Petrarch, "dial. 41. li. 2."; this is probably a dialogue in his *De remediis utriusque fortunae*, but we have not confirmed it.
