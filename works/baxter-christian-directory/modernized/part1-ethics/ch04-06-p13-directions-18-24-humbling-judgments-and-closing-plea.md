# Chapter 4: Directions 18 to 24 against Pride, and Baxter's closing plea for humility

*This file begins at the start of Direction 18, partway through the section "Directions against Pride, and for Humility"; the section heading is in an earlier file. It ends the section and the chapter's matter on pride.*

**Direction 18. Look to the humbling judgments of God on yourselves and others, and turn them all against your pride.**

You will surely think it an unsuitable and unseasonable thing for the calamitous to be proud. Are you not often complaining of one thing or another, upon your consciences, your bodies, your estates, your names, your relations, or friends? and yet will you be proud while you complain? If the judgments that have already befallen you do not humble you, if God loves you, and will save you, you may expect you should feel more, and the load should be increased, until it makes you stoop. O miserable, obstinate sinners! that can groan with sickness, and yet be proud! and murmur under want, and yet be proud! and daily crossed by one or another, and yet be proud! yes, and tormented with fears of God's displeasure, and yet be proud! Have not all the wars, and blood, and ruins that have befallen us in these kingdoms, been yet enough to take down pride? Many humbling sights we have seen, and many humbling stripes we have felt, and yet are we not humbled! We have seen houses robbed, and towns set on fire, and the country pillaged, and the blood of many thousands shed, and their carcasses scattered about the fields, and yet are we not humbled! If we were proud of our riches, they have been taken from us; if proud of our buildings, they have been turned into ruinous heaps; if we have been proud of our government, and the fame and glory of our country, we have seen how our sins have pulled down our government, dishonored our rulers, and blemished our glory, and turned it into shame; and yet are we not humbled! If you lived in a house infected with the plague, and had buried father and mother, and brothers and sisters, and only a very few were left alive, expecting when their turn would come next; if these few were not humbled, would you not think them blind and stupid persons? Do you yet look high, and contend for preeminence, and look for honor, and envy others, and desire to domineer, and have your will and way, and show yourselves off in the neatest dress? Must you have sharper stripes, before you will be humbled? Must greater injuries, and violences, and losses, and fears, and reproaches be the means? Why will you choose so painful a remedy, by frustrating the easier? If it must be so, the judgment will shortly come still nearer to you: it will either strip you of the rest, or cover you with shame, or lay you in pain upon your couch, where your head will ache, and your heart be sick, and your body weary, and you will pant and gasp for breath; will you then be proud, and contest for honor, when you expect hourly when your proud and guilty soul will be turned out of your body, and appear before the holy God? when the bell is ready to toll for you, and your winding-sheet to be fetched out, and your coffin prepared, and the bier to be fetched to carry you to your grave, and leave you in the dark with worms and rottenness; will you then be proud? Where then are your high looks, and lofty minds, and splendid ornaments, and honors? Then will you be climbing into higher positions, and seeking to be revenged on those who did eclipse your honor? David says, even of princes, and all the sons of men, Psalm 146:3-4, "Their life's breath departs, they return to the ground. On that day their plans die."

**Direction 19. Look on the lamentable effects of pride about you in the world, and that will help you to see the odiousness and pernicious nature of it.**

Do you not see how it sets the whole world on fire? how it raises wars, and ruins kingdoms, and draws out men's blood, and fills the world with malice and hatred, and cruelty and injustice, and treasons and rebellions, and destroys mercy, truth, and honesty, and all that is left of God upon the mind of man? From where is all the confusion and calamity, all the censoriousness, revilings, and cruelties, which we have seen, or felt, or heard of, but from pride? What is it that has trampled upon the interest of Christ and his gospel through the world, but pride? What else is it that has burnt his martyrs, and made havoc of his servants, and disrupted and divided his church with schisms, and set up so many sect-masters and sects, and caused them almost all to set against others, but this cursed, unmortified pride? He who has seen but what pride has been doing in England in this age, and yet does not discern its hatefulness and perniciousness, is strangely blind. Every proud man is a plague or burden to the place he lives in: if he gets high, he is a Nabal; a man can scarcely speak to him; he thinks all under him are made but to serve his will and honor, as inferior creatures are made for man. If he is an inferior, he scorns at the honor and government of his superiors, and thinks they take too much upon them, and that it is below him to obey. If he is rich, he thinks the poor must bow all to him, as to the golden calf, or Nebuchadnezzar's golden image: if he is poor, he envies the rich, and is impatient of the state that God has set him in: if he is learned, he thinks himself an oracle: if unlearned, he despises the knowledge which he lacks, and scorns to be taught. Whatever state he is in, he is a very salamander, that lives in the fire, he troubles house, and town, and country, if his power matches his heart: he is an unpolished stone, that will never lie even in any building; he is a natural enemy to quietness and peace.

**Direction 20. Consider well how God has designed the humbling of all that he will save, in his whole plan of the work of our redemption.**

He could have saved man by keeping him in his original innocence, if he had pleased. Though he does not cause sin, he knows why he permits it. He did not think it enough that man should have the thought of creation to humble him, as being taken from the dust, and made of nothing; but he will also have the sense of his moral nothingness and sinfulness to humble him: he will have him indebted to his Redeemer and Sanctifier for his new life and his salvation, as much as to his Creator for his natural life. He is permitted first to undo himself, and bring himself under condemnation, to be a child of death, and near to hell, before he is ransomed and delivered; that he may take to himself the shame of his misery, and ascribe all his hopes and recovery to God. No flesh will be justified by the works of the law, or by a righteousness of his own performance; but by the satisfaction and merits of his Redeemer; that so all boasting may be excluded, and that no flesh might glory in his sight, and that man might be humbled, and our Redeemer have the praise to all eternity. [238] And therefore God prepares men for faith and pardon, by humbling works, and forces sinners to condemn themselves before he will justify them.

**Direction 21. Read over the character which Christ himself gives of his true disciples; and you will see what great self-denial and humility he requires in all.**

In your first conversion you must become as little children, Matthew 18:3. Instead of contending for superiority and greatness, you must be ambitious to be servants to all, Matthew 23:11; 20:27. You must learn from him to be meek and lowly of heart, Matthew 11:28-29; and to stoop to wash your brothers' feet, John 13:5, 14. Instead of revenge or unpeaceable contending for your right, you must rather obey those who unjustly command you, and turn the other cheek to him who strikes you, and let go the rest to him who has unjustly taken from you; and bless those who curse you, and pray for them who hurt and persecute you, and treat you spitefully, Matthew 5:39-41, 44. [239] These are the followers of Christ.

**Direction 22. Remember how pride contradicts itself by exposing you to the hatred or contempt of all.**

All men abhor that pride in others which they cherish in themselves. A humble man is well thought of by all who know him; and a proud man is the target of common abuse. The rich disdain him, the poor envy him, and all hate him, and many deride him. This is his success.

**Direction 23. Look always to that dismal end, which pride tends to.**

It threatens apostasy. If God forsakes any one among you, and any of you forsakes God, his truth, and your consciences, and is made as Lot's wife, a monument of his vengeance for a warning to others, it will be the proud and self-conceited person. It makes all the mercies of God, your duties and parts, and objectively your very graces, to be its food and fuel. It is a sign you are near some dreadful fall, or heavy judgment: for God has given you this prognostic, Luke 14:11; 1:51; Proverbs 15:25; 16:5; Isaiah 2:11-12. An Ahab is safer when he humbles himself; and a Hezekiah is falling when he is lifted up. They are the most hardened sinners, scorning reproof, and therefore ordinarily forsaken both by God and man, and left to their self-delusion, until they perish.

**Direction 24. Converse with humbled and afflicted persons, and not with proud, secure worldlings.**

Be much in the "house of mourning," where you may see "the end of all the living, and be made better by laying it to heart;" and do not let your "hearts be in the house of mirth," Ecclesiastes 7:2-4. Do not delight to converse with "men that are in honor, and do not understand, but are like the beasts that perish; for though they think of perpetuating their houses, and call their lands after their own names," yet they "do not abide in" their "honor:" and "this their way is their folly," though "yet their posterity approve their sayings," Psalm 49:20, 12-14. Converse with penitent, humbled souls, who have seen the odiousness of sin, and the wickedness and deceitfulness of the heart, and can tell you by their own feeling what cause of humiliation is still before you. With these are you most safe.

I have been more extensive against pride, as seeing its prevalence in the world, and its harmful effects on souls and families, church and state; and because it is not discerned and resisted by many as it ought. I would gladly have God dwell in your hearts, and peace in your societies; and gladly have you stand fast in the hour of temptation, from prosperity or adversity; and gladly have affliction easy to you. But none of this will be without humility. I am loath that under the mighty hand of God we should be unhumbled, even when judgments bid us lay our mouths in the dust. [240] The storms have been long up; the cedars have fallen; it is the shrubs and bending willows that now are likeliest to escape. I am loath to see the prognostics of wrath upon your souls, or upon the land. I am loath that any of you should through pride be unhumbled of sin, or ashamed to own despised godliness; or that any who have seemed religious, should prove seditious, unpeaceable, or apostates. And therefore I beseech you, in a special manner, take heed of pride; be little in your own esteem; do not praise one another unseasonably; do not be offended by plain reproofs: look to your duties, and then leave your reputations to the will of God. Rebuke pride in your children; accustom them to humble attire and employments; do not cherish that in them which is most natural (now) and most pernicious. God dwells with the humble, and will take the humble to dwell with him, Isaiah 57:15; Job 22:29. "Clothe yourselves with ... humility, gentleness, and patience, bearing with one another," Colossians 3:12-13. "Be clothed with humility: serve the Lord with all humility of mind, and he will exalt you in due time," Acts 20:19; 1 Peter 5:6-7.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). Every NET wording below was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Start of range:** the file opens at Direction 18, inside the section "Directions against Pride, and for Humility"; the section heading was not repeated. Each Direction is headed in bold with Baxter's first sentence, and the rest follows as ordinary text. The source's last line, "PART VI.", is a printed part heading for what follows, not a sentence of the text, and is not carried into the body.
- **Psalm 146:4 (Direction 18):** Baxter wrote "His breath goeth forth: he returneth to his earth: in that very day his thoughts perish," the King James rendering. NET reads "Their life's breath departs, they return to the ground. On that day their plans die." The meaning is much the same; the NET is plural ("their"), where Baxter applies the singular "his" to each person, and has "plans" for "thoughts." Baxter cites verses 3 and 4; the quotation is of verse 4 only (verse 3 begins "Do not trust in princes," which is what he alludes to in "even of princes").
- **Ecclesiastes 7:2-4 (Direction 24):** Baxter's quotation is his own blend, not the King James. NET reads: "death is the destiny of every person, and the living should take this to heart" (7:2); "The heart of the wise is in the house of mourning, but the heart of fools is in the house of merrymaking" (7:4). Baxter's "be made better by laying it to heart" has no equivalent in the NET (the NET has "sober reflection is good for the heart" in 7:3), and his "house of mirth" is the King James ("merrymaking" in NET). We keep his wording, modernized, in his quotation marks.
- **Psalm 49 (Direction 24):** Baxter's quotation is the King James and its sense differs notably from the NET at three points, which affects his argument. (1) "men that be in honour, and understand not": NET 49:20 reads "Wealthy people do not understand; they are like animals that perish" (wealth, not honor). (2) "though they think of perpetuating their houses": the King James reads "Their inward thought is, that their houses shall continue for ever"; NET 49:11 reads "Their grave becomes their permanent residence, their eternal dwelling place. They name their lands after themselves," so the thought of perpetuating houses is not in the NET. (3) "abide not in honour" and "this their way is their folly" and "approve their sayings": NET 49:12-13 reads "despite their wealth, people do not last ... This is the destiny of fools, and of those who approve of their philosophy." Because the NET differs this much, we have kept Baxter's wording (modernized) and not substituted the NET. His verse order (20, then 12-14) is as printed.
- **Colossians 3:12-13 (closing paragraph):** Baxter wrote "Put on humbleness of mind, meekness, long-suffering, forbearing one another." NET reads "clothe yourselves with a heart of mercy, kindness, humility, gentleness, and patience, bearing with one another." We quote the NET with "..." for the words Baxter skips. The King James "humbleness of mind," "meekness," and "long-suffering" become "humility," "gentleness," and "patience."
- **Acts 20:19; 1 Peter 5:6-7 (closing paragraph):** Baxter's quotation is a blend of several verses and is not the wording of either reference. "Be clothed with humility" is the King James of 1 Peter 5:5 (not cited by him here, but cited in note 240). NET Acts 20:19 reads "serving the Lord with all humility and with tears"; NET 1 Peter 5:6 reads "And God will exalt you in due time, if you humble yourselves under his mighty hand." We keep his wording, modernized.
- **Unmarked Scripture allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "No flesh will be justified by the works of the law" (Romans 3:20; the NET has "no one will be declared righteous before him by the works of the law") and "no flesh might glory in his sight" (1 Corinthians 1:29, which Baxter cites in note 238); "lay our mouths in the dust" (Lamentations 3:29, cited in note 240); Lot's wife (Genesis 19:26); Nabal (1 Samuel 25); the golden calf (Exodus 32); Nebuchadnezzar's golden image (Daniel 3); Ahab humbling himself (1 Kings 21:27-29) and Hezekiah lifted up (2 Chronicles 32:25). "The bell ... to toll" is not Scripture.
- **Matthew 18:3; 23:11; 20:27; 11:28-29; John 13:5, 14; Matthew 5:39-41, 44 (Direction 21):** Baxter gives references only and does not put the verses in quotation marks; none are quoted. Modern references are converted from his.
- **Note 238:** the source prints "Cor. i. 29" with no number before it; this is presumably 1 Corinthians 1:29, which matches the text. We show the supplied "1" in brackets.
- **Note 239:** the source's "Mark x. 44; ix. 35, 36" is carried as printed.
- **Word swaps (Baxter's word, then ours):** You will sure think, "You will surely think"; yea, yes; oft, often; sottish, stupid; Whence is all ... but from pride, "From where is all ... but from pride" (his sentence kept); "look still unto," "look always to"; "impatient of," "scorns at," "that so," "let go" kept as Baxter's; set on fire (towns "fired"); primitive innocency, original innocence; contrivance, plan; beholden, indebted; distracted (his church), disrupted; answerable to his heart, matches his heart; obloquy, abuse; mark (of common obloquy), target; rooms (higher rooms), positions; brethren's, brothers'; injuriously, unjustly; despitefully use, treat spitefully; prevalency, prevalence; mischievous, harmful; fain, gladly; loth, loath; scape, escape; use them to mean attire, accustom them to humble attire; "set out yourselves in the neatest dress," show yourselves off in the neatest dress; "in a special manner" kept as written; "ambitious of being servants unto all," ambitious to be servants to all.
- **Technical or kept words:** winding-sheet (a burial cloth) and bier (a stand for a coffin) are kept. **Prognostic** (a forecast or sign) is kept. **objectively** (Direction 23) is kept as Baxter's scholastic word; it seems to mean that even the graces become a thing for pride to feed on, but we have not confirmed his intent. **secure** (Direction 24) is Baxter's "careless confidence." **unpeaceable** (Directions 21 and the closing paragraph) is kept as Baxter's word for those who will not live peaceably. **unmortified, interest (of Christ), sect-masters, salamander** are kept (the salamander was thought to live in fire; Baxter's image). **conversion** is his "first conversion."
- **Historical references:** "the wars, and blood, and ruins that have befallen us in these kingdoms," "houses robbed, and towns fired," "the plague," and "what pride has been doing in England in this age" refer to the civil wars and the troubles of Baxter's own lifetime; the passage names no event or date, so we add none.
- **Tenses:** Baxter's "if God love you," "if he get high," "if God forsake," and "if he be an inferior" are subjunctives; modernized to the indicative ("if God loves you," etc.). "and be made as Lot's wife" becomes "and is made as Lot's wife."
- **Word order and sentences:** none changed. The one place where we added a word is "do not" for his "not ... " imperatives ("praise not," "be not offended," "delight not," "cherish not"), which is the modern form of the same words.
