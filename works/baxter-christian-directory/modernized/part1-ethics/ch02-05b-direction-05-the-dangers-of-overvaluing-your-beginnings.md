# Chapter 2: Direction 5 (part 2 of 2)

*In the margin: The danger of overvaluing your young abilities or graces.*

1. If you imagine that you are at once men of great understanding, and ability, and holiness, while you are young beginners, and but newborn babes, you are entering into "the snare and condemnation of the devil," even into the odious sin of pride; indeed, a pride of those spiritual gifts which are most contrary to pride; indeed, a pride of that which you do not have, which is the most foolish pride. Note the words of Paul, when he forbids choosing a young beginner in religion for the ministry: "He must not be a recent convert, or he may become arrogant and fall into the punishment that the devil will exact" (1 Timothy 3:6). Why are young beginners more in danger of this than other Christians? One would think their infancy would be conscious of its own weakness. But Paul knew what he said. It is, (1.) partly because of the suddenness of their change; coming out of darkness into a light which they never saw before amazes them, and transports them, and makes them think they are almost in heaven, and that there is not much more to be attained. Like the beggar who had a hundred pounds given him, having never seen the hundredth part before, imagined that he had as much money as the king. (2.) And it is partly because they do not have knowledge enough to know how many things there are that they are still ignorant of. [53] They have never heard of the Scripture difficulties, and the knots in school divinity, nor the hard cases of conscience: whereas seven years of painful study will tell them of many hundreds of difficulties which they never saw; and forty or fifty years' study more will clothe them with shame and humility in the sense of their lamentable darkness. (3.) And it is also because the devil with greatest industry lays this net to entrap young converts, it being the way in which he has the greatest hope.

2. Your hasty notions of your own goodness or ability will make you presume on your own strength, and so venture upon dangerous temptations, which is the way to ruin. You will think you are not so ignorant but that you may venture into the company of papists, or any heretics or deceivers, or read their books, or be present at their worship. And I admit you may escape; but it may be otherwise, and God may leave you "to show you all that was in your hearts," as it is said of Hezekiah (2 Chronicles 32:25–26, 31).

3. And your overvaluing your first grace will make you too carelessly confident, when your souls have need of holy awe and care, to "continue working out your salvation with awe and reverence" (Philippians 2:12); and to "offer worship pleasing to God in devotion and awe," for "our God is indeed a devouring fire" (Hebrews 12:28–29). And careless confidence is the forerunner of a fall.

4. It will make you neglect the due labor and patience in the use of means for further knowledge and increase of grace, while you think you are so well already. [54] And so you will be worse than those that are ever learning, and never come to any ripe knowledge; for you will think that you are fit to be teachers when you have need to be taught that which you will not submit to learn. And then, as the Scripture says, "though you should in fact be teachers by this time, you need someone to teach you the beginning elements of God's utterances. You have gone back to needing milk, not solid food." Note here how the Holy Spirit makes time and exercise necessary to such growth as must enable you to be teachers (Hebrews 5:12–14). Therefore he adds, "But solid food is for the mature, whose perceptions are trained by practice to discern both good and evil." Note here how wisdom and strength are to be expected.

5. This over-hasty notion of your own ability will tempt you to run into controversies, and matters that you are not fit for; and so divert you from necessary and seasonable studies.

6. It will make you over-confident of all your own opinions, and stiff in all your own notions; too like him in Proverbs 14:16, "a fool throws off restraint and is overconfident." How many times have I heard a man who did not understand what he talked of, and could scarcely speak sense, plead for his opinion so confidently as to scorn or pity the wisest contradictor, when his ignorance, and frenzied confidence and rage, made him a real object of pity to men of ordinary understanding. There is a kind of madness in this disease, that will not leave you wit enough to know that you are mad.

7. It will make you also very censorious of others: this ignorant pride will make you think other men's knowledge to be ignorance if they are not just of your pet opinions; and other men's graces to be none if they are not of your mind and way. None are so ready as such to censure those that are better than themselves, or that they have no acquaintance with, as being but civil, moral men, or being mistaken or deluded. It is a very loathsome thing to hear an ignorant, self-conceited fellow talk of those that are a hundred times wiser and much better than himself in a lordly way, with a proud compassion or contempt, as if he were indeed the wise man, who does not know what he says.

8. And it will make you rebellious against your governors and teachers, and utterly unteachable, as despising those that should instruct and rule you. [55] You will think yourselves wiser than your teachers, while you are but in the lowest form. It is such that James speaks to: "Not many of you should become teachers, my brothers and sisters, because you know that we will be judged more strictly" (James 3:1). And that whole chapter, well worth your studying, is spoken to such.

9. And thus it will entangle you in heretical opinions, to which there is no greater preparation than pride-possessed, half-witted young beginners in religion.

10. And so it will make you troublers of the church, contending unpeaceably for that which you do not understand.

11. And it tends to hypocrisy, making you give thanks for that which you never had; as puffed up with a knowledge that is not enough to keep you humble, and lacking the love which would build up yourselves and others (1 Corinthians 8:1).

12. And it tends to delude you in the matter of assurance of salvation; taking your own overvaluing self-esteem for true assurance; which is not ordinarily to be expected until grace has come to strength.

13. Lastly, it tends to corrupt your understanding of the nature of Christianity itself; while you will judge of it in others according to your own overvalued measure: when, if you knew it as it is in the heart and practice of the sober, wise, humble, loving, peaceable, mortified, heavenly believer, you would see that it has a higher glory than any that is displayed by you.

I have named to you all these sad effects of overvaluing your beginnings in religion, that as you love your souls, you may avoid them. I take it to be a matter of exceeding great importance for your safety and perseverance that while you are infants in grace, you know yourselves to be such; that you may keep your place in the class, and learn first the lessons that must first be learned, and "walk humbly with your God," and obey "those that are over you in the Lord" (Hebrews 13:7, 17; 1 Thessalonians 5:12), and may wait on the Spirit in the use of means, and may not gladden the tempter by corrupting all that you have received, and imitating him in falling from your state of hope.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[53] Qui discipulum rudem et elatum habet, contra ventum adverso flumine navigat, serpentem nutrit, aconitum excolit, hostem docet. Petrarch. Dial. 41. li. 2. (He who has a rough and puffed-up pupil sails against the wind up a river against the current, feeds a serpent, cultivates aconite, and teaches an enemy. Petrarch, *Dialogue* 41, book 2.)

[54] Beatus est cui vel in senectute contigerit, qua sapientiam erasque opiniones consequi posset. Cicero de fin. (Blessed is he to whom it falls, even in old age, to be able to attain wisdom and true opinions. Cicero, *On Ends*. The source prints "erasque," apparently for "veramque.")

[55] Even when a teacher is impatient with his people's unprofitableness, they often think most highly of their knowledge, and they are proud while their dullness tires out their guides: for, Quo quisque est solertior et ingeniosior, hoc docet iracundius et laboriosius. Quod enim ipse celeriter arripuit, id quum tarde percipi videt, discruciatur. Cicero pro Ros. (The more skillful and talented anyone is, the more irritably and laboriously he teaches; for what he himself grasped quickly, when he sees it grasped slowly, he is tormented. Cicero, *For Roscius*.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **1 Timothy 3:6:** Baxter's quotation, "Not a novice … lest being lifted up (or besotted) with pride, he fall into the condemnation of the devil," is the King James with Baxter's own glosses. The NET reads "He must not be a recent convert, or he may become arrogant and fall into the punishment that the devil will exact." Baxter's earlier phrase "the snare and condemnation of the devil" blends 1 Timothy 3:6–7; we keep it in quotation marks as Baxter wrote it. Baxter's parenthetical glosses ("that is, a young, raw Christian"; "or besotted") are omitted because the NET's words ("recent convert," "arrogant") already say what they say.
- **2 Chronicles 32:25–26, 31 (reference corrected):** Baxter cites "xxxii. 21, 25, 26." The phrase "to show you all that was in your hearts" is his adaptation of verse 31, where God "left him alone to test him in order to know his true motives" (NET). Verses 25–26 are about Hezekiah's pride, which fits the Direction. We cite 32:25–26, 31.
- **Philippians 2:12; Hebrews 12:28–29 (important):** Baxter quotes "fear and trembling" and "reverence and godly fear." The NET reads "awe and reverence" and "devotion and awe." "Fear" is gone from both. Baxter's point in item 3 is that holy fear and care are needed, so the NET's wording is gentler than his. Flagged.
- **Hebrews 5:12–14:** NET wording. Baxter's "strong meat" is "solid food," and "full age" is "mature." Baxter's paraphrase of verse 12 ("you will have need to be catechised") is replaced by the NET's wording.
- **Proverbs 14:16:** NET reads "a fool throws off restraint and is overconfident"; Baxter's "The fool rageth and is confident." The NET's "overconfident" matches Baxter's use in item 6.
- **James 3:1:** NET wording. Baxter's "be not many masters, (or teachers), knowing that ye shall receive the greater condemnation" is "Not many of you should become teachers … because you know that we will be judged more strictly." Baxter's gloss "(or teachers)" is dropped.
- **1 Corinthians 8:1** (item 11): "love … build up" is the NET's wording ("knowledge puffs up, but love builds up"); Baxter writes "charity … edify."
- **Hebrews 13:7, 17; 1 Thessalonians 5:12:** the source prints "1 Thess. i. 5, 12," which is probably a misprint; verse 12 of chapter 5 ("those who are over you in the Lord") fits. We cite 5:12. "Walk humbly with your God" is an unmarked allusion to Micah 6:8.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "ever learning, and never come to any ripe knowledge" (2 Timothy 3:7); "puffed up" (Colossians 2:18).
- **school divinity:** the technical theology of the schools, the academic theology of the day. **cases of conscience:** see Direction 3.
- **forty or fifty years' study** (item 1): Baxter's point that a lifetime of study makes you more aware of how much you do not know.
- **at once:** Baxter's "presently." **carelessly confident; careless confidence:** his "secure" and "security," in the older sense. **hasty notions:** his "hasty conceits."
- **awe** (item 3): Baxter's "awfulness," meaning awe and holy fear. **pet opinions:** his "fond opinions."
- **in a lordly way:** Baxter's "magisterially." **frenzied:** his "phrenetic." **unpeaceably:** left as written.
- **keep your place in the class:** Baxter's "keep your form," where "form" meant a school bench or class. **gladden:** his "rejoice."
- **in the lowest form** (item 8): in the lowest class.
- **papists** (item 2): kept as Baxter's word for Roman Catholics.
- **understanding:** Baxter's "apprehensions" (item 13).
- **Petrarch** (note 53): Francesco Petrarca; **Cicero** (notes 54, 55): see the earlier notes. The *pro Roscio* Latin is a saying about the irritability of gifted teachers.
