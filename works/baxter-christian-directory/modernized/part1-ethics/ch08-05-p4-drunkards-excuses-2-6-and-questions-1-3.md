# Chapter 8: Part 4, Title 2, the drunkard's excuses (Objections 2–6), two questions of conscience, and questions for the drunkard (1–3)

*This file begins partway through section IV of Part 4, Title 2 (the drunkard's excuses), at Objection 2; the opening of section IV and Objection 1 are in the previous file, and Baxter's questions for the drunkard under section V continue in the next file.*

*Objection 2.* But I have heard some physicians say that it is wholesome to be drunk sometimes.

*Answer.* None but some sot, that had first drunk away his own understanding. I have known physicians that have been drunkards themselves, and they have been apt to plead for their own vice; but they quickly killed themselves, and all their skill could not save their lives from the effects of their own beastliness; even as the knowledge and doctrine of a wicked preacher will not save his soul, if he lives contrary to his profession. And what if the vomiting of a drunkard did him some good with all the harm? Are there not easier, safer, more lawful means enough to do the same good without the harm? He is a brute himself, and not a physician, that knows no better remedy than this. But your conscience tells you this is but a false excuse.

*Objection 3.* But I wrong nobody in my drink; the hurt is my own.

*Answer.* No thanks to you if you wrong nobody: but read over the former aggravations, and then justify yourself in this if you can. It seems you make nothing of wronging God by disobedience. But suppose it is no one's hurt but your own: do you hate yourself? is your own hurt nothing to you? what! do you make nothing of the damning of your own soul? whom will you love if you hate yourself? It is the aggravation of this sin, as well as fornication (1 Corinthians 6:18), that it is against your own bodies, and much more as against your own souls.

*Objection 4.* But I was only merry, I was not drunk.

*Answer.* It would be well for you if God would abide by your names and definitions, and take none for a sinner that does not take himself for one. There are several degrees of drunkenness short of the highest degree. And if your reason was not disturbed, yet the excess of drink only, and tippling, and greediness, will prove a greater sin than you suppose.

*Objection 5.* But I drink only a little; but my head is weak and a little overturns it.

*Answer.* If you know that beforehand, you are the more inexcusable, that will not avoid that measure which you know you cannot bear. If you knew that less poison will kill you than another, you would be the more fearful of it, and not the less.

*Objection 6.* But I have a thirst upon me, and I take no more than will quench it.

*Answer.* So the whoremonger says he has a lust upon him, and he takes no more than will quench it. And the malicious man that beats you or ruins you may say that he has a passion upon him, and he takes no more revenge on you than satisfies it. But if you add drunkenness to thirst, read your doom again (Deuteronomy 29:19). If it is a natural, moderate thirst, moderation will satisfy it; if it is a diseased thirst, as in a fever or dropsy, the physician must direct you in the cure; and small drink is fitter for a thirst than strong: but if it is the thirst of a drunkard's raging appetite, that has been used to be pleased, and therefore is loath to be denied, you had best quench it upon better and cheaper terms than the displeasing God and damning your souls; lest you find it more troublesome in the flames of hell to lack a drop of water for your tongues than it would have been to have bridled a beastly appetite. [444] And lest you then cry out as Lysimachus, when thirst forced him to yield to the Scythians for a little drink, *Quam brevis voluptatis gratia, quantum felicitatis amisi*! For how short a pleasure did I lose so great happiness! Take heed of reasoning your souls into impenitence.

*Question 1.* Is it not lawful to drink when we are thirsty, and know of no harm that it is likely to do us, seeing thirst tells us what the stomach needs?

*Answer.* A beast may do so, that has no higher faculty to guide him. And a man may take in the consideration of his thirst to guide his reason in judging of the due quantity and time; but not otherwise. A man must never drink to please his appetite, either against reason, or without it. And no man must so subject his reason to sense as to think that his appetite is his principal rule or guide in this; nor be so brutish as to know no otherwise what does him good or hurt but by his present feeling; sometimes true reason may tell a man that thirst is a sign that drink is needful to his health, and then he may take it. Sometimes (and commonly with dull-witted people) pleasing a thirst may hurt their health, and they are so foolish that they do not know it; either because they are ignorant of such things, or because their appetite makes them unwilling to believe it till they feel it; and because they judge only by the present effects: so a man may kill himself with drinking cold drink in a heat, in some fevers, in a dropsy, a cough, cachexy, etc. And excess imperceptibly corrupts the blood, and heaps up matter of many diseases which are incurable, before the sot will believe that drinking when he was thirsty did him any harm. If really it will do no harm, you may drink when you are thirsty (because it will do good). But if it will quench natural heat and hinder digestion, and breed diseases through unseasonableness, or ill quality, or excess, it is neither your thirst nor your sottish ignorance of the hurt that will excuse you from the sin, or prevent the coughs, stone, gout, colic, swellings, palsies, agues, fevers, or death, which it will bring.

*Question 2.* Is it not lawful to drink a health sometimes when it would be taken badly to refuse it, or to be bareheaded while others drink it?

*Answer.* Distinguish between, 1. Drinking moderately as you need it, and immoderately when you do not need it. 2. Between the foreseen effects; and doing it ordinarily, or when it will do hurt, or extraordinarily, when it will more prevent hurt. And so I conclude,

1. It is unlawful to drink more than is good for your health, by the urging of other men.

2. It is unlawful to do that which tempts and encourages others to drink too much. And so does the custom of pledging healths, especially when it is taken for a crime to deny it.

3. Therefore the ordinary pledging or drinking of such healths is unlawful, because it is the scandalous hardening of others in their sin to their ruin.

4. But if we fall in among such furious beasts as would stab a man if he did not drink a health, it is lawful to do it to save one's life, as it is to give a thief my purse; because it is a sin not simply evil of itself to drink that cup, but by accident, which a greater accident may outweigh.

5. Therefore any other accident besides the saving of your life, which will really outweigh the hurtful accident, may make it lawful; as possibly in some cases and companies the offense given by denying it may be such as will do more hurt by far than yielding would do. (As if a malignant company would make one's loyalty to the king hang upon it, etc.)

6. Christian prudence therefore (without carnal compliance) must be always the present decider of the case, by comparing the good and evil effects.

7. To be bareheaded when others make the honor of the king or superiors hang upon it is a ceremony that on the aforesaid reason may be complied with.

8. When to avoid a greater evil we are extraordinarily pressed into any such ceremony, it is fitting that we join such words (where we have liberty) as may prevent the scandal, or hardening any present in sin.

9. And it is a duty to avoid the company which will press us into such inconveniences, as far as our calling will allow us.

V. But because it is the drunkard's heart or will that needs persuasion, more than his understanding needs direction, I shall before the directions yet attempt his fuller conviction, if he will but read, and consider soberly (if ever he is sober), these following questions, and not leave them till he answers them to the satisfaction of his own conscience.

*Question 1.* Do you know that you are a man? and what a man is? Do you know that reason differentiates him from a beast that is ruled by appetite and has no reason? If you do, let your reason do its office, and do not drown it, or set the beast above it.

*Question 2.* Do you believe that there is a God that is the Governor of the world, or not? If not, tell me how you came to be a man? And how came your tongue and palate to taste your drink or food, any more than your finger? Look on your finger and on your tongue, and you can see no reason why one should taste and not the other? If you live in the midst of such a world, which he has made and daily governs, and yet do not believe that there is a God, you are so much worse already than drunk or mad that it is no wonder if you are a drunkard. But if you do believe indeed that there is a God, hear further, you stupid beast, and tremble! Is he the Governor of heaven and earth, and is he not worthy to be the Governor of you? Is all the world at his disposal, and is he not worthy to dispose of your throat and appetite? Are crowns, and kingdoms, heaven, and hell, at his disposal and will, and is he not worthy to be master of your cup and company? will you say to him by your practice, go rule sun and moon, and rule all the world, except my appetite and my cup?

*Question 3.* Do you truly believe that God is present with you, and sees and hears all that is done and said among you? If not, you do not believe that he is God! For he that is absent, and ignorant, and is not infinite, omnipresent, and omniscient, is not God; and if God is not there, you are not there yourself; for what can uphold you, and continue your life, and breath, and being? But if you believe that God is present, do you dare drink on, and do you dare before him waste your time in chattering over a pot with your companions?

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source. A draft English translation follows the Latin in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[444] Bibendi consuetudo auget aviditatem. Plin. (The habit of drinking increases the craving. Pliny.) Perinde est vinolentiam bibendo velle sedare, atque ignem materia apposita pergere extingere: nam quod naturæ appetitioni datur moderatum est, at vitiosa et preter naturam libido, nullo expletur. Acosta ub. sup. (It is just the same to want to allay a craving for wine by drinking as to go on putting out a fire by heaping fuel on it: for what is given to the appetite of nature is moderate, but a vicious lust that is beyond nature is filled by nothing. Acosta, in the place cited above.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). No verse is quoted in this range; the NET wording given below for the cited verses was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Labels:** this file sits in Part 4, Title 2 (against drunkenness). "Tit." in the source is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," a heading or division within a Part. The roman "V." is Baxter's section number within Title 2 (section IV, the drunkard's excuses, began in the previous file). Baxter numbers his Objections and Questions with roman numerals; they are given here as *Objection 2.*, *Question 1.*, and so on. His Questions are numbered twice: once for the two questions of conscience about thirst and healths, and again from 1 for the questions put to the drunkard under section V; both numberings are kept.
- **1 Corinthians 6:18** (Objection 3): cited, not quoted; Baxter's "against your own bodies" is his own wording. NET: "Flee sexual immorality! 'Every sin a person commits is outside of the body'—but the immoral person sins against his own body." The NET sets the middle clause in quotation marks as a saying Paul is citing, but the point Baxter uses (the sin is against one's own body) stands in both.
- **Deuteronomy 29:19** (Objection 6): "if you add drunkenness to thirst" is the King James phrase ("to add drunkenness to thirst"), which Baxter leans on here as he did in the previous file. The NET has nothing about drunkenness or thirst in this verse: "When such a person hears the words of this oath he secretly blesses himself and says, 'I will have peace though I continue to walk with a stubborn spirit.' This will destroy the watered ground with the parched." So the verse does not carry Baxter's link to drinking in the NET. Kept in Baxter's wording, since it is not set as a quotation.
- **Unmarked allusion:** "to lack a drop of water for your tongues" in the flames of hell is the rich man in Luke 16:24 (NET: "send Lazarus to dip the tip of his finger in water and cool my tongue because I am in anguish in this fire").
- **Lysimachus** (Objection 6): Baxter gives the Latin saying and his own English translation ("For how short a pleasure did I lose so great felicity!"); his English is kept, with "felicity" changed to "happiness." We have not traced the source of the anecdote.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): drank away → drunk away; bestiality → beastliness (in 1673 "bestiality" meant beastly, brutish behavior, not its modern sexual sense); lawfuller → more lawful; but merry → only merry; stand to → abide by; gulosity → greediness (Latin-derived word for gluttonous greed); unexcusable → inexcusable; undoes → ruins; want → lack; felicity → happiness; captivate his reason to sense → subject his reason to sense ("captivate" meant to make captive); herein → in this; blockish → dull-witted; insensibly → imperceptibly; vitiate → corrupt; concoction → digestion (the old physiology's term for the stomach's "cooking" of food); cholic → colic; ill-taken → taken badly; uncovered / bare → bareheaded (removing the hat as a mark of honor while a health is drunk); measurably / unmeasurably → moderately / immoderately; provocation → urging; preponderate → outweigh; lay ... upon it → make ... hang upon it (make it the test of one's loyalty or of the king's honor); put on / put upon → pressed into / press into; meet → fitting; endeavour → attempt; differenceth → differentiates; meat → food (in 1673 "meat" meant food generally); verily → truly; prating → chattering.
- **Words kept:** sot / sottish (a habitual drunkard, a fool); aggravations (Baxter's technical term for what makes a sin heavier; "the former aggravations" are the points on the greatness of the sin in the previous file); tippling (habitual small drinking); dropsy (an old name for swelling from fluid retention); small drink (weak beer, as opposed to strong drink); cachexy (a general wasting or ill condition of the body); stone (kidney or bladder stones); agues (fevers with fits of shivering); palsies (paralysis or tremors); natural heat (a term of the medicine of Baxter's day for the body's vital warmth); healths and pledging (toasts, and answering a toast by drinking in return); inconveniences (in Baxter's day a stronger word: harms or improprieties, not mere nuisances); conviction (being brought to see and own one's sin).
- **Dated medicine:** Baxter's medical claims (cold drink killing a man "in a heat," excess "corrupting the blood," quenching "natural heat") follow the physiology of his time; they are kept as his.
- **"by accident"** (items 4–5): a scholastic term (Latin *per accidens*): drinking that cup is not evil in itself but only because of its circumstances, and a weightier circumstance may outweigh them.
- **"a malignant company"** (item 5): kept. "Malignant" means ill-disposed or hostile; in the political language of the mid-seventeenth century it was also used as a party label, and we have not determined whether Baxter intends any such overtone here.
- **"the displeasing God"** (Objection 6): kept as printed (it reads as "than displeasing God").
- **"you can see no reason why one should taste and not the other?"** (Question 2 under section V): printed with a question mark after a statement; kept as printed.
- **Note 444:** "preter" is printed for *praeter* and "extingere" for *extinguere*; both kept as printed. "Plin." is presumably Pliny the Elder; we have not located the passage. "Acosta ub. sup." (*ubi supra*, "where cited above") refers to Acosta as cited in an earlier note; we have not checked which work or passage is meant.
