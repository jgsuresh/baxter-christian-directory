# Chapter 4: Directions against Inordinate Man-pleasing, Direction 8 and Direction 9 (points 1-12)

**Direction 8. And remember that honor is such a thing as is found sooner by an honest contempt of it, than by an inordinate affection of it, and seeking it.**

It is a shadow which goes from you if you follow it, and follows you as fast as you go from it. Whose names are now more honorable upon earth, than those prophets, and apostles, and martyrs, and preachers, and holy, mortified Christians, who in their days thought least of the approval of the world, and were made the scorn or football of the times in which they lived? Those that have been satisfied with the approval of their heavenly Father, who saw them "in secret," have been "rewarded by him openly." It is, even in the eyes of rational men, a far greater honor to live to God, above worldly honor, than to seek it. And so much as a man is perceived to strive for and seek it, so much he loses of it: for he is thought to need it; and men perceive that he plays a low and pitiful game, that is so desirous of their applause! As they would despise a man that should lick up the spittle of every man where he comes, so will they despise him that lives on their thoughts and breath, and honor him more that lives on God.

**Direction 9. If nothing else will cure this disease, at least let the impossibility of pleasing men, and attaining your ends, suffice against so fruitless an attempt.**

And here I shall show you how impossible it is, or, at least, a thing which you cannot reasonably expect.

1. Remember what a multitude you have to please; and when you have pleased some, how many more will be still unpleased, and how many displeased when you have done your best. [177] Alas! we are insufficient at once to observe all those that observe us and would be pleased by us. You are like one that has but twelve pence in his purse, and a thousand beggars come about him for it, and every one will be displeased if he has it not all. If you resolve to give all that you have to the poor, if you do it to please God, you may attain your end; but if you do it to please them, when you have pleased those few that you gave it to, perhaps twice as many will revile or curse you, because they had nothing. The beggar that fares well will proclaim you generous; and the beggar that fares badly will proclaim you stingy and unmerciful; and so you will have more to offend and dishonor you, than to comfort you by their praise, if that must be your comfort.

2. Remember that all men are so selfish, that their expectations will be higher than you are able to satisfy. They will not consider your hindrances, or distractions, or what you do for others, but most of them look to have as much to themselves, as if you had nobody else to mind but them. Many and many a time, when I have had an hour or a day to spend, a multitude have every one expected that I should have spent it with them. When I visit one, there are ten offended that I am not visiting them at the same hour: when I am talking with one, many more are offended that I am not speaking to them all at once: if those that I speak to account me courteous, and humble, and respectful, those that I could not speak to, or but in a word, account me discourteous and morose. How many have censured me, because I have not allowed them the time, which God and conscience commanded me to spend upon greater and more necessary work! If you have any office to give, or benefit to bestow, which one only can have, every one thinks himself the fittest; and when you have pleased one that has it, you have displeased all that went without it, and missed of their desires.

3. You have abundance to please that are so ignorant, unreasonable, and weak, that they take your greatest virtues for your faults, and know not when you do well or ill; and yet none more bold in censuring than those that least understand the things they censure. [178] Many and many a time my own and others' sermons have been censured, and openly defamed, for that which never was in them, upon the ignorance or heedlessness of a censorious hearer; yes, for that which they directly spoke against; because they were not understood: especially he that has a close style, free from tautology, where every word must be marked by him that will not misunderstand, shall frequently be misreported.

4. You will have many factious zealots to please, who being strangers to the love of holiness, Christianity, and unity, are ruled by the interest of an opinion or a sect; and these will never be pleased by you, unless you will be one of their side or party, and conform yourself to their opinions. If you are not against them, but set yourselves to reconcile and end the differences in the church, they will hate you as not promoting their opinions, but weakening them by some abhorred syncretisms. As in civil, so in ecclesiastical wars, the firebrands cannot endure the peaceable: if you will be neutrals, you shall be used as enemies. If you are never so much for Christ, and holiness, and common truth, all is nothing, unless you are also for them, and their notions.

5. Most of the world are haters of holiness, and have a serpentine enmity to the image of God, being not renewed by the Holy Spirit; and will not be pleased with you, unless you will sin against your Lord, and do as they do. 1 Peter 4:3-5, "You lived then in debauchery, evil desires, drunkenness, carousing, drinking bouts, and wanton idolatries. So they are astonished when you do not rush with them into the same flood of wickedness, and they vilify you. They will face a reckoning before Jesus Christ who stands ready to judge the living and the dead." You must be counted as Lot among the Sodomites, a busy fellow that comes among them to make himself their judge, and to control them, if you tell them of their sin. You shall be called a precise, hypocritical coxcomb, (or somewhat much worse,) if you will not be as bad as they, and if by your abstinence (though you say nothing) you seem to rebuke their indulgence in physical pleasure and contempt of God. Among bedlams you must play the bedlam, if you will escape the fangs of their revilings. And can you hope to please such men as these?

6. You shall have satanical God-haters, and men of seared and desperate consciences to please, that are malicious and cruel, and will be pleased with nothing but some horrid iniquity, and the damning of your own souls, and drawing others to damnation. Like that monster of Milan, that when he had got down his enemy, made him blaspheme God in hope to save his life, and then stabbed him, calling it a noble revenge, that killed the body and damned the soul at once. There are such in the world, that will so visibly act the devil's part, that they would debauch your consciences with the most horrid perjuries, treachery, and impiety, that they may triumph over your miserable souls. [179] And if you think it worth the willful damning of your souls, it is possible they may be pleased. If you tell them, we cannot please you, unless we will be dishonest, and displease God, and sin against our knowledge and consciences, and risk our salvation, they will make but a jest of such arguments as these, and expect you should venture your souls and all upon their opinions, and care as little for God and your souls as they do. Desperate sinners are reluctant to go to hell alone; it is a torment to them to see others better than themselves. They that are cruel and unmerciful to themselves, and have no pity on their own souls, but will sell them for a whore, or for advancement, and honor, or physical delights, will scarcely have mercy on the souls of others: Matthew 27:25, "Let his blood be on us and on our children!"

7. You will have rigorous, captious, uncharitable, and unrighteous men to please, who will "bear false testimony against a person, who entrap the one who arbitrates at the city gate and deprive the innocent of justice by making false charges," Isaiah 29:20-21. That have none of that charity which covers faults, and interprets words and actions favorably; nor none of that justice which causes men to do as they would be done by, and judge as they would be judged; but judging without mercy, are likely to have judgment without mercy. And are glad when they can find any matter to reproach you: and if once they meet with it (true or false) they will never forget it, but dwell as the fly on the ulcerated place. [180]

8. You will have emotional persons to please, whose judgments are blinded, and are not capable of being pleased. Like the sick and sore that are hurt with every touch; and at last, says Seneca, with the very notion that you touched them. How can you please them, when displeasedness is their disease, that abides within them, at the very heart?

9. You will find that censoriousness is a common vice, and though few are competent judges of your actions, as not being acquainted with all the case, yet every one almost will be venturing to cast in his censure. A proud, presumptuous understanding is a very common vice; which thinks itself at once capable of judging, as soon as it hears but a piece of the case, and is not conscious of its own fallibility, though it has daily experience of it. Few are at your elbow, and none in your heart, and therefore know not the circumstances and reasons of all that you do, nor hear what you have to say for yourselves; and yet they will presume to censure you, who would have absolved you, if they had but heard you speak. It is rare to meet even with professors of greatest sincerity, that are very tender and fearful of sinning, in this point of rash, ungrounded judging, without capacity or call.

10. You live among quarrelsome tattlers and tale-carriers, that would please others by accusing you. Who is it that has ears that has not such vermin as these earwigs busy at them? except here and there an upright man, whose angry countenance has still driven away such backbiting tongues. And all shall be said behind your backs, when you are incapable of answering for yourselves. And if it be a man that the hearers think well of, that accuses or backbites you, they think it lawful then to believe them: and most that are their friends, and of their party, and for their interest, shall be sure to be thought so honest as to be credible. And it is not strange, for a learned, ingenious, yes, a godly person to be too forward in uttering, from the mouth of others, an evil report; and then the hearer thinks he is fully justified for believing it, and reporting it again to others. David himself by the temptation of a Ziba, is drawn to wrong Mephibosheth, the son of his great deserving friend, 2 Samuel 16:3-4. No wonder then if Saul do hearken to a Doeg, to the wrong of David, and murder of the priests. Proverbs 18:8, "The words of a gossip are like choice morsels." Proverbs 26:20, "Where there is no wood, a fire goes out, and where there is no gossip, contention ceases." And when these are still near men, and you far off, it is easy for them to continue the most odious representation of the most laudable person's actions in the world.

11. The imperfection of all men's understandings and godliness is so great, that the differences of judgment that are among the best, will tend to the injury and undervaluing of their brethren. One is confident that his way is right, and another is confident of the contrary: and to how great contendings and injuries such differences may proceed, he that knows not in this age, shall not know for me. [181] We need not go to Paul and Barnabas for an instance (that was a far lighter case); nor to Epiphanius, Jerome, and Chrysostom; nor to those ages and tragedies of contending bishops, that in the eastern and western churches have been before us: every one thinking his cause so plain, as to justify himself, in all that he says and does against those that presume to differ from him. And surely you may well expect some displeasure, even from good and learned men, when the church has felt such dreadful shocks, and bleeds to this day, by so horrid divisions, through the remnants of that pride and ignorance which her reverend guides have still been guilty of. [182]

12. You have men of great changeableness to please; that one hour may be ready to worship you as gods, and the next to stone you, or account you as devils, as they did by Paul, and Christ himself. What a weathercock is the mind of man! especially of the common people and the temporizers! When you have spent all your days in building your reputation on this sand, one blast of wind or storm at last, does tumble it down, and all your cost and labor are lost. Serve men as submissively and carefully as you can; and after all, some accident or failing of their unrighteous expectations, may make all that ever you did forgotten, and turn you out of the world with Wolsey's groans, "If I had served God as faithfully as man, I had been better rewarded, and not forsaken in my distress." How many have fallen by the hands or frowns of those whose favor they had dearly purchased, perhaps at the price of their salvation! If ever you put such confidence in a friend, as not to consider that it is possible he may one day prove your enemy, you know not man; and may perhaps be better taught to know him, to your cost.

The translations of Latin below are drafts prepared for this project and need a Latinist's check. Latin is kept exactly as printed; English in the notes is modernized like the body.

[177] Unus mihi pro populo est, et populus pro uno. Sen. Ep. 7. ex Democr. Satis sunt mihi pauci, satis est unus; satis est nullus. Senec. Epist. 7. (English: One is to me as the people, and the people as one. Seneca, *Epistle* 7, from Democritus. Few are enough for me, one is enough, none is enough. Seneca, *Epistle* 7.) Socrates was condemned by the votes of more against him of his judges than those that absolved him; and they would not allow Plato to speak for him. His sentence was, Jura violat Socrates, quos ex majorum instituto suscepit civitas, deos esse negans, alia vero nova dæmonia inducens. Laert. in Socrat. (English: Socrates breaks the laws, denying that the gods are those whom the city has received by the institution of the elders, and bringing in other, new divinities. Laertius, in the life of Socrates; draft translation, rendered loosely since the Latin grammar is awkward.)

[178] Quæ ego scio populus non probat: quæ probat populus ego nescio. Sen. Epi. 29. (English: What I know, the people do not approve; what the people approve, I do not know. Seneca, *Epistle* 29.) Imperitia in omnibus majori ex parte dominatur, et multitudo verborum. Cleobulus in Laert. (English: Inexperience rules in all things for the most part, and a multitude of words. Cleobulus, in Laertius; rendered loosely.)

[179] Inter hæc quid agant quibus loquendi à Christo officium mandatur? Deo displicent, si taceant; hominibus si loquantur. Salvian. ad Eccles. Cath. 1. 4. (English: Among these things, what are they to do to whom the duty of speaking is committed by Christ? They displease God if they are silent; men, if they speak. Salvian, *To the Catholic Church*, book 4.)

[180] Even for the greatness of your services, you may perish, by the suspicion and envy of those great ones whom you served: as is proved by the case of Saul and David, Belisarius, Narses, Bonifacius, the two sons of Huniades imprisoned, and one slain, and multitudes such like.

[181] Persium non curo legere: Lælium Decimum volo: ut Lucilius. (English: I do not care to read Persius; I want Laelius Decimus: as Lucilius said. Draft translation, rendered loosely; the sense of "volo" here is uncertain.)

[182] I may add that you have guilty consciences to please. And the guilty are, as Seneca speaks, like one that has an ulcer, that at first is hurt with every touch, and at last even with the suspicion of a touch. Tutum aliqua res in mala conscientia præstat, nulla securum. Putat enim etiamsi non deprehenditur, se posse deprehendi; et inter somnos movetur, et quoties alicujus scelus loquitur, de suo cogitat. Sen. Epis. 106. (English: Something may give an evil conscience protection; nothing gives it security. For it thinks, even if it is not caught, that it can be caught; and it is disturbed in its sleep, and whenever someone speaks of another's crime, it thinks of its own. Seneca, *Epistle* 106, as printed.) Prima et maxima peccantium pœna est peccasse.--Hæc et secundæ pœnæ premunt et sequuntur, timere semper et expavescere et securitate diffidere. Epis. 97. (English: The first and greatest punishment of sinners is to have sinned. These secondary punishments also press upon them and follow: always to fear and tremble and to distrust security. *Epistle* 97.) Tyranno amici quoque sæpe suspecti sunt. Tu ergo, si tyrannidem tuto tenere cupis, atque in ea constabiliri, civitatis principes tolle, sive illi amici, sive inimici videantur. Thrasybulus in Epist. Periand. in Laertio. (English: Even friends are often suspected by a tyrant. You therefore, if you wish to hold tyranny safely and be established in it, remove the leading men of the city, whether they seem to be friends or enemies. Thrasybulus, in the letter to Periander, in Laertius.) Plerorumque ingenium est, ut errata aliorum vel minima perscrutentur, benefacta vero vel in propatulo posita prætereant; sicut vultures corpora viva et sana non sentiunt, morticina vero et cadavera tametsi longe remota odore persequuntur. Galiadus in Arcan. Jesuit, p. 55. (English: It is the nature of most people to search out even the smallest faults of others, but to pass over good deeds even when set in the open; just as vultures do not notice living and healthy bodies, but follow dead carcasses and corpses by scent, even when far away. Galiadus, in the *Arcana* of the Jesuits, page 55.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). Each quoted verse was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Range:** the file begins at the start of Direction 8 and ends at the end of point 12 of Direction 9; the rest of Direction 9 (points 13 onward) continues in the next file. Direction labels: Baxter's "Direct. VIII." and "IX." are rendered "Direction 8." and "9."; the first sentence of each is in bold. The section heading for this part of Chapter 4 appears in an earlier file.
- **"in secret" / "rewarded by him openly" (Direction 8; flag):** Baxter gives no reference. The words echo Matthew 6:4, 6, 18. The NET reads "your Father, who sees in secret, will reward you" (Matthew 6:4; the same clause at 6:6 and 6:18). "In secret" matches the NET. The NET has no "openly" (the King James has "reward thee openly"). Baxter's argument (honor comes "openly" to those who seek only God's approval) leans on the missing word, so we kept his wording inside his quotation marks, modernized.
- **1 Peter 4:3-5 (flag):** Baxter's quotation is King James wording ("Walking in lasciviousness, lusts, excess of wine, revellings, banquetings, and abominable idolatries, wherein they think it strange that ye run not with them to the same excess of riot ... who shall give account to him that is ready to judge the quick and the dead"). We used the NET for the range 4:3-5. The NET changes some details: "You lived then in" (a past tense, where the King James has the participle "Walking"); "debauchery, evil desires, drunkenness, carousing, drinking bouts, and wanton idolatries" (for the King James's list; "wanton" for "abominable"); "astonished" for "think it strange"; "the same flood of wickedness" for "the same excess of riot"; "They will face a reckoning" for "who shall give account." The NET also begins verse 3 with a sentence about the time having been "sufficient ... to do what the non-Christians desire," which Baxter does not quote; we begin at "You lived." The NET has "the living and the dead" for "the quick and the dead."
- **Matthew 27:25:** NET wording. The NET has "In reply all the people said, 'Let his blood be on us and on our children!'" Baxter's "His blood be on us" is the King James form; we quote the NET words only.
- **Isaiah 29:20-21 (flag):** Baxter's quotation is the King James: "make a man an offender for a word, and lay a snare for him that reproveth in the gate, and turn aside the just for a thing of nought, and watch for iniquity." The NET reads differently and in a different order: "all those who love to do wrong will be eliminated (v. 20) ... those who bear false testimony against a person, who entrap the one who arbitrates at the city gate and deprive the innocent of justice by making false charges (v. 21)." The NET has "false testimony" and "false charges" where the King James has "for a word" and "for a thing of nought," so the idea of condemning a man over a mere word is lost; the NET also has "arbitrates" for "reproveth." The King James's fourth clause, "watch for iniquity," corresponds to the NET's "all those who love to do wrong" at 29:20, which we left out of the quotation; the quotation is the NET's verse 21 from "bear" onward (its "those who" is left out so that Baxter's "who will" leads in). His printed reference is "Isa. xxix. 20, 21."
- **Proverbs 18:8 (flag):** Baxter's "as wounds" is the King James ("the words of a talebearer are as wounds"). The NET reads "The words of a gossip are like choice morsels, and they have gone down into the person's innermost being." The NET makes the point that gossip is tempting to swallow, not that it wounds, though both readings go on to the same inner effect. We quote only the first clause of the NET, "The words of a gossip are like choice morsels." The Hebrew word is difficult; the NET's rendering differs from the King James at this point, and we leave the question to a specialist.
- **Proverbs 26:20:** NET wording. Baxter's "the fire goeth out: so where" has "so"; the NET has "a fire goes out, and where there is no gossip." "Tale-bearer" is the NET's "gossip."
- **2 Samuel 16:3-4:** cited only (Ziba's report about Mephibosheth, and David's judgment). **Doeg** and **Saul** and the murder of the priests (1 Samuel 22): unmarked allusion, as Baxter has it. **Lot among the Sodomites:** unmarked allusion (Genesis 19); we have not quoted it. **Paul and Christ himself** being worshipped, then stoned or called a devil (item 12): unmarked allusions (Acts 14:11-19 for Paul; for Christ, compare John 8:48 and 10:20); we have not quoted them. **Paul and Barnabas** (item 11): their quarrel is at Acts 15:36-41 (unmarked).
- **Word swaps (Baxter's word, then ours):** "honour(able)" to "honor(able)"; "set lightest by" to "thought least of"; "approbation" to "approval"; "foot-ball" to "football"; "contemn" to "despise"; "affect" (in "affect and seek it," meaning to aim at or pursue) to "strive for"; "hinderances" to "hindrances"; "discoursing" to "talking" (item 2); "avocations" (then, things that call a person away from his main business) to "distractions"; "liberal" to "generous"; "niggardly" to "stingy"; "speeds well / ill" to "fares well / badly" ("speed" meaning to succeed or fare); "conceits" to "notions" (items 4 and 8); "Holy Ghost" to "Holy Spirit"; "sensuality" to "indulgence in physical pleasure" (item 5); "reprehend" to "rebuke"; "perfidiousness" to "treachery"; "wilful" to "willful"; "loth" to "reluctant"; "preferment" to "advancement"; "sensual delights" to "physical delights"; "hazard" to "risk"; "scarce" to "scarcely"; "passionate" to "emotional"; "presently" to "at once"; "uncapable" to "incapable"; "unpeaceable" to "quarrelsome"; "mutability" to "changeableness"; "the vulgar" to "the common people"; "Hierom" to "Jerome"; "concussions" to "shocks"; "neuters" to "neutrals"; "yea" to "yes"; "suffer" (in note 177) to "allow." "Like to have" is rendered "likely to have."
- **Kept as Baxter wrote:** "temporizers" (those who change their views to suit the time); "coxcomb" (a vain, foolish person); "precise" (strict in religion; a common word of abuse for Puritans, here in the mouth of Baxter's opponents); "bedlams" (madmen; from the Bethlehem hospital in London); "captious" (fond of finding fault); "censure(s)" and "censorious(ness)" (judgment, usually adverse; fault-finding); "syncretisms" (attempts to join differing views); "firebrands"; "tautology" (needless repetition; Baxter means a plain style with no repeated words, so every word must be heeded); "professors" (people who profess religion); "debauch" (to corrupt); "ingenious" (here, clever or learned); "displeasedness" (his own word); "backbiting"; "earwigs" (a figure for those who whisper in the ear); "laudable"; "control" (item 5, in the sense of check or correct); "serpentine"; "morose" (sullen, ill-humored); "satanical."
- **Baxter's own wording, kept:** "twelve pence" (a shilling). "Satanical God-haters." Items 4 and 6: strong words about factions and about "that monster of Milan"; Baxter gives no source for the story and we have not identified it. We make no claim about it.
- **Wolsey (item 12):** the words are attributed by Baxter to Wolsey; we take this to be Cardinal Wolsey, but we have not confirmed the saying and keep it as Baxter's. The quotation marks are Baxter's; the words are modernized.
- **"Seneca" (item 8):** the saying about the sick being hurt "with the very conceit" of a touch is attributed to Seneca without a reference; Baxter gives the same comparison in his own words in note 182, whose Latin from Seneca's *Epistle* 106 (as printed) is about a guilty conscience. We have not checked whether the same idea appears elsewhere in Seneca.
- **Notes 177, 178:** "Sen." is Seneca; "Laert." is Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of the Philosophers*. Baxter's references are kept as printed and we have not checked them against editions. Note 177's Latin "Jura violat Socrates, quos ... deos esse negans" is kept as printed; the relative "quos" seems to refer to "deos." **Note 179:** "Salvian" is, we take it, the fifth-century writer Salvian of Marseilles; the reference is as Baxter gives it. **Note 180:** Belisarius, Narses, and Bonifacius are named as Baxter has them; he gives no details, and we have not added any. **Note 181:** the source names "Lucilius" without further detail; we have not traced the saying. **Note 182:** "Galiadus in Arcan. Jesuit" is as printed; we have not identified the work. The marker "--" in the Latin after "peccasse" is kept as printed.
- **Note 182:** begins with Baxter's English ("I may add that you have guilty consciences to please"), which reads as a further point added to the end of point 11; we have kept it where Baxter put it.
