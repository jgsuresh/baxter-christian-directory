# Advertisement to the Readers (part 1 of 3)

**Advertisement.**

Readers,

The book is so big that I must make no longer preface than to give you this necessary, short account, I. Of the quality; II. And the reasons of this work.

1. The matter you will see in the contents: As Amesius's *Cases of Conscience* are to his *Medulla*, the second and practical part of theology, so is this to a *Methodus Theologiæ* (Method of Theology) which I have not yet published. And, 1. As to the method of this, it is partly natural, but principally moral; that is, partly suited to the real order of the matter, but chiefly of usefulness, _secundum ordinem intentionis_ (according to the order of intention), where our reasons for each placement are drawn from the end. Therefore unless I were to be tedious in setting out my reasons _à fine_ (from the end) for the order of every particular, I do not know how to give you full satisfaction. But in this practical part I am the less concerned about the accuracy of method, because it belongs more to the former part (the theory), where I do it as well as I am able.

2. This book was written in 1664 and 1665 (except the Ecclesiastical Cases of Conscience, and a few sheets since added). And since the writing of it, some invitations drew me to publish my *Reasons of the Christian Religion*, my *Life of Faith*, and *Directions for Weak Christians*; by which the work of the first two chapters here is more fully done; and therefore I was inclined here to leave them out; but for the use of such families as may have this without the others, I refrained from dismembering it.

3. But there is a great disproportion between the several parts of the book. 1. The First Part is largest, because I thought that the heart must be kept with greatest diligence, and that if the tree is good the fruit will be good; and I remember Paul's counsel, "Be conscientious about how you live and what you teach. Persevere in this, because by doing so you will save both yourself and those who listen to you" (1 Timothy 4:16). Nothing is well done by him that does not begin at home: as the man is, so is his strength, and work. 2. The first two chapters are too coarse and tedious for those of the higher class, who may pass them over. But the rest must be spoken to; to whom that is unprofitable which is most suitable and pleasant to more practiced and precise minds. The grand directions are but the explanations of the essentials of Christianity, or of the baptismal covenant, even of our relational duties to God the Father, Son (in several parts of his relation), and of the Holy Spirit. The doctrine of Temptations is handled with brevity, because they are so numerous; lest a due amplification should have swelled the book too much; when a small part of their number makes up so much of Mr. John Downame's great and excellent treatise, called *The Christian Warfare*. The great radical sins are handled more largely than seems proportionate to the rest, because all die when they are dead. And I am long about Redeeming Time, because in it the sum of a holy, obedient life is included.

4. If any say, Why do you call that a Sum of Practical Theology which is but the directing part, and leaves out the explanation, reasons, various uses, marks, motives, etc.? I answer, 1. Had I intended, in the manner of a sermon, to say all that might well be said on each subject, it would have made many volumes as big as this. 2. Where I thought them needful, the explanation of each duty and sin is added, with marks, contraries, counterfeits, motives, etc. And uses are easily added by an ordinary reader, without my naming them.

5. I especially desire you to observe that the resolving of practical cases of conscience, and the reducing of theoretical knowledge into serious Christian practice, and promoting a skillful ease in the faithful exercise of universal obedience, and holiness of heart and life, is the great work of this treatise; and that where I thought it needful, the cases are reduced to express questions and answers. But had I done so with all, many such volumes would have been too little; and therefore I thought the directing way most brief and fit for Christian practice; for if you mark them, you will find few directions in the book which may not pass for the answer to an implied question or case of conscience; and when I have given you the answer in a direction, an intelligent reader can tell what question it is that is answered. And so, many hundred cases are here resolved, especially in the first two parts, which are not named as questions.

6. And I must do myself the justice of notifying the reader that this treatise was written when I was (for not subscribing, declaring, etc.) forbidden by the law to preach, and when I had been long separated far from my library and from all books, except an inconsiderable handful which wandered with me, wherever I went; by which means this book has two defects: 1. It has no cases of conscience but what my bare memory brought to hand: and cases are so innumerable that it is far harder, it seems to me, to remember them than to answer them; by which it came to pass that some of the ecclesiastical cases are put out of their proper place, because I could not remember them at the right time. For I had no one casuist but Amesius with me. But (after about twelve years' separation) having received my library, I find that the very sight of Sayrus, Fragoso, Roderiquez, Tolet, etc. might have helped my memory to a greater number. But perhaps these will be enough for those that I intend them for. 2. And by the same cause the margin is unsupplied with such citations as are counted an ornament, and in some cases are very useful. The scraps inserted out of my few trivial books at hand being so poor that I am well content (except about Monarchy, Part IV) that the reader pass them by as not worthy of his notice.

And it is likely that the absence of books will appear to the reader's loss in the materials of the treatise; but I shall have this advantage by it, that he will not accuse me as a plagiarist. And it may be some little advantage to him that he has no transcript of any man's books which he had before; but the product of some experience, with a naked, unbiased perception of the matter or things themselves.

7. Note also that the Third and Fourth Parts are very much lacking in what they should contain about the power and government of God's officers in church and state; of which no readers will expect a reason but strangers, whose expectations I may not satisfy. But as I must profess that I hope nothing here has proceeded from disloyalty, or disrespect to authority, government, unity, concord, peace, or order; or from any opposition to faith, piety, love, or justice; so if, unknown to me, there is anything found here that is contrary or injurious to any one of these, I do hereby renounce it, and desire it may be taken as _non scriptum_ (unwritten).

II. The ends and uses for which I wrote this book are these: 1. That when I could not preach the gospel as I would, I might do it as I could. 2. That three sorts might have the benefit, as follows.

1. That the younger and more ill-equipped and inexperienced sort of ministers might have a storehouse at hand for practical resolutions and directions on the subjects that they need to deal in. And though Sayrus and Fragoso have done well, I would not have us under a necessity of going to the Romanists for our ordinary supplies. Long have our divines been wishing for some fuller treatise of cases of conscience: Perkins began well; Bishop Sanderson has done excellently _de juramento_ (on the oath); Amesius has exceeded all, though briefly. Mr. David Dickson has put more of our English cases about the state of sanctification into Latin than ever was done before him. Bishop Jeremy Taylor has in two folios but begun the copious performance of the work. And still men are calling for more, which I have attempted: hoping that others will come after, and do better than we all.

If any call it my pride to think that any ministers or students are so raw as to need anything that I can add to them, let him but pardon me for saying that such demure pleadings for a feigned humility shall not draw me into an alliance with blindness, hypocrisy, and sloth, and I will pardon him for his charge of pride.

It is long ago that many foreign divines signed a request that the English would give them in Latin a Sum of our Practical Theology, which Mr. Dury sent over, and twelve great divines of ours wrote to Bishop Usher (as Dr. Bernard tells you in his *Life*) to draw them up a form or method. But it was never done among them all. And it is said that Bishop Downame, at last undertaking it, died in the attempt. Had this been done, it is likely my labor might have been spared. But it being undone, I have thus made this attempt. But I have been compelled to leave out much (about conversion, mortification, self-denial, self-acquaintance, faith, justification, judgment, glory, etc.) because I had written of them all before.

2. And I thought it not unuseful to the more judicious heads of families, who may choose and read such portions to their families as at any time the case requires. And indeed I began it simply, with an intention of that plainness and brevity which families require; but finding that it swelled to a bigger bulk than I intended, I was obliged to write my *Life of Faith*, as an abridgment and substitute, for the families and persons that cannot have and use so large a volume: presupposing my *Directions for Sound Conversion*, for *Weak Christians*, and for *Peace of Conscience*, printed long ago.

3. And to private Christians I thought it not in vain to have at hand so universal a directory and resolution of doubts; not expecting that they remember all, but that they may, on every occasion, turn to such particulars as they most need.

But I must expect to be assaulted with these objections: and it is not only profane mockers and malicious enemies that are used by Satan to vilify and oppose our service of God.

## Translator's notes

- **Latin:** the Latin phrases are kept exactly as printed, with a draft English translation in parentheses after each. The translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition; have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.
- **Scripture translation:** quoted Scripture is from the NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **1 Timothy 4:16 (flag):** Baxter quotes the King James, "Take heed unto thyself, and unto the doctrine; continue in them: for in doing this thou shalt both save thyself, and them that hear thee." He leans on "take heed unto thyself" to support beginning "at home," with the heart. The NET's "Be conscientious about how you live and what you teach" puts the stress on conduct rather than on oneself, so the link to keeping the heart is a little less direct. The rest of the sense (saving yourself and your hearers) is kept.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "the heart must be kept with greatest diligence" (compare Proverbs 4:23); "if the tree is good the fruit will be good" (compare Matthew 12:33); "as the man is, so is his strength" (compare Judges 8:21). These references are ours, not in Baxter's text.
- **Amesius:** the Latin form of the name of William Ames, the English Puritan theologian. His *Cases of Conscience* and his *Medulla* (probably the *Medulla Theologiae*, "Marrow of Theology") are the two works Baxter pairs. Baxter's comparison: the *Directory* stands to his own unpublished systematic work as Ames's practical book stands to his doctrinal one.
- ***Methodus Theologiæ*:** Baxter's planned "Method of Theology," which he says he has not yet published. We have not checked its later publication history.
- **method "natural" and "moral"** (point 1): Baxter means that the order of the book follows partly the order of the subject matter itself, but chiefly the order of purpose or use, arranged by the end each part serves.
- **solicitous:** changed to "concerned." **forbore to dismember it:** "refrained from dismembering it" (he means he did not cut the first two chapters out).
- **Ecclesiastical Cases of Conscience** (point 2): Baxter's name for a part of this book (probably Part III; not checked); kept capitalized as he has it.
- **the two first chapters:** rendered "the first two chapters" (and "the first two parts" in point 5).
- **those of the higher class** (point 3): Baxter's "of the higher form," a school form or class: more advanced readers. **wits:** "minds."
- **relational duties:** Baxter's "relation-duties," the duties that arise from our relation to each Person of the Trinity.
- **"because all die when they are dead"** (point 3): Baxter's compressed wording, kept. He means that when the great root sins die, all the other sins die with them.
- **Redeeming Time:** the title of a section of the book, probably in Part 1 (not checked; the phrase is from "redeeming the time," Ephesians 5:16, Colossians 4:5); kept capitalized.
- **Mr. John Downame's *The Christian Warfare*:** a large Puritan treatise on temptation, named by Baxter.
- **sermonwise:** "in the manner of a sermon" (point 4).
- **skillful ease:** Baxter's "skilful facility." **universal obedience:** kept; obedience to all of God's commands.
- **intelligent reader:** Baxter's "ingenious reader," which in his day could mean able or thoughtful as well as clever.
- **not named as questions:** Baxter's "not interrogatively named."
- **"for not subscribing, declaring, etc."** (point 6): Baxter refers to the oaths and declarations required of ministers after the Restoration; this is probably the Act of Uniformity of 1662, under which he and many others were silenced. We have not checked the exact statutes he means.
- **casuist:** kept; a writer on cases of conscience. **Sayrus, Fragoso, Roderiquez, Tolet:** names kept as Baxter prints them. These are Roman Catholic writers on cases of conscience; we have not confirmed the identification of each (Tolet is probably Francisco de Toledo).
- **methinks:** "it seems to me." **seasonably:** "at the right time." **parcel:** "handful." **mean:** "poor." **plagiary:** "plagiarist." **unbiassed:** "unbiased."
- **Monarchy, Part IV:** Baxter's cross-reference to the material on monarchy in Part IV (the political part) of this book. Not checked.
- **"of which no readers will expect a reason but strangers"** (point 7): kept as written. Baxter implies that his readers at home will understand why he could not write freely about church and state authority in his circumstances.
- ***non scriptum*:** "unwritten," that is, to be treated as never written.
- **storehouse:** Baxter's "promptuary," a store of ready material. **ill-equipped:** his "unfurnished." **treatise of cases of conscience:** his "casuistical tractate."
- **Romanists:** Baxter's term for Roman Catholics; kept as his polemic usage.
- **divines:** kept; theologians, clergy.
- **Perkins; Bishop Sanderson; Mr. David Dickson; Bishop Jeremy Taylor:** names kept as Baxter gives them. Sanderson's work *de juramento* is probably his Latin lectures on the obligation of oaths; Taylor's "two folios" is probably his *Ductor Dubitantium*. Both identifications are ours and unchecked.
- **Mr. Dury; Bishop Usher; Dr. Bernard; Bishop Downame:** names kept as printed. Probably John Dury, Archbishop James Ussher (modern spelling), and Nicholas Bernard, whose *Life* of Ussher Baxter cites; the "Bishop Downame" who died in the attempt is probably George Downame. These identifications are ours and unchecked, as is the story Baxter reports ("it is said").
- **essay:** "attempt." **necessitated:** "compelled." **confederacy:** "alliance."
- **heads of families:** Baxter's "masters of families." **parcels:** "portions."
- **began it simply:** Baxter's "began it rudely," meaning in a plain, unpolished way. **fain:** "obliged." **breviate:** "abridgment."
- **Baxter's books named:** *Reasons of the Christian Religion*, *Life of Faith*, *Directions for Weak Christians*, *Directions for Sound Conversion*, and directions for *Peace of Conscience*; titles as Baxter gives them, not checked against the title pages.
- **profane mockers and malicious enemies:** Baxter's "profane deriders and malignant enemies." "Malignant" was also a party label in his day for royalist opponents of the Puritans; we chose "malicious," and the political overtone may be lost.
- **End of range:** the range stops just before Baxter's list of objections; the next file continues with them.
