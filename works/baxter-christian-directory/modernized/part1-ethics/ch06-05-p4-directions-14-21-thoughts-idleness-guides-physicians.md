# Chapter 6: Title 5, Directions 14-21 to the melancholy

*This file continues Title 5 (directions to the melancholy) from the previous file; it begins at a new Direction and runs to the end of Title 5.*

**Direction 14. Do not make too great a matter of your own thoughts; and do not take too much notice of them; but if Satan casts in molesting thoughts, if you cannot cast them out, make light of them, and take less notice of them.**

Making a great matter of every thought that is cast into your mind will keep those thoughts in your mind the longer. For that which we are most aware of, we most think on; and that which we least regard, we least remember. If you would never be rid of them, the way is to be always noting them, and making too great a matter of them. These troublesome thoughts are like troublesome scolds, that if you regard them, and answer them, will never have done with you; but if you let them talk, and take no notice of them, nor make any answer to them, they will be weary and give up. The devil's design is to vex and disquiet you; and if he sees you will not be vexed and disquieted, he will give up attempting it. I know you will say, Should I be so ungodly as to make light of such sinful thoughts? I answer, Do not make so light of them as to be indifferent what thoughts are in your mind, nor so as to take the small sin to be none; but make so light of them as not to take them for greater nor more dangerous sins than they are; and so light of them as not to take distinct, particular notice of them, nor to disquiet yourselves about them; for if you do, you will have no room in your thoughts for Christ and heaven, and that which should take up your thoughts; but the devil will rejoice to see how he employs you in thinking over your own thoughts, or rather his temptations; and that he can employ you all the day in listening to all that he will say to you, and in thinking of his promptings instead of thinking on the works of God. There are none of God's servants without irregularities and sin of thoughts, which they must daily ask forgiveness of, and rejoice to think that they have a sufficient Savior and remedy, and that sin shall only occasion the magnifying of grace; but if they should excessively observe and be troubled at every unwarranted thought, it would be a snare to take them off almost all their greater duties. Would you like it in your servant, if he should stop in observing and troubling himself about every ordinary imperfection in his work, instead of going on to do it?

**Direction 15. Remember that it is no sin to be tempted, but only to yield to the temptation; and that Christ himself was carried about and tempted blasphemously by the devil, even to fall down and worship him; and yet he made these temptations only an advantage to the glory of his victory.**

Do not take the devil's sin to be yours. Are your temptations more horrid and odious than Christ's were? What if the devil had carried you to the pinnacle of the temple as he did Christ? Would you not have thought that God had forsaken you, and given you up to the power of Satan? But you will say that you yield to the temptation, and Christ did not do so. I answer, It cannot be expected that sinful man should bear a temptation as innocently as Christ did? Satan found nothing in Christ to comply with him; but in us he finds a sinful nature! Wax will receive an impression when marble will not. But it is not every sinful taint that is a consent to the sin to which we are tempted.

**Direction 16. Consider how far you are from loving, delighting in, or being reluctant to leave these sinful thoughts; and that no sin condemns but that which is so loved and delighted in as that you would rather keep than leave it.**

Would you not gladly be delivered from all these horrid thoughts and sins? Could you not be willing to live in disgrace, or poverty, or banishment, so long as you might only be free from sin? If so, why do you doubt about the pardon of it? Can you have any surer sign of repentance, or that your sin is not a reigning, unpardoned sin, than that it is not loved and desired by you? The less will, the less sin, and the more will, the more sin. The greedy man loves his money, and the fornicator loves his lust, and the proud man loves his honor, and the drunkard loves his cups, and the glutton loves to satisfy his appetite; and so love these that they will not leave them. But do you love your disturbing, confused, or blasphemous thoughts? Are you not so weary of them as to be even weary of your lives because of them? would you not be glad and thankful never to be troubled with them more? And yet do you doubt about pardon?

**Direction 17. Do not charge your souls any deeper than there is cause with the effects of your disease.**

Indeed remotely a man that in madness thinks or speaks amiss may be said to be faulty, so far as his sin did cause his disease; but directly and of itself, the involuntary effects of sickness are no sin. Melancholy is a mere disease in the spirits and imagination, though you feel no sickness; and it is as natural for a melancholy person to be hurried and molested with doubts, and fears, and despairing thoughts, and blasphemous temptations, as it is for a man to talk nonsense in a fever when his understanding fails; or to think of and desire drink, when his fever kindles vehement thirst. And how much would you have a man in a fever accuse himself for such a thirst, or such thoughts, desire, or talk? If you had those hideous thoughts in your dreams which you have when you are awake, would you think them unpardoned sins, or rather unavoidable infirmities? why, your disorder makes them to be to you only almost as dreams.

**Direction 18. Be sure that you keep yourself constantly employed (as far as your strength will bear) in the diligent labors of a lawful calling; and spend none of your precious time in idleness.**

Idleness is the tide-time of the tempter: when you are idle, you invite the devil to come and vex you. Then you can have time to listen to him, and think on all that he will put into your minds, and then to think over all those thoughts again! When you have nothing else to do, the devil will find you such work. Then you must sit still and muse; and your thoughts must be stirring in the mud of your own disorders, as children lie paddling in the dirt. And idleness is a sin which God will not favor. He has commanded you to "labor six days, and in the sweat of your brows to eat your bread; and he that will not labor is unworthy to eat" (2 Thessalonians 3). Remember that time is precious, and hastens away, and God has given you none in vain. Therefore, as you are troubled for other sins, make a matter of conscience of this sin, and do not waste one quarter of an hour's time in your idle, unprofitable musings. It is just for God to make your sin itself to be your punishment, and your own idle thoughts to chastise you daily, when you will not get up and go about your lawful business. Nor will pretexts of prayer, or any devotion, excuse your idleness, for it is against the law of God. Above all that I have said to you, let me entreat you therefore to obey this one direction. I have known despairing, melancholy persons cured by setting themselves resolutely and diligently about their callings (and changing air and company, and riding out.) If you will sit musing in a corner, and sin against God by idleness and loss of time, and increase your own miseries as well, rather than you will rouse up yourself, and ply your business, your calamity is just. Do not say that you have little or nothing to do; for God has made it the duty of all, be they ever so rich, to labor in such employment as is suitable to their place and strength.

**Direction 19. Only mark well how much the devil gets by keeping you in sad, despondent thoughts; and then you may easily see that it cannot be your duty, nor best for you, which is so gainful and pleasing to the devil.**

By keeping you in these self-perplexing doubts and fears, he robs God of the thanks and praise which you owe him for all his mercies. These highest duties you cast aside, as if they did not belong to you. You do not give God the honor of his most miraculous mercy, in our redemption; nor do you study, or relish, or admire, or magnify the riches of grace in Jesus Christ! You have poor, low thoughts of the infinite love of God, and are unfit to judge of it or perceive it, being like a choleric stomach, which puts a continual bitterness in the mouth, which hinders it from tasting any sweetness in their food. It by this unfits you for the love of God, and more inclines you to hate him, or flee from him as an enemy, while the devil represents him to you as one that hates you; it wastes your time; it deprives you of all your willingness to duty, and delight in duty, and makes all God's service a burden and vexation to you. It is very contrary to the spirit of adoption, and to the whole frame of evangelical worship and obedience. And will you, under pretense of being more humbled, and sorrowful, and sensitive, thus gratify Satan, and wrong God and yourselves.

**Direction 20. Do not trust your own judgment, in your melancholy state, either as to the condition of your souls, or the choice and conduct of your thoughts or ways; but commit yourselves to the judgment and direction of some experienced, faithful guide.**

You are no fit judges of your own condition, nor of the way of your duty, in this dark, disordered condition that you are in. Either your mind and imagination is well or ill: if it is well, why do you complain of all those disturbances, and confusions, and inability to meditate and pray? If it is ill, why will you be so self-conceited as to think yourselves able to judge of yourselves, with such a disordered imagination of mind? It is one of the worst things in melancholy persons, that commonly they are most wise in their own eyes, and stiff in their own notions, when their brains are sickest, and their understanding weakest; and that they are confident, and unruly, and unpersuadable, as if they were proud of those pitiful understandings, and thought nobody knows so well as they. Oh! they say, you do not know my case! Am I not more likely to know your case, who have seen so many score in that case, than you are that never knew it in any but yourself? A man that stands by may better know the case of a man that is in a dream than he can know his own. You say that others do not feel what you feel! no more does the physician feel what a man in a fever, or epilepsy, or madness feels; and yet by the report of what you say you feel, and by what he sees, he far better knows your disease, the nature and the cure of it, than you that feel it. Therefore as a wise man, when he is sick, will trust himself, under God, to the direction of his physician and the help of his friends about him, and not lie wrangling against their help and counsel, and willfully refuse it, because they advise him contrary to his feeling; so will you do, if you are wise; trust yourself with some fit director; and do not despise his judgment either about your state, or about your duty. You think you are lost, and there is no hope: hear what he says that is now fitter to judge. Do not set your weak wit too willfully against him. Do you think he is so foolish as to mistake? should not humility make you rather think so of yourself? Be advised by him about the matter of your thoughts, the manner and length of your secret duties, and all your scruples that you need advice in. Will you answer me this one question? Do you know anybody that is wiser than yourself? and fitter to judge of your condition and advise you? If you say, no; how proud are you of such a crazed wit! If you say, yes; then believe and trust that person, and resolve to follow his direction. And I would ask you, were you not once of another judgment concerning yourself? If so, then were you not as sound and able to judge, and more likely to be in the right than you are now.

**Direction 21. My last advice is to seek out the cure of your disease, and commit yourself to the care of your physician, and obey him; and do not do as most melancholy persons do, who will not believe that medicine will do them good, but that it is only their soul that is afflicted; for it is the spirits, imagination, and passions that are diseased, and so the soul is like an eye that looks through a colored glass, and thinks all things are of the same color as the glass is.**

I have seen a great many cured by medicine; and till the body is cured, the mind will hardly ever be cured, but the clearest reasons will be all in vain.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **Title:** Baxter's "Tit." is his abbreviation for "Title," that is, a heading or division of the chapter. Chapter 6 has six Titles; this file finishes Title 5, the directions to the melancholy. The next line of the source begins Title 6 (directions for young students).
- **melancholy:** kept as Baxter's word (see `GLOSSARY.md`): a lasting low, fearful, or brooding state, understood in his day as partly rooted in the body. It is not a modern clinical diagnosis, and none is substituted here. His medical assumptions (the "spirits," a "choleric stomach," the body's state darkening the mind) are his and his age's; they are kept as written.
- **2 Thessalonians 3 (Direction 18):** Baxter's quotation is a blend, not a quotation of one verse, so his wording is kept (modernized) in his quotation marks with his reference. "Labor six days" echoes Exodus 20:9 (NET: "For six days you may labor and do all your work"); "in the sweat of your brows to eat your bread" echoes Genesis 3:19 (NET: "By the sweat of your brow you will eat food"); "he that will not labor is unworthy to eat" is his paraphrase of 2 Thessalonians 3:10 (NET: "If anyone is not willing to work, neither should he eat"). The Exodus and Genesis references are not in Baxter's text. The NET's "you may labor" in Exodus 20:9 reads as a permission where Baxter treats the verse as a command ("He has commanded you"); flagged.
- **"eat you bread"** (Direction 18): the source prints "eat you bread," evidently a misprint for "your bread"; corrected.
- **Direction 15, unmarked allusions:** Christ "carried about" by the devil, the "pinnacle of the temple," and the temptation to "fall down and worship him" are from Matthew 4:5–9 (compare Luke 4:5–9). "Satan found nothing in Christ to comply with him" alludes to John 14:30 (King James: "the prince of this world cometh, and hath nothing in me"). The NET reads "He has no power over me," which loses the "nothing in me" on which Baxter builds (no sinful nature in Christ for Satan to work on, unlike the "wax" of our nature); flagged. Not quoted, so kept in Baxter's words.
- **"It cannot be expected ... as Christ did?"** (Direction 15): the source ends this sentence with a question mark although it reads as a statement; kept.
- **Other unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "the spirit of adoption" (Romans 8:15); "wise in their own eyes" (compare Proverbs 3:7; 26:12; Isaiah 5:21); "the riches of grace" (compare Ephesians 1:7; 2:7). References not in Baxter's text.
- **Direction 19, final sentence:** "And will you ... wrong God and yourselves." is a question in sense but ends with a period in the source; kept. Its "It ... it ... it" (from "It by this unfits you") refers to the sad, despondent state of mind, not to the devil; kept as Baxter wrote it.
- **Direction 20, final sentence:** "If so, then were you not as sound ..." is ambiguous in the source (it may mean "then you were not" or be a question); Baxter's word order and period are kept.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): set light by, make light of; sensible of, aware of; still (noting), always; give over, give up; hearkening/hearken, listening/listen; motions, promptings; Saviour, Savior; unwarrantable, unwarranted; loth, reluctant; fain, gladly; want, poverty; so you might but be, so long as you might only be; doubt you of / doubt of, do you doubt about / doubt about; covetous, greedy; honour, honor; distraction, madness (in his day "distraction" meant derangement of mind, not inattention); talk idly, talk nonsense (the raving of fever); distemper(s)/distempered, disorder(s)/disordered; while (time), time; doth haste, hastens; make conscience of, make a matter of conscience of; just with God, just for God; pretences/pretence, pretexts/pretense; withal, as well; never so rich, ever so rich; do but mark, only mark; meat, food; hereby, by this; loseth your time, wastes your time; fly, flee; sensible (feeling one's sin deeply), sensitive; fantasy, imagination (his "fantasy" is the faculty of imagination, not daydream); conceits, notions; disability (to meditate), inability; liker, more likely; falling-sickness, epilepsy; look out for, seek out; physic, medicine; abundance, a great many; riding abroad, riding out (riding on horseback away from home, not foreign travel); yea, yes.
- **Kept with a note:** "reigning sin" (Direction 16), Baxter's technical term for a sin that rules the heart and is not repented of. "the spirits" (Directions 17, 21): the "animal spirits," subtle bodily fluids then thought to carry the action of the brain and nerves. "a mere disease" (Direction 17): "mere" here means "wholly, purely" a disease. "tide-time of the tempter" (Direction 18): the tempter's best season, when the tide serves him. "choleric stomach" (Direction 19): a stomach troubled by "choler," bile, then thought to leave a bitter taste. "crazed wit" (Direction 20): a cracked, damaged mind, not only "insane." "secret duties" (Direction 20): private devotions such as secret prayer and meditation. "scolds" (Direction 14): quarrelsome, nagging people. "calling" (Direction 18): one's work or occupation. "self-conceited" (Direction 20): opinionated, wise in one's own opinion.
- **"and Christ did not do so"** (Direction 15): Baxter's "and so did not Christ"; the words are rearranged into modern order, with "do" added.
- **Heading sentence of Direction 21:** the whole Direction is one paragraph in the source; its first sentence is set as the bold heading and the rest as the body, as in the other Directions.
