# Chapter 7: Title 3, Directions 9–20 against sinful desires

**Direction 9. Remember that it is one of the greatest plagues on this side of hell, to be given up to our own desires, and that by your eagerness and discontents you provoke God thus to give you up.**

"I gave them over to their stubborn desires; they did what seemed right to them. If only my people would obey me!" etc. (Psalm 81:12–13). "Therefore God gave them over in the desires of their hearts to impurity," etc. (Romans 1:24, 26). "For this reason God gave them over to dishonorable passions" (verse 28). "And just as they did not see fit to acknowledge God, God gave them over to a depraved mind, to do what should not be done" (2 Thessalonians 2:10–12). God may give you that which you so eagerly desire, as he gave "Israel a king, even in his anger" (Hosea 13:10, 11). Or as he gave the Israelites "their own desire, even meat which he rained upon them as dust, and feathered birds as the sand of the sea; they were not estranged from their craving: but while their food was still in their mouths, the wrath of God came upon them, and killed the fattest of them" (Psalm 78:27, 29–31). "In the wilderness they had an insatiable craving for meat; they challenged God in the wastelands. He granted their request, then struck them with a disease" (Psalm 106:14, 15). God may say, Follow your own desire, and if you are so eager, take that which you desire; take that person, that thing, that dignity which you are so earnest for; but take my curse and vengeance with it: may it never do you good, but be a snare and torment to you. "Let a fire come out of the bramble and devour you" (Judges 9:15).

**Direction 10. Be careful lest concupiscence and partiality entice you to justify your sinful desires and take them to be lawful.**

For if you do so, you will not repent of them, you will not confess them to God, nor beg pardon for them, nor beg help against them, nor use the means to extinguish them; but will cherish them, and be angry with all that are against them, and love those tempters best that encourage them: and how dangerous a case is this! And yet nothing is more ordinary among sinners than to be blinded by their own affections, and think that they have sufficient reason to desire that which they do desire. And affection makes them very clever and resolute to deceive themselves. It sets them on studying all that can be said to defend their enemy, and put a deceitful gloss upon their cause. Test your desires well (as I directed you before). Question 1. Is the thing that you desire a thing that God has told you to desire, or promised in his word to give you (as grace, Christ, and heaven)? If it is so, then desire it, and do not hold back; but if not so, Question 2. Why then are you so eager for it when you should at most have only a submissive, conditional desire for it? Question 3. Indeed, is it not something which you are forbidden to desire? If so, do you dare excuse it?

**Direction 11. Remember that concupiscence or sinful desire is the beginning of all sin of commission, and leads directly to the act.**

Theft, adultery, murder, fraud, contention, and all such mischiefs begin in inordinate desires. For "each one is tempted when he is lured and enticed by his own desires. Then when desire conceives, it gives birth to sin, and when sin is full grown, it gives birth to death" (James 1:14, 15). By "lust" is meant any fleshly desire or will; therefore when the apostle forbids gluttony and drunkenness, sexual immorality and lewdness, strife and envy, he strikes at the root of all in this one word, "make no provision for the flesh to arouse its desires" (or wills) (Romans 13:13, 14).

**Direction 12. Pull off the deceiving mask, and see that which you so eagerly desire as it is.**

What will it be to you at the last? It is now in its spring or summer; but see it in its fall and winter? It is now in its youth; but see it withered to skin and bone in its decrepit age. It is now in its clean and elaborate ornaments; but see it in its uncleanness and in its plain dress: cure your deception, and your desire is cured.

**Direction 13. Do not promise yourselves long life, but live as dying men, with your grave and burial shroud always in your eye; and it will cure your thirst after the creature when you are aware how short a time you must enjoy it, and especially how near you are to eternity.**

This is the apostle's method, 1 Corinthians 7:29–31, "And I say this, brothers and sisters: The time is short. So then those who have wives should be as those who have none, those with tears like those not weeping, those who rejoice like those not rejoicing, those who buy like those without possessions, those who use the world as though they were not using it to the full (or as if they did not use it). For the present shape of this world is passing away." So you will desire as if you did not desire, when you perceive well how quickly the thing desired will pass away.

**Direction 14. In all your desires, remember the account as well as the thing desired.**

Do not think only of what it is now at hand, but of what account you must make to God of it; "for to whom men give or commit much, of them they require the more" (Luke 12:48). Will you thirst after more power, more honor, more wealth, when you remember that you have the more to give account of? (Matthew 25). Have you not enough to reckon for already, unless you had hearts to use it better?

**Direction 15. Keep yourselves to the holy use of all your mercies, and do not let the flesh devour them, nor any inordinate appetite fare any the better for them when you have them, and this will powerfully extinguish the inordinate desire itself.**

We are in little danger of being overeager for things spiritual and holy, for the honor of God; resolve therefore that all you have shall be thus sanctified to God, and used for him, and not at all to satisfy any inordinate desire of the flesh, and then the flesh will cease its plea, when it finds it fares none the better for it. You are able to do much in this way if you will. If you cannot at once suppress the desire, you may at once resolve to deny the flesh the thing desired (as David would not drink the water though he longed for it, 2 Samuel 23:15, 17), and you may at once deny it the more of what you have. If you cannot refrain from your thirst, you can refrain from drinking; if you cannot refrain from being hungry, you can refrain from eating whatever is forbidden or unfit: if Eve must have an appetite for the forbidden fruit, yet she might have commanded her hands and teeth, and not have eaten it. If you cannot otherwise cool your desire for elaborate clothing, wear that which is somewhat plainer than you would otherwise have worn, on purpose to rebuke and control that desire: if you cannot otherwise quench your greedy desires, give so much the more to the poor to cross that desire. You cannot say that the outward act is out of your power, if you are only willing.

**Direction 16. When your desires are overeager, think of the mercies which you have received already and do possess.**

Has God done so much for you, and are you still calling for more, even of that which is unnecessary, when you should be giving thanks for what you have? This unthankful greediness is a hateful sin. Think what you have already for soul and body, estate and friends; and will not all this quiet you (even this with Christ and heaven) unless you have the other desire or fancy satisfied, and unless God humors you in your sick desires?

**Direction 17. Understand how little it will satisfy you, if God should give you all that you earnestly desire.**

When you have it, it will not quiet you, nor answer your expectations. You think it will make you happy, and be exceedingly sweet to you; but it deceives you, and you promise yourselves you do not know what, and therefore desire you do not know what. It would be to you only like a dream feast, which would leave you hungry in the morning (Isaiah 29:8).

**Direction 18. Remember always that the greatest hurt that the creature can do you is in being over-loved and desired, and it is never so dangerous to you as when it seems most desirable.**

If you remembered this rightly, you would be cast into the greatest fear and caution when anything below is presented very pleasing and desirable to you.

**Direction 19. Consider that your desires only make those wants a burden and misery to you which otherwise would be none.**

Thirst makes the lack of drink a torment, which to another is no pain or trouble at all. The lustful, wanton person is ready to die for love of the desired mate whom nobody else cares for, nor is ever the worse for being without. A proud, ambitious Haman thinks himself undone if he is not honored, and is vexed if he is only cast down into the humble condition of a farmer; when many thousand honest, contented men live merrily and quietly in as low a condition. It is men's own desires, and not their real wants, which torment them.

**Direction 20. Remember that when you have done all, if God loves you he will be the chooser, and will not grant your sick desires, but will correct you for them till they are cured.**

If your child cries for a knife, or for unwholesome food, or anything that would hurt him, you will quiet him with the rod if he does not stop. And it is a sign some rod of God is near you when you are sick for this, or that, or the other thing, and will not be quiet and content unless your fancy and concupiscence are humored.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). All NET wording was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Title:** "Title" is Baxter's word ("Tit.") for one of the ten divisions of Chapter 7. This file continues Title 3 (against sinful desires and discontent) and begins at the start of Direction 9; Title 3's heading and Directions 1–8 are in the previous file (not checked here).
- **Headings:** Baxter runs each Direction's heading sentence into the body. As in the finished files, the heading sentence is set in bold and the rest follows as a paragraph; no words were moved or dropped.
- **Romans 1 and 2 Thessalonians 2 (Direction 9, reference problem):** The source prints "Rom. i. 24, 26" after the first quotation, "ver. 28" after "God gave them up to vile affections," and "2 Thess. ii. 10–12" after "even as they did not like to retain God in their knowledge, God gave them over to a reprobate mind." The second quotation is actually Romans 1:26 and the third is Romans 1:28; 2 Thessalonians 2:10–12 (God "sends on them a deluding influence") is a parallel, not the source of the words. The references look displaced by one place, but since we are not sure how Baxter meant them, we kept them as printed. The quotations themselves are in NET wording (Romans 1:24, 1:26, 1:28). The NET "dishonorable passions" and "depraved mind" stand for the King James "vile affections" and "reprobate mind."
- **Psalm 81:12–13:** NET wording. Baxter cites verse 12 only, but his quotation runs into verse 13 ("Oh that my people had hearkened to me!"), so we cite 12–13. The King James "gave them up unto their own hearts' lust" is the NET "gave them over to their stubborn desires"; the King James "walked in their own counsels" is the NET "did what seemed right to them." The point Baxter leans on (being given up to one's own desires) survives.
- **Hosea 13:10, 11:** Baxter's "Israel a king, even in his anger" is his third-person adaptation of the King James "I gave thee a king in mine anger" (verse 11). Kept in his wording; the NET reads "I granted you a king in my anger."
- **Psalm 78:27, 29–31 (Baxter's blend):** Baxter strings together King James phrases from verses 29, 27, 30, and 31 in his own order, so we kept his wording, modernized ("flesh" to "meat," "fowls" to "birds," "lust" to "craving," "meat" to "food," "slew" to "killed"), in his quotation marks. The NET differs in places Baxter leans on: verse 30 "They were not yet filled up" has nothing of the King James "they were not estranged from their lust," and verse 31 has "He killed some of the strongest of them" where the King James has "slew the fattest of them." NET for reference: "He rained down meat on them like dust, birds as numerous as the sand on the seashores ... he gave them what they desired. They were not yet filled up; their food was still in their mouths when the anger of God flared up against them. He killed some of the strongest of them."
- **Psalm 106:14, 15 (important):** NET wording. The King James ends "he gave them their request; but sent leanness into their soul," which is the climax Baxter is aiming at: granted desires starve the soul. The NET reads "then struck them with a disease," which makes the punishment bodily, and so loses the spiritual "leanness" his point rests on. Flagged. The King James "lusted exceedingly ... and tempted God" is the NET "had an insatiable craving for meat ... challenged God."
- **Judges 9:15:** Baxter's "Let a fire come out of the bramble and devour you" adapts Jotham's fable (the King James "let fire come out of the bramble, and devour the cedars of Lebanon"), applying it to the reader. Kept in his wording. The NET reads "may fire blaze from the thornbush and consume the cedars of Lebanon!"
- **James 1:14, 15 and Baxter's gloss on "lust" (Direction 11):** NET wording. The King James has "drawn away of his own lust ... when lust hath conceived"; Baxter then explains, "By 'lust' is meant any fleshly desire or will." We kept his word "lust" in that gloss because he is explaining the King James word. The NET already reads "desires" and "desire," so in the NET the gloss confirms what the reader sees rather than correcting a narrow reading.
- **Romans 13:13, 14 (Direction 11):** NET wording for verse 14. Baxter's "(or wills)" is his own gloss on the word "lusts," kept. The King James "to fulfil the lusts thereof" is the NET "to arouse its desires": the King James speaks of satisfying desires, the NET of stirring them up, which shifts the sense slightly from Baxter's "satisfy." Flagged. Baxter's list of vices ("gluttony and drunkenness, chambering and wantonness, strife and envying") is in his own prose, not quotation marks, so it was modernized as prose: "chambering" to "sexual immorality," "wantonness" to "lewdness," "envying" to "envy." His "gluttony" probably stands for the King James "rioting"; the NET has "carousing" (NET verse 13: "not in carousing and drunkenness, not in sexual immorality and sensuality, not in discord and jealousy").
- **1 Corinthians 7:29–31:** NET wording. Baxter's parenthesis "(or as if they used it not)" is his gloss on the King James "as not abusing it"; we kept it, modernized, after the NET phrase "as though they were not using it to the full," which already comes close to his gloss. The King James "the fashion of this world passeth away" is the NET "the present shape of this world is passing away." Baxter's following sentence, "desire as if you did not desire," echoes the King James wording of these verses ("as though they wept not").
- **Luke 12:48:** Baxter's "for to whom men give or commit much, of them they require the more" combines the two halves of the King James verse; kept in his wording as a paraphrase. NET: "From everyone who has been given much, much will be required, and from the one who has been entrusted with much, even more will be asked."
- **Cited only:** Matthew 25 (the parable of the talents, probably); 2 Samuel 23:15, 17 (David and the water from Bethlehem); Isaiah 29:8 (the hungry man dreaming that he eats).
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): Eve and the forbidden fruit (Genesis 3:6); Haman, who is vexed while one man will not honor him (Esther 5:13); the rod of correction (compare Proverbs 13:24; 22:15; Hebrews 12:6).
- **concupiscence:** kept as Baxter's technical word; he defines it himself in Direction 11 as "sinful desire."
- **lust** in Baxter's own prose (Direction 9 "Follow your own lust"; Direction 16 "the other lust or fancy") was rendered "desire," since in his usage it means desire generally, not only sexual desire (he says so in Direction 11). In Direction 19 "The lustful wanton" became "The lustful, wanton person."
- **affections, affection** (Direction 10): kept; see GLOSSARY.md (emotions and desires).
- **the creature** (Directions 13, 18): Baxter's term for any created thing, as opposed to God. Kept.
- **mercies** (Directions 15, 16): blessings received from God. Kept.
- **wants** (Direction 19): kept in the sense of "lacks" (things one is without); "the want of drink" became "the lack of drink."
- **Other word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): Take heed, Be careful; witty, clever; Try, Test; bid, told; spare not, do not hold back; Nay, Indeed; Q., Question; vizor, mask; curious (ornaments, apparel), elaborate; homely, plain; homelier, plainer; deceit, deception; decrepid, decrepit; winding-sheet, burial shroud; sensible, aware; presently, at once; suit, plea; forbear, refrain from; must needs, must; apparel, clothing; covetous, greedy; bethink you of, think of; odious, hateful; humour, humor; dreaming feast, dream feast; still (Direction 18), always; aright, rightly; mean (condition), humble; meat (Direction 20), food; give not over, does not stop.
- **farmer** (Direction 19): kept. In Baxter's day the word could mean a tenant who rents land, a fairly low station, which fits his contrast with Haman's ambition; we have not confirmed which sense he intends.
- **Direction 12 "but see it in its fall and winter?":** the question mark is Baxter's, kept.
