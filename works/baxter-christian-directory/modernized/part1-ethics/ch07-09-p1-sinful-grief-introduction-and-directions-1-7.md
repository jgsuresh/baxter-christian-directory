# Chapter 7: Title 9, against sinful grief and trouble (introduction and Directions 1-7)

**Title 9. Directions against sinful grief and trouble.**

Sorrow is planted in nature to make man a subject capable of government, by making him capable of punishment, that he might be kept from sin by the fear or sense of that which nature has made its punishment: and that the beginnings of pain might help to prevent the sin that would bring more; and might drive the wounded soul to its remedy; or by sympathy might grieve with the misery of others.

Sorrow or grief, in itself considered, is neither morally good nor evil; but it is a natural passion; and evil, that is, hurtful, to him that has it; but good, that is, a fit, conducive means to the universal or higher ends of government to which the Creator and Universal King has planted it in man: the same may be said of all capacity of pain and natural misery.

Mere sorrow, in itself considered, is a thing that God does not command, nor take pleasure in. Sorrow for our natural or penal hurt is in itself no duty, but a necessary thing. God does not command it, but threatens it; therefore there is no moral good in it. God will not command or entreat men to feel when they are hurt, or mourn under their torment; but will make them do it whether they will or not: therefore humble souls must beware of thinking they merit or please God merely by sorrowing for their sufferings. But yet sorrow for misery may accidentally become a duty and a moral good. 1. *Ratione principii*, by respect to the principle it proceeds from. As when it is, (1.) The belief of God's threatenings which causes the sorrow. (2.) When it comes from a love to God. 2. *Ratione materiae*, for the matter's sake, when it is the absence of God, and his favor, and his Spirit, and image, which is the misery that we lament (which therefore has the savor of some love to God); and not mere fleshly, sensory suffering. 3. *Ratione finis*, in respect of the end; when we sorrow with intent to drive our hearts to Christ our Savior, and to value mercy and grace, and to recover us to God. 4. *Ratione effecti*, in respect of the effect, when these aforementioned ends become the fruits of it. [358]

Sorrow for sin is a duty and moral good. 1. Formally in itself considered: for to be sorrowful for offending God, and violating his law, essentially contains a will to please God and obey his law. 2. It must be also made good by a good principle; that is, by faith and love. 3. By a right end; that it be to carry us from the sin to God. 4. And by a right guide and matter; that it be sin indeed, and not a mistaken, seeming sin, that is what we sorrow for. But sorrow for sin (materially) may be made sinful, 1. By an ill end and formal reason; when we mourn not for sin as sin, but as one sin hinders another, or as it marred some ill design. 2. And by the effect; when it does but sink men in despair, or torment them, and not at all separate them from the sin. 3. When it comes not at all from any love to God, or care to please him, but only an unwillingness to be damned, and so it is lamented only as a means of damnation; which, though it be a sorrow positively neither good nor evil, yet it is evil privatively.

But it is the passion of grief as in its excess that I am now to speak against. And it is in excess, 1. When we grieve for that which we ought not at all to grieve for; that is, either for some good, or for a thing indifferent that is neither good nor bad; both which come from the error of the mind. 2. When we grieve too much for that which we may grieve for lawfully in some measure; that is, for our own afflictions or penal suffering. 3. When we grieve too much for that which we are bound to grieve for in some measure: as (1.) For our sin. (2.) For our loss of the favor of God, or of his grace and Spirit. (3.) For other men's sin and suffering. (4.) For the sufferings of the church, and calamities of the world. (5.) For God's dishonor. [359]

Though it is not easy to have too much sorrow for sin, considering it estimatively; that is, we can hardly take sin for a worse evil than it is, and accordingly grieve for it; yet it is often too easy to have too much sorrow for sin, or any other evil, intensively as to the greatness of the passion. And thus sorrow for sin is too great, 1. When it distracts the mind, and overturns reason, and it is made unfit for the ends of sorrow. 2. When it so clouds and clothes the soul in grief that it makes us unfit to see and consider the promise, to relish mercy, or believe it; to acknowledge benefits, or own grace received, or be thankful for it; to feel the love of God, or love him for it, to praise him, or to be mindful of him, or to call upon him; when it drives the soul from God, and weakens it to duty, and teaches it to deny mercy, and sinks it towards despair; all this is too much and sinful sorrow: and so is all that does the soul more hurt than good; for sorrow is not good of itself, but as it does good, or shows good.

**Direction 1. Keep your hearts as true and close to God as possible, and make sure of his love, that you may know you have not an unregenerate, miserable soul to mourn for, and then all other grief is the more curable and more tolerable.**

Be once able to say that God is on your side, that Christ, and the Spirit, and heaven are yours, and then you have the greatest cordial against excessive grief that this world affords. If you say, How should this be done? I answer that it is explained in its proper place. No wonder if sorrow overwhelms that soul that is in the chains of sin, under the curse of God, as soon as awakened conscience comes to feel it. And it is most miserable when it has the smallest sorrow; there being some hope that sorrow may drive it home to Christ. Therefore if you have been a secure, unhumbled, carnal wretch, and God is now beginning to humble you, by showing you your sin and misery, be careful, as you love your soul, that you do not drive away necessary, healing sorrow and repentance, under the pretense of driving away melancholy or overmuch sorrow; your pain tends to your hopes of cure.

**Direction 2. Do not renew the wounds of conscience by renewed, willful, gross sin.**

For sin will bring sorrow, especially if you have any life of grace to feel it: even as falls, and breaking the bones, bring pain. Obey carefully if you would have peace.

**Direction 3. Be well acquainted with the general grounds of hope in the mercy of God, the office and death of Christ, and the free, universal offer of pardon, grace, and life in the new covenant.**

Abundance of grief dwells in many humbled souls through the ignorance of these general grounds of comfort; which would vanish away if these were known.

**Direction 4. Know well the true nature and use of godly sorrow; how it is but a means to higher grace, and a thing which may exceed, and not a thing that we should stop in, or think we can never have too much of it.**

Desire it but in its place, and to its proper ends.

**Direction 5. Know well the nature and excellency of those higher graces which sorrow tends to; even love, and thankfulness, and delight in God, and fruitful obedience.**

And then you will be carried after these, and will learn to hate the sorrow that hinders them, and to cherish that sorrow which leads you up to them, and to value it but as a means to them.

**Direction 6. Manage all your affairs, especially those of your souls, with prudent foresight; and do not look only on things as they appear at hand.**

[360]

Do not judge by sense, but by reason; for sense cannot foresee, but pleases itself at present with that which must be bitterness in the end. Thus, carnal delight is the common way to overwhelming sorrow. He that would not have the pain and sickness of overeating tomorrow must not please his appetite against reason today. Poison will cramp and kill none the less for tasting sweet. You must foreknow how that which you take will work, and what will be the effects of it, and not only how it tastes, if you would escape the pain. The drunkard does not think of his vomiting, and poverty, or shame, or sickness, and therefore causes them. There is no sorrow so intolerable as that of a guilty soul that is passing in terror to the bar of God, and from there to everlasting pain. Foresee this sorrow in your most pleasant sin; and remember that when you are tempted to sin, you are tempted to sorrow; and then you may prevent it. And in all your particular actions use a foreseeing judgment, and ask what is likely to be the end, before you enter on the beginning. Most of our sorrows come for want of this, and express themselves by, Had I known, or had I thought of this, I would have prevented it. Do nothing which you may foresee must be repented of; for repentance is sorrowful; and the weightier the case the deeper the sorrow. How easy and comfortable a life and death might men attain, if they would not buy a little forbidden, poisonous pleasure with the price of future pain and sorrows! And if they did not foolishly and over-tenderly refuse those holy, necessary, medicinal sorrows, by which their greater, overwhelming, and undoing sorrows should have been prevented!

**Direction 7. Look always on your remedy when you look on your misery; and when you find any dangerous sin or sign in you, at once consider what is your duty in order to your recovery and escape.**

It is an ordinary thing with peevish, disordered natures, when they are reproved for any sin, to resist the reproof by excuses as long as they can; and when they can resist no longer, then they fall into despairing lamentations, if they are so bad, what then shall they do! and in the meantime never set themselves against the sin, and cast it off and return to their obedience, that their comforts may return: they will do anything rather than amend. The reason why God convicts them of sin is that they may forsake it, and they are sooner brought to anything than to this: convict them of their pride, or malice, or worldliness, or disobedience, or slothfulness, or passion, and they will sooner sink in sorrow and despair than they will set about a resolved reformation. This is indeed what the devil desires; he can allow you grief and desperation, but not to amend. But is this best for you? Or is it pleasing to God? Do not deny your sin, but see also that there is enough in Christ for your pardon and deliverance. He has appointed you means for your present recovery, and he is ready to help you. Ask what is your duty for your cure, and set about it without delay.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[358] Even sorrow that does no good may testify a just affection. It is said by Laertius that when Solon was reproved for mourning for his son, with a Nihil proficis; he answered, At propter hoc ipsum illachrymor, quia nihil proficio. (English: reproved with a "You accomplish nothing [by it]"; he answered, "But I weep for this very reason, because I accomplish nothing." Diogenes Laertius, life of Solon.)

[359] That very old book of Hermes, called "Pastor," notably shows how much grief and heaviness is an enemy to Christianity and the Spirit of God.

[360] Pittaci sententia fuit, prudentiam virorum esse prius-quam adversa contingant, providere ne veniant: fortium vero, cum illa contigerint, æquo animo ferre. Laert. in Pittac. (English: It was the saying of Pittacus that it belongs to the prudence of men, before adversities happen, to provide that they do not come; but to the brave, when they have happened, to bear them with an even mind. Laertius, in his life of Pittacus.)

## Translator's notes

- **Title:** "Tit." is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," his name for each of the ten main divisions of Chapter 7 ("Directions for the Government of the Passions"). This file begins Title 9, "against sinful grief and trouble" (source line 36608); nothing of Title 8 falls in this file.
- **Direction headings:** Baxter runs each Direction's opening sentence and its body together; following the finished files, the opening sentence is set in bold and the rest follows as body text. In Direction 6, the footnote marker [360] falls at the end of the bold heading sentence, as in the source.
- **Scripture:** no verse is quoted in this range, so no NET text was needed. Unmarked allusions (kept in Baxter's wording): "God is on your side" (compare Psalm 118:6; Romans 8:31); "God convicts them of sin" (compare John 16:8); "the bar of God" (compare 2 Corinthians 5:10). These references are ours, not Baxter's.
- **passion, passions:** Baxter's word for the emotions and affections; kept. In Direction 7's list of sins, "passion" means a hot, angry temper.
- **melancholy** (Direction 1): kept as Baxter's word; see GLOSSARY.md.
- **Latin labels** (*Ratione principii*, *materiae*, *finis*, *effecti*): kept, since Baxter glosses each in English himself ("in respect of the principle / the matter / the end / the effect"). The source prints *materiæ*; we print *materiae*.
- **accidentally:** kept. It is the scholastic sense (per accidens): not by its own nature, but by something joined to it.
- **positively ... privatively; estimatively ... intensively; formally ... materially:** kept as Baxter's scholastic terms. "Privatively" means evil by what it lacks (here, the lack of love to God) rather than by anything it positively is. "Estimatively" means in our judgment of how great an evil sin is; "intensively" means in the strength of the felt passion.
- **penal:** suffering that comes as punishment; kept.
- **secure** (Direction 1): Baxter's technical sense, carelessly confident, unconcerned about one's spiritual state; kept.
- **carnal:** unspiritual, ruled by the flesh; kept.
- **cordial** (Direction 1): kept. In Baxter's day a cordial was a medicine or drink to strengthen and revive the heart.
- **Word swaps:** condole → grieve with; apt, conducible → fit, conducive; savoureth of → has the savor of; sensitive → sensory; forementioned → aforementioned; take heed → beware / be careful; opened → explained; no marvel → no wonder; smart → pain; pretence → pretense; surfeit → overeating; gripe (of poison, to cause griping pains in the bowels) → cramp; never the less → none the less; thence → from there; like to be → likely to be; I had prevented it → I would have prevented it; presently → at once; distempered → disordered; convinceth / convince (of sin) → convicts / convict; set upon → set about; withal → also; commandeth not / thinketh not / judge not / look not / renew not / deny not → does not command / does not think / do not judge / do not look / do not renew / do not deny; mind him → be mindful of him; consider of → consider; profiteth not (note 358) → does no good.
- **own grace received** (introduction): "own" in the sense of acknowledge; kept.
- **Hermes, "Pastor"** (note 359): this is probably the early Christian work usually called *The Shepherd* of Hermas (Latin *Pastor*); Baxter's "Hermes" is his spelling. We have not checked which passage he has in mind.
- **Solon and Pittacus** (notes 358, 360): both are in Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of the Philosophers*; we have not checked Baxter's citations against an edition.
