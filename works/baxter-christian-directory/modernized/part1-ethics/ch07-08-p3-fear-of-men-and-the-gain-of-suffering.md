# Chapter 7: Title 8, against sinful fear of men (Direction 4 continued, Directions 5-8)

*This section begins partway through Direction 4 against sinful fear of men, at a margin objection.*

*In the margin: Objection: We fear them only as God's instruments. Answered.*

I know you will say that it is only as God's instruments that you fear them, and that if you were certain of his favor, and were not first afraid of his wrath, you would not fear the wrath of men. *Answer.* By this you may see then what it is to be disobedient, and to cherish your fears of God's displeasure, and to hinder your own assurance of his love, when this must be the cause of, or the pretext for, so many other sins. But if really you fear them but as the instruments of God's displeasure; 1. Why then did you no more fear his displeasure before, when the danger from men did not appear? you know God never lacks instruments to execute his wrath or will. 2. And why do you not fear the sin which displeases him more than the instruments, when they could do you no hurt were it not for sin? 3. And why do you not more fear them as tempters than as afflicters? and consequently why do you not fear their flatteries, and enticements, and promotions, and your prosperity, more than adversity, when prosperity more draws you away to sin? 4. And why do you not fear hell more than anything that man can do against you, when God threatens hell more than human penalties? 5. And why do you not apply yourselves to God chiefly for deliverance, but study how to pacify man? why do you with more fear, and care, and diligence, and compliance, apply yourselves to those that you are afraid of, if you fear God more than them? Repent and make your peace with God through Christ, and then be quiet, if it is God that you are afraid of: your business then is not first with the creature, but with God. 6. And if you fear them only as God's instruments, why does your fear not make you the more cautiously flee from further guilt, but rather make you think of stretching your consciences as far as ever you dare, and venturing as far as you dare upon God's displeasure, to escape man's? Are these signs that you fear them only as the instruments of God's displeasure? or do you see how deceitful a thing your heart is? Indeed man is to be feared in a full subordination to God, 1. As his officers, commanding us to obey him; 2. As his executioners, punishing us for disobeying him; 3. But not as Satan's instruments, (by God's permission,) afflicting us for obeying him, or undeservedly. Romans 13:3–4, "For rulers cause no fear for good conduct but for bad. Do you desire not to fear authority? Do good and you will receive its commendation because it is God's servant for your well-being. But be afraid if you do wrong because government does not bear the sword for nothing. It is God's servant to administer punishment on the person who does wrong." Would you have the fuller exposition of this? It is in 1 Peter 3:10–17, "For the one who wants to love life and see good days must keep his tongue from evil and his lips from uttering deceit. And he must turn away from evil and do good; he must seek peace and pursue it. For the eyes of the Lord are upon the righteous and his ears are open to their prayer. But the Lord's face is against those who do evil. For who is going to harm you if you are devoted to what is good? But in fact, if you happen to suffer for doing what is right, you are blessed. But do not be terrified of them or be shaken. But set Christ apart as Lord in your hearts and always be ready to give an answer to anyone who asks about the hope you possess. Yet do it with courtesy and respect, keeping a good conscience, so that those who slander your good conduct in Christ may be put to shame when they accuse you. For it is better to suffer for doing good, if God wills it, than for doing evil." See also 1 Peter 4:13–15. [347]

**Direction 5. Either you fear suffering from men as guilty or as innocent; for evil-doing, or for well-doing, or for nothing.**

If as guilty and for evil-doing, turn your fears the right way, and fear God, and his wrath for sin, and his threatenings of more than men can inflict; and acknowledge the goodness of justice both from God and man: but if it is as innocent or for well-doing, remember that Christ commands you exceedingly to rejoice; and remember that martyrs have the most glorious crown: and will you be excessively afraid of your highest honor, and gain, and joy? Believe well what Christ has said, and you cannot be much afraid of suffering for him. Matthew 5:10–12, "Blessed are those who are persecuted for righteousness, for the kingdom of heaven belongs to them. Blessed are you when people insult you and persecute you and say all kinds of evil things about you falsely on account of me. Rejoice and be glad because your reward is great in heaven, for they persecuted the prophets before you in the same way." [348] And will you fear the way of blessedness and exceeding joy? Matthew 10:17–19, "Beware of people, because they will hand you over to councils and flog you in their synagogues. And you will be brought before governors and kings because of me, as a witness to them ... do not worry," etc. You are allowed to beware of them, but not to be over-fearful or anxious about the matter. Verses 22–23, "And you will be hated by everyone because of my name. But the one who endures to the end will be saved! Whenever they persecute you in one town, flee to another!" Flee, but do not fear them, with any immoderate fear: verse 39, "Whoever finds his life will lose it, and whoever loses his life because of me will find it." Luke 18:29–30, "I tell you the truth, there is no one who has left home or wife or brothers or parents or children for the sake of God's kingdom who will not receive many times more in this age—and in the age to come, eternal life." Can you believe all this, and yet be so afraid of your own happiness? Oh how much secret unbelief is detected by our immoderate fears! 1 Peter 4:12–16, 19, "Dear friends, do not be astonished that a trial by fire is occurring among you, as though something strange were happening to you. But rejoice in the degree that you have shared in the sufferings of Christ, so that when his glory is revealed you may also rejoice and be glad. If you are insulted for the name of Christ, you are blessed, because the Spirit of glory, who is the Spirit of God, rests on you. But let none of you suffer as ... [a] criminal ... But if you suffer as a Christian, ... glorify God that you bear such a name. ... So then let those who suffer according to the will of God entrust their souls to a faithful Creator as they do good." There is scarcely any point that God has been pleased to be more full in, in the holy Scriptures, than the encouraging of his suffering servants against the fears of men; informing them that their sufferings are the occasion of their profit and exceeding joy; and therefore not of too great fear.

**Direction 6. Experience tells us that men have never so much joy on earth as in suffering for the cause of Christ; nor so much honor as by being dishonored by men for him.**

How joyfully did the ancient Christians go to martyrdom! many of them lamented that they could not attain it: and what comfort have Christ's confessors found, above what they could ever attain before! [349] and how honorable now are the names and memorials of those martyrs, who died then under the slanders, scorn, and cruelty of men! Even the papists that bloodily make more, do yet honor the names of the ancient martyrs with keeping holy days for them, and magnifying their shrines and relics; for God will have it so, for the honor of his holy sufferers, that even that same generation that persecutes the living saints shall honor the dead, and they that murder those they find alive shall honor those whom their forefathers murdered: Matthew 23:29–31, "Woe to you, experts in the law and you Pharisees, hypocrites! You build tombs for the prophets and decorate the graves of the righteous. And you say, 'If we had lived in the days of our ancestors, we would not have participated with them in shedding the blood of the prophets.'" Comfort and honor attend the pain and shame of the cross. Acts 5:41, "So they left the council rejoicing because they had been considered worthy to suffer dishonor for the sake of the name." Acts 16:25, "Paul and Silas sang praises to God at midnight in the prison and stocks," when their backs were sore with lashes. It is written of some of the Christians that were imprisoned by Julian, that they would not refrain in the emperor's hearing as he passed by, from singing, "Let God arise, and his enemies shall be scattered."

**Direction 7. Love better the holy image of God upon your souls, and then you will be glad of the great helps to holiness which sufferings do afford.**

Who does not find that adversity is more safe and profitable to the soul than prosperity? especially that adversity which Christ is engaged to bless to his servants, as being undergone for him? Romans 5:3–5, "We also rejoice in sufferings, knowing that suffering produces endurance, and endurance, character, and character, hope. And hope does not disappoint." God disciplines us "for our benefit, that we may share his holiness. Now all discipline seems painful at the time, not joyful. But later it produces the fruit of peace and righteousness for those trained by it," Hebrews 12:10–11. Moses "regarded abuse suffered for Christ to be greater wealth than the treasures of Egypt": and therefore chose "rather to be ill-treated with the people of God than to enjoy sin's fleeting pleasure," Hebrews 11:25–26. It is but "for a short time" that "you may have to suffer ... in various trials," that "the proven character of your faith, which is much more valuable than gold—gold that is tested by fire, even though it is passing away— ... will bring praise and glory and honor when Jesus Christ is revealed," 1 Peter 1:6–7. Who is it that knows himself, that does not feel a need of some afflictions? to awake us from our drowsiness, and enliven us from our dullness, and refine us from our dross, and wean us from the world, and help us to mortify the flesh, and save us from the deceits of sin?

**Direction 8. Remember that sufferings are the ordinary way to heaven.**

Love heaven better, and your sufferings will seem lighter, and your fear of them will be less. [350] Christ has resolved on it, that "if anyone comes to him, and does not hate his father, and mother, and wife, and children, and brothers, and sisters, yes, and his own life also, he cannot be his disciple: and whoever does not bear his cross and follow him cannot be his disciple: and whoever he is of you that does not forsake all that he has, he cannot be his disciple," Luke 14:26, 27, 33. "In the world we shall have tribulation, but peace in him," John 16:33. "We must enter the kingdom of God through many persecutions," Acts 14:22. "If indeed we suffer with him so we may also be glorified with him. For I consider that our present sufferings cannot even be compared to the coming glory that will be revealed to us," Romans 8:17–18. "This is why we work hard and struggle, because we have set our hope on the living God," 1 Timothy 4:10. In preaching the gospel, Paul says, he "suffer[s] hardship to the point of imprisonment as a criminal, but God's message is not imprisoned!" 2 Timothy 2:9. "I suffer as I do," he says, "but I am not ashamed because I know the one in whom my faith is set and I am convinced that he is able to protect what has been entrusted to me until that day," 2 Timothy 1:12. "Now in fact all who want to live godly lives in Christ Jesus will be persecuted," 2 Timothy 3:12. Our patience in sufferings is the joy of our friends, and therefore they are not too much to be feared. 2 Thessalonians 1:4–5, "As a result we ourselves boast about you in the churches of God for your perseverance and faith in all the persecutions and afflictions you are enduring. This is evidence of God's righteous judgment, to make you worthy of the kingdom of God, for which in fact you are suffering." [351] Therefore take the conclusion of all from God, Revelation 2:10, "Do not be afraid of the things you are about to suffer. The devil is about to have some of you thrown into prison so you may be tested, and you will experience suffering for ten days. Remain faithful even to the point of death, and I will give you the crown that is life itself." Philippians 1:28–30, "And by not being intimidated in any way by your opponents. This is a sign of their destruction, but of your salvation—a sign which is from God. For it has been granted to you not only to believe in Christ but also to suffer for him." And shall we fear so great a gift?

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[347] When Socrates' wife, lamenting him, said, Injustè morieris: he answered, An tu juste malles? Laert. in Socrat. ("You will die unjustly." "Would you rather [I died] justly?" Laertius, on Socrates.)

[348] The seven brothers that suffered in Africa under Hunnericus, Incedebant cum fiducia ad supplicium quasi ad epulas, decantantes, Gloria Deo in excelsis, &c. Votiva nobis hæc est dies, et omni solennitate festivior. Ecce nunc tempus acceptabile, ecce nunc dies est salutis, quando pro fide nunc domini dei nostri perferimus præparatum supplicium, ne amittamus acquisitæ fidei intumentum. Sed et populi publica voce clamabant: Ne timeatis populi Dei, neque formidetis minas atque terrores præsentium tribulationum, sed moriamur pro Christo, ut ipse mortuus est, redimens nos pretioso sanguine salutari. Victor. Uticens. p. 368. (They went to their punishment with confidence as if to a feast, singing, "Glory to God in the highest," etc. "This is a day we have longed for, and more festive than any solemn feast. Behold, now is the acceptable time, behold, now is the day of salvation, when for the faith of the Lord our God we endure the punishment prepared, so that we may not lose the garment of the faith we have gained." But the people also cried out with a public voice: "Do not fear, people of God, nor dread the threats and terrors of the present tribulations, but let us die for Christ, as he himself died, redeeming us with his precious, saving blood." Victor Uticensis, p. 368. The source prints "intumentum," probably a misprint for "indumentum," "garment.") In Paulo quinque gloriationes observavi. Gloriatur in imbecillitate, in cruce Christi, in bona conscientia; in afflictionibus, in spe vitæ æternæ. Bucholtzer. (In Paul I have observed five kinds of glorying. He glories in weakness, in the cross of Christ, in a good conscience; in afflictions, in the hope of eternal life. Bucholtzer.)

[349] Idololatria tam altas in mundo egit radices, ut non possit extirpari: ideo optimum est confiteri et pati. Bucholtzer. (Idolatry has driven such deep roots in the world that it cannot be rooted out: therefore it is best to confess and to suffer. Bucholtzer.) Victor Uticensis says that Gensericus commanded that when Masculinus came to die, if he were fearful, they should execute him, that he might die with shame, but if he were constant, they should refrain, lest he should have the honor of a glorious martyrdom. And so his boldness saved his life. Et si martyrem invidus hostis noluit facere confessorem, tamen non potuit violare. (And though the envious enemy would not make him a martyr, yet he could not violate the confessor. Rendered loosely: the enemy could not take from him his standing as a confessor.)

[350] Anacharsis (in Laertio) percontanti quædam esset securissima navis: ea inquit, quæ in portum venerit: in heaven we shall be quiet from all these tumults. (Anacharsis [in Laertius], to one asking which ship was safest, said: "The one that has come into port." The source prints "quædam," apparently a misprint for "quænam," "which.")

[351] Ingenii philosophici est ex inimicorum odio decerpere aliquid quod vertat in suum bonum. Paul. Scalig. p. 728. (It is the mark of a philosophical mind to pluck from the hatred of enemies something that it may turn to its own good. Paul. Scalig., p. 728.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). All NET wording quoted here was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Where this section begins:** the range opens inside Direction 4 against sinful fear of men ("Remember that men as well as devils are chained..."), which began in the previous file, so no new Direction heading is given. "Title" is Baxter's word for each of the ten divisions of Chapter 7 (this is Title 8, against sinful fear).
- **Direction headings:** Baxter's Directions are run-in paragraphs; we set the first sentence of each in bold as its heading and the rest as the body, without changing any words.
- **Romans 13:3–4 (flag):** NET wording. The NET prints verse 3's opening clause in parentheses (it closes a sentence begun in verse 2); we dropped the parentheses and capitalized "For." The King James calls the ruler "the minister of God ... a revenger to execute wrath," a person ("he"); the NET speaks of "authority" and "government" as "it." Baxter's point, that man is to be feared as God's officer and executioner, still stands, but the NET is less personal.
- **1 Peter 3:10–17 (corrected reference; flags):** Baxter prints "1 Pet. iii. 10-15," but his quotation runs through verse 17 ("Having a good conscience..." is verse 16; "For it is better..." is verse 17). We cite 3:10–17. NET differences: verse 14, King James "be not afraid of their terror" is "do not be terrified of them," close to Baxter's sense; verse 15, King James "sanctify the Lord God in your hearts" is "set Christ apart as Lord in your hearts," following a different Greek reading; verse 16, "with meekness and fear" becomes "with courtesy and respect," so the word "fear," which touches Baxter's subject, is gone.
- **1 Peter 4:13–15; Philippians 1:28–30; Matthew 23:29–31:** Baxter's references are kept as printed. In the last two he cites one verse more than he quotes (he quotes Philippians 1:28–29 and Matthew 23:29–30); we quote only what he quotes. 1 Peter 4:13–15 is cited only.
- **Matthew 10:17–19 (flag):** NET wording, with "..." where Baxter's "--" skips to "take no thought." Baxter then says "not to be over-fearful or thoughtful of the matter," where "thoughtful" means anxious; we render it **anxious**, which matches the NET's "do not worry." The NET's "as a witness to them" (it continues "and to the Gentiles," where Baxter's quotation stops) stands where the King James has "for a testimony against them."
- **Matthew 10:22–23; 10:39; Matthew 5:10–12; Luke 18:29–30:** NET wording. Baxter's "Fly, but fear them not" is rendered **Flee, but do not fear them**, echoing the NET's "flee to another." Luke 18:30 "in this present time" is NET "in this age."
- **1 Peter 4:12–16, 19:** NET wording, with "..." where Baxter's "--" marks a skip. In verse 15 Baxter quotes only "as an evil-doer"; the NET has "a murderer or thief or criminal or as a troublemaker," so we quote only "[a] criminal." In verse 16 he skips "let him not be ashamed."
- **Acts 5:41:** NET wording. Baxter's "for his name" is NET "for the sake of the name."
- **Acts 16:25 (paraphrase):** Baxter's "Paul and Silas sang praises to God at midnight in the prison and stocks" is his own blend of verses 24 (the stocks) and 25; we kept his wording, modernized. NET: "About midnight Paul and Silas were praying and singing hymns to God" (16:25); "fastened their feet in the stocks" (16:24).
- **"Let God arise, and his enemies shall be scattered" (flag):** Psalm 68:1 (the reference is not in Baxter's text). These are the words Baxter reports the imprisoned Christians singing, so we kept the King James wording he gives. The NET reads "God springs into action. His enemies scatter," a statement rather than the prayer "Let God arise," which would change what the singers are reported to have sung. Julian is the emperor Julian (fourth century), who turned back to paganism. The story is Baxter's report; he names no source and we have not traced it.
- **Romans 5:3–5 (corrected reference; flag):** Baxter prints "Rom. x. 3-5," but the words are Romans 5:3–5; Romans 10:3–5 does not contain them. NET "rejoice in sufferings" stands for King James "glory in tribulation"; "hope does not disappoint" for "hope maketh not ashamed"; the King James "experience" (proof by testing) is the NET's "character." The NET verse 3 begins "Not only this, but"; Baxter's quotation starts after that.
- **Hebrews 12:10–11:** NET wording. Baxter's "chasteneth us" stands outside the quotation as **disciplines us**, since the NET verse has "he does so." The NET's "the fruit of peace and righteousness" differs slightly from the King James "the peaceable fruit of righteousness."
- **Hebrews 11:25–26 (flag):** Baxter quotes verse 26 then verse 25, joined by his own "and therefore." We kept his order and his "and therefore ... chose" outside the quotation marks (the NET has "choosing"), and quoted the NET for the rest. His "the very reproach of Christ" is NET "abuse suffered for Christ," so his emphatic "very" is lost; "the pleasures of sin for a season" is NET "sin's fleeting pleasure."
- **1 Peter 1:6–7 (flag):** Baxter's "now for a season, and if need be, that we are in heaviness through manifold temptations" leans on "if need be": suffering is sometimes needed. The NET reads "you may have to suffer for a short time in various trials," which keeps that sense only loosely in "may have to." The NET orders the words differently from the King James, so we fitted NET phrases into Baxter's sentence ("It is but ... that ... that ..."), with "..." for skipped words. NET "trials" stands for the King James "temptations." Baxter puts the verse in the first person plural ("that we are in heaviness"); the NET's "you" is kept inside the quotation marks, so his "we" is lost.
- **Luke 14:26, 27, 33 (paraphrase):** Baxter turns Christ's first person ("my disciple") into the third person ("his disciple"), so we kept his wording, modernized. NET: "If anyone comes to me and does not hate his own father and mother, and wife and children, and brothers and sisters, and even his own life, he cannot be my disciple. Whoever does not carry his own cross and follow me cannot be my disciple. ... not one of you can be my disciple if he does not renounce all his own possessions."
- **John 16:33 (paraphrase):** Baxter's "In the world we shall have tribulation, but peace in him" is his own summary in the first person plural; kept. NET: "I have told you these things so that in me you may have peace. In the world you have trouble and suffering, but take courage—I have conquered the world."
- **Acts 14:22; Romans 8:17–18:** NET wording. Baxter's "much tribulation" is NET "many persecutions"; King James "revealed in us" is NET "revealed to us."
- **1 Timothy 4:10 (important flag):** Baxter quotes the King James, "we both labour and suffer reproach," as a proof that sufferings are the ordinary way to heaven. The NET reads "we work hard and struggle," following a different Greek reading, so "reproach" (being insulted for the faith), which is Baxter's point, is gone. The NET also has "have set our hope" for "trust." The NET verse begins "In fact"; Baxter's "Therefore" corresponds to "this is why."
- **2 Timothy 2:9:** Baxter writes "Paul saith, he 'suffered as an evil-doer even unto bonds.'" The NET is Paul's first person ("I suffer hardship ..."), so we bracketed "[s]" to fit Baxter's "he." NET "as a criminal" stands for "as an evil-doer."
- **2 Timothy 1:12 (flag):** NET wording, split around "he says" as Baxter splits it. The King James "that which I have committed unto him" (what Paul has entrusted to God) is NET "what has been entrusted to me" (what God has entrusted to Paul). Baxter's point, God's ability to keep, still holds, but the thing kept is different.
- **2 Timothy 3:12; 2 Thessalonians 1:4–5; Revelation 2:10:** NET wording. NET "boast" for King James "glory," "perseverance" for "patience" (Baxter's own sentence before it keeps his word **patience**); "a crown of life" is "the crown that is life itself."
- **Philippians 1:28–29:** NET wording; the NET's verse 28 continues a sentence from verse 27, so we capitalized "And." Baxter's closing "so great a gift" builds on the King James "it is given"; the NET's "it has been granted" keeps that sense.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): pretence, pretext; wanteth, lacks; preferments (advancement in rank or office), promotions; without desert, undeservedly; fly, flee; felicity, happiness; what a deal of, how much; acquainting, informing; matter (in "the matter of their profit"), occasion; holidays (the church's feast days for the martyrs), holy days; stripes, lashes; forbear ... to sing, refrain ... from singing; quicken, enliven; scarce, scarcely; thoughtful, anxious; brethren, brothers (note 348).
- **Kept:** "be quiet" (be at peace); "the creature" (any created being, here men, as opposed to God); "engaged to bless" (pledged to bless); "confessors" (Christians who suffered for confessing Christ without being killed); "dross"; "mortify the flesh"; "afflicters."
- **papists** (Direction 6): Baxter's word for Roman Catholics. His remark that they "bloodily make more" martyrs while honoring the ancient ones is his polemic, kept as written.
- **Numbering:** Baxter's numbered questions "1." to "6." and his three ways of fearing men "1." to "3." are kept as he numbers them.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "martyrs have the most glorious crown" (compare James 1:12; Revelation 2:10); "how deceitful a thing your heart is" (Jeremiah 17:9); "the holy image of God" (compare Colossians 3:10).
- **Note 347:** an exchange between Socrates and his wife, which Baxter takes from Diogenes Laertius.
- **Notes 348 and 349:** "Victor. Uticens." is Victor Uticensis (Victor of Vita), who wrote on the Vandal persecution of Catholic Christians in North Africa; Hunnericus and Gensericus are the Vandal kings Huneric and Genseric. "Masculinus" is printed as in the source; we have not checked the name against Victor. "Bucholtzer" is probably the German chronologer Abraham Bucholzer, but we have not confirmed which work Baxter quotes. The Latin in note 348 echoes 2 Corinthians 6:2 ("now is the acceptable time ... now is the day of salvation"). "Votiva ... dies" is rendered "a day we have longed for" (literally a day of vows or prayers).
- **Note 350:** Anacharsis, the Scythian sage, as reported in Diogenes Laertius.
- **Note 351:** "Paul. Scalig." is probably Paul Scaliger (Pavao Skalić), a sixteenth-century writer; we have not confirmed the work or page.
