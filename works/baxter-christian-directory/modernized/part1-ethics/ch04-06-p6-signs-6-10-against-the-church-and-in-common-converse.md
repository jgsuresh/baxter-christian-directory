# Chapter 4: Signs of Pride in Church Matters (Signs 6-10) and the First Signs of Pride in Common Interaction (Signs 1-5)

*This file begins in the middle of Direction 2 ("Observe the motions and discoveries of pride"), partway through Baxter's list of signs of pride, at Sign 6 of the signs concerning the church and the ministry. It ends partway through the Direction, at Sign 5 of the signs in common interaction.*

**Sign 6.** A proud man is more troubled when he perceives that he is undervalued and misses the honor which he sought, than that his preaching does not succeed for the good of souls, or his prayers do not prevail for their spiritual good. [217] Every man is most troubled about missing that which is his end. To do good and get good is the end of the sincere, and this he looks after, and rejoices if he obtains it, and is troubled if he misses it. To seem good, and wise, and able is the proud man's end; and if the people honor him, it puffs him up with gladness, as if he were a happy man; and if they slight him or despise him, he is cast down, or cast into some turbulent passion, and begins hating or quarreling with those who deny him the honor he expects, as if they did him a heinous wrong: as if a physician should lack both skill and care to cure his patients, but hate and revile them, because they prefer another who is abler, and will not die to secure his honor, or magnify his skill for killing their friends. The proud man's honor is his life and idol.

**Sign 7.** The heart of the proud is not inclined to humbling duties, to penitent confessions, and lamentations for sin, and earnest prayer for grace and pardon; but to some formal observances and lip-labor, or the Pharisee's self-applause, "I thank you that I am not like other people ... or even like this tax collector." Not but that the humblest have great cause to bless God for their spiritual mercies and his differencing grace; but the proud thank God for that which they do not have; for sanctification, when they are unsanctified; and for justification, when they are unjustified; and for the assured hope of glory, when they are sure to be damned if they are not changed by renewing grace; and for being made the heirs of heaven while they continue the heirs of hell. And therefore the proud are least afraid of coming without right or preparation to the sacrament of the body and blood of Christ: they rush in with confident presumption; when the humble soul is trembling outside, as being often more fearful to enter than it ought.

**Sign 8.** Proud persons are of all others the most impatient of church discipline, and incapable of living under the government of Christ. If they sin, they can scarcely endure the gentlest admonition; but if they are reproved sharply (or cuttingly) that they may be sound in the faith, you will perceive that they smart by their impatience. But if you proceed to more public reproof and admonition, and call them to an open confession of their sin to those whom they have wronged, or before the congregation, and to ask forgiveness, and seriously ask for the prayers of the church, you will then see the power of pride against the ordinance and commands of God. How scornfully will they spurn at these reproofs and exhortations! How obstinately will they refuse to submit to their unquestionable duty! And how hardly are they brought to confess the most notorious sins! or to confess that it is their duty to confess them; though they would easily believe that it is the duty of another, and would exhort another to do that which they themselves refuse! The medicine seems so loathsome to them which Christ has prescribed for them, that they hate him who brings it, and will die and be damned before they will take it; but perhaps will turn again and completely tear you apart (unless where they are restrained by the civil authority.) But if you proceed to reject them, for their obstinate impenitence in heinous sin, from the visible communion of the church, you will then see yet more how contrary pride is to the church order and government ordained by Christ. How bitterly will they hate those who put them to such (necessary) disgrace! How will they storm, and rage, and turn their fury against the church; as if Christ's remedy were the greatest injury to them in the world! You may read their character in Psalm 2. Therefore Christ calls men to come as little children into his school; or else they will be unteachable and incorrigible (Matthew 18:3).

**Sign 9.** A proud man has an heretical disposition, even when he cries out against heretics. He is apt to look most after matters of dispute and contention in religion; obscure prophecies, God's decrees, controversies which trouble the church more than they edify, circumstances, ceremonies, forms, externals, orders, and words: and for his opinion in these he must be somebody.

**Sign 10.** A proud man is unsatisfied with his standing in communion with the church of Christ, and is either ambitiously aspiring to a dominion over it, or is inclined to a separation from it. They are too good to stand on even ground with their brethren: if they are teachers or rulers they can approve the constitution of the church; but otherwise it is too bad for them to have communion with; they must be of some more refined or elevated society: they are not content to come out and be separate from the unbelieving and idolatrous world, but they must also come out and be separate from the churches of Christ, consisting of men who make a credible profession of faith and godliness. They think it not enough to forbear sin themselves, and to have no fellowship with the works of darkness, but reprove them, nor to separate from men as they separate from Christ; but they will also separate from them in their duty, and odiously aggravate every imperfection, and fill the church with clamors and contentions, and break it into fractions by their schisms, and this not for any true reformation or edifying of the body, (for how can division edify it?) but to tell the world that they account themselves more holy than the church. [218] Thus Christ himself was quarreled with as unholy by the Pharisees for eating with publicans and sinners; and his disciples for not washing before eating, and observing the traditions of the elders; [219] and for rubbing out grain to eat on the Sabbath day. And they who will not be strict in their conformity to Christ, will be righteous overmuch, and stricter than Christ would have them be, where pride commands it. They will be of the strictest party and opinions, and make opinions and parties that are stricter than God's commands; and run into errors and schisms that they may be singular, from the general communion of the church; and will be of a lesser than Christ's little flock.

**Signs of Pride in Common Interaction.**

**Sign 1.** Pride causes subjects to be too quick in censuring the actions of their governors, and too impatient of what they suffer from them, and apt to murmur at them, and rebel against them. It makes inferiors think themselves competent judges of those commands and actions of their superiors, the reasons of which they never heard, nor can be fit to judge of, unless they were of their council. It makes them forget all the benefits of government, and mind only the burdens and suffering part, and say as Korah, "You take too much upon yourselves, seeing that the whole community is holy, every one of them, and the Lord is among them. Why then do you exalt yourselves above the community of the Lord?" (Numbers 16:3). "Is it a small thing that you have brought us up out of the land that flows with milk and honey, to kill us in the wilderness? Now do you want to make yourself a prince over us? ... Do you think you can blind these men?" (Numbers 16:13-14). Proud men are impatient, and aggravate their disappointments, and think they have reason and justice on their side.

**Sign 2.** A proud man is more disposed to command than to obey, and cannot serve God contentedly in a mean and low condition. He is never a good subject, or servant, or child, for subjection seems a slavery to him. He thinks it a baseness to be governed by another. He has a reason of his own, which still contradicts the reason of his rulers, and a will of his own that must needs be fulfilled, and cannot submit or yield to government. He is still ready to step out of his rank, and prepare for suffering by disorder, that he may taste the sweetness of present liberty; as if your horse or cattle should break out from you to be free, and starve in the winter, when snow deprives them of grass. Whereas the humble know it is much easier to obey than govern, and that the valleys are the most fruitful grounds, and that it is the cedars and mountain trees that are blown down, and not the shrubs, and that a low condition affords not only more safety, but more quietness and leisure to converse with God, and that it is a mercy that others may be employed in his preservation, and keeping the walls, and watching the house, while he may follow his work in quietness and peace; and therefore willingly pays honor and tribute to whom it is due.

**Sign 3.** If a proud man is a ruler, he is apt to be lifted up in mind; and to despise his inferiors, as if they were not men, or he were more. He is apt to disdain the counsels of the wise, and to scorn admonition from the ministers of Christ, and to hate every Micaiah who prophesies not good of him, and to value none but flatterers, and disfavor faithful dealers, and not endure to hear of his faults. He is apt to quarrel with the power of godliness, and the gospel of Christ, as that which seems to cross his interest; and to forget his own subjection to God, and the danger of his subjects. He is more desirous to be obeyed by his inferiors, than himself to obey his absolute Lord. He expects that his commands be obeyed, though God command the contrary; and is more offended at the neglect of his laws and honor, than at the contempt of the honor and laws of God.

**Sign 4.** If there is any place of office, honor, or promotion vacant, a proud man thinks that he is the fittest for it; and if he seeks it he takes it for an injury if another is preferred before him as more deserving: and though those who had a hand in putting him aside, and preferring another, did it ever so judiciously, and impartially, and for the common good, without any respect to any friend or interest of their own, yet all this will not satisfy the proud, who knows no reason or law but selfishness; but he will bear a grudge toward men for the most righteous, necessary action. What ignorant men and impious have we known displeased, because they were not thought worthy to be teachers in the church! or because a people who knew the worth of their souls, had the wit and conscience to prefer a worthier man before them! What worthless men (in corporations and elsewhere) have we seen displeased, because they were not chosen to be governors! So unreasonable a sin is pride.

**Sign 5.** A proud man thinks, when he looks at the works of his superiors, that he could do them better himself, if he had the doing of them. There is not one of them in a hundred but think that they could rule better than the king does, and judge better than the judge does, and perhaps preach better than the preacher does, unless his ignorance is so obvious that he cannot question it. Absalom would do the people justice better than his father David, if he were king. If all the matters of church and commonwealth were at his disposal, how confident is he that they would be well ordered, and all faults mended; and oh! how happy a world we would have!

## Translator's notes

- **Position in the Direction:** the range begins at Sign 6 of the signs of pride in the church and ministry and carries on through the heading "Signs of Pride in common Converse" to Sign 5. Baxter numbers his signs in Roman numerals within each group; they are rendered here as "Sign 6" and so on. The earlier file ends at an earlier sign; the next file continues after Sign 5.
- **Luke 18:11 (Sign 7):** Baxter gives no reference for "I thank thee that I am not as other men, nor as this publican." The reference is added here by the translator and is not in Baxter's text. We use the NET with an ellipsis for the words between "other people" and "or even like this tax collector" (the NET lists "extortionists, unrighteous people, adulterers" there). The NET has "or even like," where the King James had "or even as"; "tax collector" is the NET word for the King James "publican," which Baxter also uses later in this file ("publicans and sinners"; kept as written).
- **Matthew 18:3 (Sign 8):** Baxter's "come as little children into his school" is his own paraphrase, with the reference given. The NET reads "unless you turn around and become like little children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven!" The King James had "except ye be converted, and become as little children." The NET's "turn around" is close to Baxter's sense of coming into Christ's school, but he is not quoting it.
- **Psalm 2 (Sign 8):** cited by Baxter ("the second Psalm") without quotation; it speaks of rulers who rage against the Lord and his anointed.
- **Numbers 16:3, 13-14 (Sign 1, common interaction):** NET wording. Differences from Baxter's King James: where Baxter has "congregation," the NET has "community." Baxter's "except thou make thyself altogether a prince over us?" has no "except" or "altogether" in the NET, which reads "Now do you want to make yourself a prince over us?" Baxter's "Wilt thou put out the eyes of these men?" is the King James; the NET reads "Do you think you can blind these men?" (verse 14), and Baxter's "--" skips the rest of verse 14. We use the ellipsis there. The loss of "altogether" is flagged because Baxter may lean on it for the charge of total domination. Baxter writes "Ver. 13, 14"; rendered as "Numbers 16:13-14".
- **Isaiah 65:5 (note 218):** a bare reference. For the reader's convenience, the NET of the verse reads: "They say, 'Keep to yourself! Don't get near me, for I am holier than you!'" The King James had "I am holier than thou."
- **Titus 1:13 (Sign 8):** Baxter gives no reference for "sound in the faith." The King James of Titus 1:13 has "that they may be sound in the faith"; the NET reads "rebuke them sharply that they may be healthy in the faith." The sense is close, but Baxter's "sound" is the King James word. "(or cuttingly)" is Baxter's own gloss on "sharply." The reference is not in Baxter's text.
- **Ecclesiastes 7:16 (Sign 10):** Baxter's "righteous overmuch" is the King James wording of an unmarked allusion; the NET has "do not be excessively righteous." Kept in Baxter's words.
- **Heading "Common Interaction":** Baxter's "Common Converse" (everyday dealings with others) is rendered "Common Interaction" in the heading only.
- **Matthew 11:19; 9:11; 15:2-3 (note 219):** a bare reference; unquoted allusions to the Pharisees' complaints about Christ and his disciples. Baxter's "rubbing out corn to eat on the sabbath-day" is unmarked; compare Matthew 12:1-2 and Luke 6:1-2 (the references are not in Baxter's text).
- **Word swaps:** perceiveth to perceives; oft to often; honour to honor; misseth to misses; falls a hating to begins hating; wrangling to quarreling; lip-labour to lip-labor; uncapable to incapable; scarce to scarcely; physic to medicine; all to rent you to "completely tear you apart" (Baxter's "all to rent" uses "all to" as an intensifier meaning "completely"); the secular arm to "the civil authority" (the power of the state or magistrates to enforce; the phrase "secular arm" is Baxter's); crave to ask for; outwards to externals; meat (in "washing before meat") to eating; corn to grain (the word then meant grain in general); sabbath-day to Sabbath day; infidel to unbelieving; fall out with to quarrel with; discountenance to disfavor; void to vacant; preferment to promotion; putting him by to putting him aside; never so judiciously to "ever so judiciously"; famish to starve; should we have to "we would have"; "should be well ordered" to "would be well ordered."
- **Kept as Baxter's words:** differencing grace (his phrase for the grace of God that makes the difference between one person and another); lip-labor (worship that is only words); heretical disposition; formal observances; reproved sharply (or cuttingly) (compare Titus 1:13 and the "sound in the faith" wording; the reference is not in Baxter's text); fractions (broken parts, compare "factions"; left as written); singular (wishing to stand out, different from the rest); mean (lowly); corporations (towns or other bodies with a charter; kept); faithful dealers (people who deal honestly and faithfully with him).
- **Sign 5 wording:** "There is not one of them in a hundred but think that they could rule better" keeps Baxter's order and his shift from "one" to "they."
- **Allusions (unmarked, kept in Baxter's wording):** the Pharisee and the publican (Luke 18); "every Micaiah that prophesieth not good of him" (1 Kings 22:8, 18; the reference is not in Baxter's text); "righteous overmuch" (Ecclesiastes 7:16); "come out and be separate" (compare 2 Corinthians 6:17); "no fellowship with the works of darkness, but reprove them" (Ephesians 5:11); "Christ's little flock" (Luke 12:32); Absalom and David (2 Samuel 15:1-6).
- **Baxter's polemic:** his remarks about separatists ("they will also separate from them in their duty") are his, aimed at the separation movements of his day, and are kept exactly as written.
- **"the sacrament of the body and blood of Christ"** (Sign 7): the Lord's Supper. **"right or preparation":** a right to come, or fitness to come.
