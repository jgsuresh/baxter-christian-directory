# Chapter 1, Part 1 (continued): What Baxter Assumes About the Reader, Items 10–18

*That no unbeliever can say, He is sure there is no life to come.*

10. I may take it for granted at the worst that neither you yourself, nor any unbeliever in the world, can say that you are sure that there is not another life for man, in which his present obedience shall be rewarded, and disobedience punished. The worst that ever an unbeliever could say was that he thinks there is no other life. None of you dare deny the possibility of it, nor can with any reason deny the probability. Well, then, let this be remembered while we proceed a little further with you.

*That you are sure of the brevity and emptiness of this life: and that the probability or possibility of an endless joy or misery should command all the care and diligence of a rational creature, against all that can be set against it.*

11. I suppose or expect that you have so much use of sense and reason as to know the brevity and emptiness of all the glory and pleasures of the flesh; and that they are all so quickly gone that were they greater than they are, they can be of no considerable value. Alas, what is time! How quickly gone, and then it is nothing! and all things then are nothing which have passed away with it! So that the joys or sorrows of so short a life are no great matter of gain or loss.

I may therefore suppose that you can easily conclude that the bare probability or possibility of an endless happiness should be infinitely preferred to such transitory emptiness, even the greatest matters that can be expected here; and that the probability or possibility of endless misery in hell should engage us with far greater care and diligence to avoid it than is due for avoiding anything that you can hope to escape by sinning; or any of the sufferings of this momentary life. If you do not see this, you have lost your reason; that the mere probability or possibility of a heaven and hell should much more command our care and diligence than the fading emptiness of this dreaming, transitory life.

*Therefore that a holy life is every man's duty, even if only on account of such a possibility or probability; and therefore that really there is such a joy and misery hereafter; because God does not make our faculties in vain, nor make us follow deceits and lies.*

12. Well, then, we have got thus far in the clearest light. You see that a religious, holy life is every man's duty, not only as they owe it to God as their Creator, their Owner, Governor, and Benefactor; but also because, as lovers of ourselves, our reason commands us to have ten thousandfold more regard for a probable or possible joy and torment which are endless than for any that is small and of short duration. And if this is so, that a holy life is every man's duty with respect to the life that is to come, then it is most evident that there is such a life to come indeed, and that it is more than probable or possible, even certain. For if it is but man's duty to manage this life by the hopes and fears of another life, then it must follow that either there is such a life to come, or else that God has made it man's duty to hope, and fear, and care, and labor, and live in vain: and that he himself does tantalize and cheat his creatures, and rule the world by motives of deceit, and make religion and obedience to our Maker a life of folly, delusion, and our loss. And he that believes this of God scarcely believes him to be God. Though I have mentioned this argument in another treatise, I think it not unfitting here to repeat it for your benefit.

*That all the matters of this transitory life are to be estimated as they relate to the life to come.*

13. And seeing I suppose you to be convinced of the life to come, and that man's happiness and misery are there, I must necessarily suppose that you confess that all things in this life, whether prosperity or adversity, honor or dishonor, are to be esteemed and used as they relate to the life to come. For nothing is more plain than that the means are to have all their esteem and use in order to their end. That only is good in this life which tends to the happiness of our endless life; and that is evil indeed in this life that tends to our endless hurt, and to deprive us of the everlasting good. And therefore no price or motive should hire us to sin against God, and to forfeit or hinder our endless happiness.

*That no man can love God too much, nor make too sure of his salvation.*

14. I may suppose, if you have reason, that you will confess that God cannot be too much loved, nor obeyed too exactly, nor served too diligently (especially by such reluctant sinners that have scarcely any inclination to love or worship him at all); and that no man can make too sure of heaven, or pay too dearly for it, or do too much for his salvation, if it is but that which God has appointed him to do. And that you have nothing else that is so much worth your time, and love, and care, and labor. And therefore though you have need to be stopped in your love, and care, and labor for the world, because for it you may easily pay too dearly and do too much; yet there is no need of stopping men in their love, and care, and labor for God and their salvation; which is worth more than ever we can do, and where the best are apt to do too little.

*That this life is given us for trial and preparation for the life to come.*

15. I also suppose you to be one that knows that this present life is given us on trial, [14] to prepare for the life that shall come after; and that as men live here, they shall fare forever; and that time cannot be recalled when it is gone, and therefore that we should make the best of it while we have it.

*That man's thoughts should be serious and frequent about his future state.*

16. I suppose you also to be easily convinced that seeing man has his reason and life for matters of everlasting consequence, his thoughts of them should be frequent and very serious, and his reason should be used about these things by secluded, sober deliberation.

*That you can tell, or can find out, which way your hearts and diligence are bent, whether most for this life or for that to come.*

17. And I suppose you to be a man, and therefore so far acquainted with yourself that you may know, if you will, whether your heart and life answer your convictions, and whether they are more for heaven or earth; and therefore that you are capable of self-judgment in this case.

Perhaps you will say that while I am directing you to be holy, I suppose you to be holy first; for all this seems to go far toward it. But I must profess that I do not see anything in all these suppositions but what I may suppose to be in a heathen; and that I think all this is but supposing you to have the use of your reason in the points in hand. Speak freely: Is there any one of all these points that you can or dare deny? I think there is not. And therefore if heathens and wicked men deny them in their practice, that does but show that sin brutalizes them, and that, as men asleep, or in a crowd of business, they do not have the use of the reason which they possess in the matters which their minds are turned from.

*That most among us profess to believe in Christ, and confess the gospel to be true, etc.*

18. Indeed, one thing more I think I may suppose in all or most that will read this book; that you also profess to believe in Jesus Christ, and in the Holy Spirit the Sanctifier, and that the Scriptures are the word of God. And if you do so indeed, I may then hope that my work is in a sense done, before I begin it: but if you do it but as a mere opinion and ineffectually, yet God and man may plead with you the truths which you profess.

Having told you what I presuppose in you, I proceed now to the directions. But I again entreat and charge you, reader, as you love your soul, and do not want to be condemned for hypocrisy and sloth, that you do not refuse to put in practice what is taught you, and show thereby that whatever you claim, you are not willing to do your part for your own salvation, no, not in the most reasonable, necessary things. [15]

(We were not sown and created rashly or by chance; there was surely some power that looked after the human race, and that would not beget or nourish a creature which, after enduring all its labors, would then fall into the everlasting evil of death. Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations*, book 1.) Nec unquam bono quicquam mali evenire potest, nec vivo nec mortuo. Nec res ejus à Diis negliguntur. Idem. 1. Tusc. (Nor can any evil ever befall a good man, either living or dead. Nor are his affairs neglected by the gods. The same, book 1.)

[15] Abeunt omnia unde orta sunt. Cic. in. lat. Maj. (All things return to where they came from. Cicero, in *Cato the Elder*.) Dii immortales sparserunt animos in corpora humana, ut essent qui terras tuerentur, quique cœlestem ordinem contemplantes, imitarentur eum vitæ modo atque constantia. Cic. in Cato Majore. (The immortal gods scattered souls into human bodies so that there would be those who would look after the earth and who, contemplating the heavenly order, would imitate it in the manner and steadiness of their lives. Cicero, *Cato the Elder*.) Ex terrâ sunt homines, non ut incolæ, et habitatores, sed quasi spectatores superarum rerum atque cœlestium; quarum spectaculum ad nullum aliud genus animantium pertinet. Cicero 2. de Nat. Deor. Bonorum mentes mihi divinæ atque æternæ videntur, et ex hominum vita ad deorum religionem et sanctimoniamque migrare. Idem. (The minds of the good seem to me divine and eternal, and to pass from the life of men to the worship and holiness of the gods. The same.) (The soul is implanted by God, and from this we may truly speak of a kinship with the heavenly beings, or of lineage or stock. The same, *On the Laws*, book 1.)

## Translator's notes

- **unbeliever** (item 10 and its margin note): Baxter's "infidel," meaning someone who does not believe in God or the afterlife. We avoid "infidel," which now reads as a slur.
- **emptiness** (items 11): Baxter's "vanity," in the sense of Ecclesiastes (worthless, fleeting), not conceit.
- **fare** (item 15): Baxter's "speed," as in the old phrase "how did you speed?" meaning "how did it go with you." Here it means how people's lives turn out forever depending on how they live now.
- **secluded** (item 16): Baxter's "retired," meaning withdrawn from distraction to think in private.
- **"men it must follow"** (item 12): the Gutenberg text prints "men it must follow." It is almost certainly a misprint for "then," and we read it that way.
- **brutalizes** (item 17): Baxter's "brutify," meaning to make like a brute or beast.
- **heathen** (items 17, 18): kept. Baxter means people who do not know the God of the Bible, here used in an argument about reason.
- **Holy Spirit** (item 18): Baxter's "Holy Ghost." The older name is modernized as a word change.
- **as a mere opinion and ineffectually** (item 18): Baxter's "opinionatively and uneffectually," meaning only as a held opinion that has no effect on the person's life.
- **claim** (closing paragraph): Baxter's "pretendest," which meant "claim," not "make believe" (see the glossary entry for *pretended*).
- **Scripture:** none quoted in this section.
