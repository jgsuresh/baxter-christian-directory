# Chapter 3: Grand Direction X (part 3 of 6)

*This section continues Grand Direction 10, begun in an earlier file; it opens with Direction 16 and runs through Direction 25.*

**Direction 16. Where God has prescribed you some particular good work or way of service, you must prefer that before another which is greater in itself.**

This explains or limits Direction 8. The reason is that God knows best what is pleasing to him, and "obedience is better than sacrifice" (1 Samuel 15:22). You must not neglect the necessary maintenance of wife and children, under the claim of doing a work of piety or greater good; because God has prescribed you this order of your duty, that you begin at home (though not to stop there). Another minister may have a greater or more needy flock; but still you must first do good in your own, and not step without a call into his charge. If God has called you to serve him in a low and humble employment, he will better accept you in that work, than if you undertook the work of another man's place, to do him greater service.

**Direction 17. Do not lose your resolutions or opportunities of doing good by unnecessary delays.**

"Do not withhold good from those who need it when you have the ability to help. Do not say to your neighbor, 'Go! Return tomorrow—and I will give it,' when you have it with you at the time" (Proverbs 3:27-28). "Do not boast about tomorrow; for you do not know what a day may bring forth" (Proverbs 27:1). It is two to one that delay will take away your opportunity, and raise such unexpected distractions or difficulties as will frustrate your intent, and destroy the work. Seize your time, if you want to do your service: it is beautiful in its season.

**Direction 18. Yet present necessity may make a lesser work your duty, when the greater may better bear delay.**

As to save a man's life in sickness or danger, when you may afterward have time to seek the saving of his soul. Not only may works of mercy be thus preferred before sacrifice, but the ordinary conveniences of our lives; as to rise, and dress ourselves, and do other business, may go before prayer, when prayer may afterward be done as well or better, and would be hindered if these did not go before.

**Direction 19. Though, *cæteris paribus*, the duties of the first table are to be preferred before those of the second, yet the greater duties of the second table must be preferred before the lesser duties of the first.**

The love of God is a greater duty than the love of man (and they must never be separated); but still we must prefer the saving of a man's life, or the quenching of a fire in the town, before a prayer, or sacrament, or observation of a sabbath. David ate the showbread, and the disciples rubbed out the grain on the sabbath day, because the preserving of life was a greater duty than the observing of a sabbath, or a positive ceremonial law. And Christ tells the Pharisees, "Go and learn what this saying means: 'I want mercy and not sacrifice'" (Matthew 9:13): the blood of our brothers is an unacceptable means of pleasing God, and maintaining piety, or promoting men's various opinions in religion.

**Direction 20. Choose that employment or calling (so far as you have your choice) in which you may be most serviceable to God.**

Do not choose that in which you may be most rich or honorable in the world; but that in which you may do most good, and best escape sinning.

*In the margin: Is doing good or avoiding sin to be most looked at in our choice of callings.*

*Question.* But what if in one calling I am most serviceable to the church, but still have the most temptations to sin? And in another I have the fewest temptations to sin, but am least serviceable to the church, (which is the ordinary difference between men in public places and men in solitude,) which of these should I choose?

*Answer.* 1. Either you are already engaged in your calling, or not; if you are, you must have greater reasons to desert it than such as might require you at first not to choose it. 2. Either the temptations to sin are such as good men ordinarily overcome, or they are extraordinarily great. You may more justifiably avoid such great ones as you are not likely to overcome than small or ordinary ones. 3. Either you are well equipped against these temptations, or not: if not, you must be more cautious in approaching them; but if you are, you may trust God the more boldly to help you out. 4. Either they are temptations to ordinary human frailties in the manner of duty, or temptations to more dangerous sin: the first will not so much justify you in avoiding doing good in order to escape them as the latter will. 5. The service that you are called to (being supposed great and necessary to be done by somebody) is either such as others will do better, or as well, if you avoid it, or not. If the church or common good suffers no harm by your refusal, you may the more insist on your own preservation; but if the needs of the church or state, and the lack of fitter instruments, or any evident call of God, single you out for that service, you must obey God, whatever the difficulties and temptations are: for no temptations can force you to sin; and God who calls you, can easily preserve you: but be careful what you thrust yourselves upon.

*In the margin: A calling may be changed.*

*Question.* But may I change my calling for the service of the church, when the apostle tells every man to remain in the calling in which he was called? 1 Corinthians 7:20.

*Answer.* The apostle only requires men to make no unlawful change (such as is the forsaking of a wife or husband) nor any unnecessary change, as if it were necessary (as in the case of uncircumcision): but in the next words he says, "Were you called as a slave? Do not worry about it. But if indeed you are able to be free, make the most of the opportunity" (1 Corinthians 7:21). He tells every man to remain with God in the place he is called to, but does not forbid them to change their state when they are called to change it, verse 24. He speaks more of relations (of single persons and married, servants and free, etc.) than of trades or offices: and yet no doubt but a single person may be married, and the married must be separated; and servants may be free. No man must take up or change any calling without sufficient cause to call him to it; but when he has such cause, he sins if he does not change it. The apostles changed their callings, when they became apostles; and so did multitudes of the pastors of the church in every age. God nowhere forbids men to change their employment for the better, upon a sufficient cause or call.

*In the margin: Who excused from a calling.*

**Direction 21. Especially be sure that you do not live out of a calling, that is, such a regular course of employment, in which you may best be serviceable to God.**

Disability indeed is an irresistible impediment. Otherwise no man must either live idly, or content himself with doing some little chores, as a recreation, or on the side; but everyone who is able, must be regularly and ordinarily employed in such work, as is serviceable to God, and the common good. *Question.* But will not wealth excuse us? *Answer.* It may excuse you from some degrading sort of work, by making you more serviceable in other; but you are no more excused from service and work of one kind or other, than the poorest man; unless you think that God requires least where he gives most. *Question.* Will not age excuse us? *Answer.* Yes, so far as it disables you; but no further. *Objection.* But I am turned out of my calling. *Answer.* You are not turned out of the service of God: he calls you to that, or to another. *Question.* But may I not cast off the world, that I may only think of my salvation? *Answer.* You may cast off all such excess of worldly cares or business as unnecessarily hinder you in spiritual things; but you may not cast off all bodily employment and mental labor in which you may serve the common good. Everyone who is a member of church or commonwealth, must employ their abilities to the utmost for the good of the church and commonwealth: public service is God's greatest service. To neglect this, and say, I will pray and meditate, is as if your servant should refuse your greatest work, and tie himself to some lesser, easy part. And God has commanded you in some way or other to labor for your daily bread, and not live as drones on the sweat of others only. Innocent Adam was put into the garden of Eden to dress it; and fallen man must "eat his bread in the sweat of his brow," Genesis 3:19; and he that "will not work must be forbidden to eat," 2 Thessalonians 3:6, 10, 12. And indeed it is necessary to ourselves, for the health of our bodies, which will grow diseased with idleness; and for the help of our souls, which will fail if the body fails: and man in flesh must have work for his body as well as for his soul. And he that will do nothing but pray and meditate, it is likely will (by sickness or melancholy) be disabled before long either to pray or meditate: unless he has an extraordinarily strong body.

**Direction 22. Be very watchful redeemers of your time, and take to heart every hour and minute, that you do not lose it, but spend it in the best and most serviceable manner that you can.**

Of this I intend to speak more particularly soon; and therefore will here add no more.

**Direction 23. Watchfully and resolutely avoid the entanglements and diverting occasions by which the tempter will be always trying to waste your time and hinder you from your work.**

Know what is the principal service that you are called to, and avoid distractions: especially magistrates and ministers, and those who have great and public work, must be careful here. For if you are not very wise and watchful, the tempter will draw you, before you are aware, into such a multitude of diverting care or business, that shall seem to be your duties, as shall make you almost unprofitable in the world: you shall have this or that little thing that must be done, and this or that friend that must be visited or spoken to, and this or that courtesy that must be performed: so that trifles shall keep you from all important works. I admit friends must not be neglected, nor courtesies be denied; but our greatest duties having the greatest necessity, all things must give place to them in their proper season. And therefore, that you may avoid the offense of friends, avoid the place or occasions of such impediments; and where that cannot be done, whatever they judge of you, do not neglect your most necessary work; else it will be at the will of men and Satan, whether you shall be serviceable to God or not.

**Direction 24. Ask yourselves seriously, how you would wish at death and judgment that you had used all your wits, and time, and wealth; and resolve accordingly to use them now.**

This is an excellent direction and motive to you for doing good, and preventing the condemnation which will pass upon unprofitable servants. Ask yourselves, Will it comfort me more at death or judgment, to think, or hear, that I spent this hour in plays or idleness, or in doing good to myself or others? How shall I wish then I had laid out my property, and every part of it? Reason itself condemns him who will not now choose the course which then he shall wish that he had chosen, when we foresee the consequence of that day.

**Direction 25. Understand how much you are indebted to God, (and not he to you,) in that he will employ you in doing any good; and how it is the way of your own receiving; and know the excellence of your work and end, that you may do it all with love and pleasure.**

Not knowing our Master, and the nature and tendency of our work, is what makes it seem tiresome and unpleasant to us: and we shall never do it well, when we do it with an ill will, as merely forced. God loves a cheerful servant, who loves his Master and his work. It is the main scheme of the devil to make our duty seem grievous, unprofitable, undesirable, and wearisome to us: for a little thing will stop him who goes unwillingly and in continual pain.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). All verses below were fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **1 Samuel 15:22:** Baxter gives no reference (we have added it here, not in his text). NET wording of the phrase quoted: "obedience is better than sacrifice." Same sense as Baxter's use.
- **Proverbs 3:27 (flag, mild):** the King James has "Withhold not good from them to whom it is due," which stresses what is owed to others. The NET has "from those who need it," which stresses need. Baxter's Direction (do not delay what you ought to do for others) works with either, but the "due" idea is weaker in the NET. In verse 28 the NET prints a dash after "tomorrow"; the fetched plain text dropped it ("tomorrowand"), and we restored it as a dash.
- **Matthew 9:13:** Baxter gives no reference for "Go, learn what this meaneth,--I will have mercy, and not sacrifice." Matthew 9:13 has "Go and learn," and the quoted words are from Hosea 6:6 (also quoted in Matthew 12:7, which has no "Go and learn"). We have chosen Matthew 9:13 and have not confirmed that this is the verse Baxter had in mind. We quote only the portion Baxter gives.
- **1 Corinthians 7:20 and 7:24 (important):** Baxter's whole answer rests on the word *calling*: "abide in the calling in which he was called." He is reading the King James "calling" as including trade or office, then arguing the verse is really about relations (married or single, slave or free). The NET renders the word as "situation in life" ("Let each one remain in that situation in life in which he was called"), which lines up with Baxter's own conclusion that Paul speaks of relations more than of trades, but loses the pun on "calling" that gives his Question its force. In his Question we kept his words ("calling in which he was called") as his paraphrase of the King James. Verse 24 is cited by number only.
- **1 Corinthians 7:21 (flag, servant/slave):** Baxter's King James has "Art thou called being a servant?"; the NET has "Were you called as a slave?" We follow the NET in the quotation. The sense Baxter relies on (a person of low state may take freedom if offered) is unchanged.
- **1 Corinthians 7:21 (flag):** Baxter's King James reads "if thou mayest be made free, use it rather." The NET has "make the most of the opportunity." The NET object ("the opportunity") is less clear than the King James "use it," and the verse has been read both as "use your freedom" and as "use your slavery" (we have not surveyed the commentators). Baxter takes it as "use your freedom," and the NET wording is neutral on that point. We do not take a side.
- **Genesis 3:19 (paraphrase):** Baxter's "eat his bread in the sweat of his brow" is a third-person paraphrase of the King James ("In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread"). We kept his wording, modernized. The NET reads "By the sweat of your brow you will eat food."
- **2 Thessalonians 3:6, 10, 12 (paraphrase):** Baxter's "he that will not work must be forbidden to eat" blends the three verses he cites. NET verse 10: "If anyone is not willing to work, neither should he eat." Verse 6 tells believers "to keep away from any brother who lives an undisciplined life," and verse 12 urges such people "to work quietly and so provide their own food to eat." His "forbidden" is his own gloss and is not in the NET. Kept as Baxter's.
- **Unmarked allusions:** "beautiful in its season" (Ecclesiastes 3:11; NET: "God has made everything fit beautifully in its appropriate time"); David eating the showbread (1 Samuel 21:1-6, compare Matthew 12:3-4) and the disciples rubbing out grain on the sabbath (Matthew 12:1; Mark 2:23); "unprofitable servants" (Matthew 25:30, compare Luke 17:10); "redeemers of your time" (compare Ephesians 5:16); "Innocent Adam was put into the garden of Eden to dress it" (Genesis 2:15). These are marked here only as likely allusions; Baxter does not cite them.
- **first table / second table (Direction 19):** The Ten Commandments were traditionally divided into two "tables": the first concerns duty to God, the second duty to other people. (Baxter's own usage here fits this; we have not checked his other works.) Baxter uses the terms without explanation.
- ***cæteris paribus*:** Latin for "other things being equal." Left in Baxter's Latin.
- **positive ceremonial law:** Baxter's phrase, kept. We take it to mean a law of worship that rests on a particular command (not on the general moral law); we have not confirmed his exact definition.
- **Word swaps (Baxter, then ours):** pretence, claim; mean employment, humble employment; explicatory, explains; diversions, distractions; cautelous, cautious; chars, chores; on the by, on the side; statedly, regularly; unresistible, irresistible; sordid, degrading; parts, abilities; anon, soon; entanglements kept; avocations, distractions; civilities, courtesies; unacquaintedness, not knowing; policy, scheme; beholden, indebted; observation (of a sabbath) kept as Baxter's word for keeping it; warrantably, justifiably; warrant (you), justify; furnished, equipped; apparent (call), evident; necessitate, force; detriment, harm; tedious, tiresome; excellency, excellence; considerable, important; brethren, brothers; piety kept; corn, grain (British sense of "corn"); shew-bread, showbread; estate, property.
- **"It is two to one, but delay will take away" (Direction 17):** "but" here means "that"; we wrote "that delay will take away."
- **Take thy time (Direction 17):** we rendered it "Seize your time," since "take your time" now means to go slowly, the opposite of Baxter's meaning (take the right moment, because it is beautiful in its season). This is a sense-based change; a purist might keep "Take your time" with a note.
- **make conscience of (Direction 22):** Baxter's idiom for being conscientious about something. Rendered "take to heart."
- **plays (Direction 24):** left as "plays," meaning stage plays or amusements in general.
- **melancholy (Direction 21):** kept; see GLOSSARY.md.
- **flesh (Direction 21):** "man in flesh" kept as "man in flesh," meaning in bodily life.
- **the Pharisees; men's several opinions (Direction 19):** "several" here means "various." Baxter's remark about the blood of brothers is aimed at religious persecution; we have left it as he wrote it.
- **Direction numbering:** the file follows Baxter's numbering (16 to 25). Direction 22 cross-refers to a later discussion of redeeming time ("anon"); we have not checked where.
