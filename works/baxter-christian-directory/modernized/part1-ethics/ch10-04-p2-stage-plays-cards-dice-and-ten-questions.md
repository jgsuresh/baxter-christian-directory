# Chapter 10: Part 2, Direction 1 (continued): stage-plays, cards, and dice, and ten questions for the gambler

*This section begins partway through Part 2, Direction 1, in the middle of Baxter's numbered reasons against common stage-plays, gaming, cards, and dice (after his question "But must all wicked men therefore forbear recreation?"); it opens at item 3. The previous file covers items 1–2, and the next file continues the questions with Question 11.*

3. At least it is plain that they are not the fittest recreations for any man that intends a lawful end. If you are students, or idle gentlemen, is not walking, or riding, or shooting, or some honest, bodily labor rather, that joins pleasure and profit together, a fitter kind of exercise for you? Or if you are laboring persons, and need only pleasure for your minds, should you not take pleasure in God, in Scripture, in holy conversation, meditation, or good books? Or if indeed you need a relaxation from both these, do you not have profitable history or geography to read? Do you not have herbs, and flowers, and trees, and beasts, and birds, and other creatures to behold? Do you not have fields, or gardens, or meadows, or woods to walk into? Do you not have your near relations to delight in; your wives, or children, or friends, or servants? May you not talk with good, and wise, and cheerful men, about things that are both pleasing and edifying to you? Has God given you such a world of lawful pleasures, and will none of them, nor all of them, meet your needs, without unlawful ones, or at least unfit ones (which therefore are unlawful): all these are undoubtedly lawful; but cards, and dice, and stage-plays are, at best, very questionable: among wise and learned men, and good men, and no small number of these, they are condemned as unlawful. [605] And should one that fears God and loves his salvation choose so doubtful an amusement, before such abundance of undoubtedly lawful ones? If you are so proud or rash as to reply, Why should I leave my amusement for another man's notions or judgment? I will tell you that which will shame your reply, and you, if you can blush. 1. It is not some whimsical, odd fanatic that I allege against you, nor an eccentric theologian; but it is the judgment of the ancient church itself. The fathers and councils condemn Christians and ministers especially, that use *spectacula*, spectacles, or behold stage-plays and dicing. 2. Even the oldest canons of our own Church of England forbid dicing to the clergy, which is because they considered it evil, or of ill report. 3. Many laws of religious princes condemn them. 4. A great many of the most learned, holy theologians condemn them. 5. The soberest and most learned of the papists condemn them. 6. And how great a number of the most religious ministers and people are against them, of the age and place in which you live, you are not ignorant. And is the judgment of the ancient church, and of councils, and fathers, and of the most learned Protestants and papists, and the most religious people, besides many ancient laws and canons, of no force with you in such a case as this? Will you hold to a thing admittedly unnecessary, against the judgment of so many that consider them sinful? Are you and your playfellows more wise and learned than all these? Or is it not extreme pride, for such unstudied, empty men to prefer their pleasure-loving notions before such a concurrent stream of wiser and more weighty judgments? Just read Dr. J. Reignolds's *Treatise against Stage-plays*, against Albericus Gentilis, and you will see what a world of witnesses are against you. And if the judgment of Voetius, Amesius, and other learned men against all lusory lots is of no authority, at least it should move you that even Mr. Gataker and others, that write for the lawfulness of them in that respect (as lusory lots), do yet lay down the rest of the requirements to make them lawful, which utterly condemn our common use of cards and dice, much more our gamblers: so that all the sober theologians that I ever read or heard condemn all these: and are you wiser than all of them?

4. Besides this, your consciences know that you are so far from using them to fit you for your callings, that you either live idly out of a calling, or else you prefer them before your callings: you have no mind for your work, because your mind is so much upon your play: you have no mind for your home or family, but are weary of your business, because your amusements withdraw your hearts; and you are so far from using them to fit you for any holy duty, that they utterly unfit you, and corrupt your hearts with such a kind of physical delight as makes them more reluctant toward all that is good; so much that many of you even grow so reckless as to hate and scorn it. This is the benefit it brings you.

5. And you cannot but know what a time-wasting sin it is. Suppose the game were ever so lawful; is it lawful to lay out so many hours upon it? as if you had neither souls, nor bodies, nor families, nor estates, nor God, nor death, nor heaven to mind?

6. And how much profaneness, or abuse of others, is in many of your stage-plays! How much wantonness and amorous folly, and representing sin in a manner to entice men to it, rather than to make it hateful, making a joke and mockery of sin; with a great deal more such evil! And your cards and dice are the exercise usually of greed, the occasion of a great deal of idle talk and foolish babble about every throw and every card: and often the occasion of cursing, and swearing, and railing, and hatred of those that win your money; and often it has occasioned fighting, and murder itself. And even your huntings are commonly recreations so costly [606] that the cost that keeps a pack of hounds would keep a poor man's family that is now in want: besides the time that this also consumes.

So that the case is clear, that our gamblers, and licentious, amusement-loving gallants, are a sort of people that have blinded their minds, and seared their consciences, and despise the laws and presence of God, and forget death and judgment, and live as if there were no life to come, neglecting their miserable souls, and having no delight in the word or holy worship of God, nor the forethoughts of eternal joys, and therefore seek for their pleasure in such foolish amusements, and spend those precious hours in these empty pursuits, which, God knows, they had need to spend most diligently, in repenting of their sins, and cleansing their souls, and preparing for another world.

If yet any impenitent gambler or idle time-waster shall reply, I will not believe that my cards, or dice, or plays are unlawful. I use them only to fit me for my duty. What! would you have all men live like hermits or anchorites, without all pleasure? I answer you only by this reasonable request: will you set yourselves as dying men in the presence of God, and the sight of eternity, and provide a true answer to these few questions; even such an answer as your consciences dare stand to at the bar of God?

*Question 1.* Do you not think in your conscience that your Maker, and Redeemer, and his work and service, and your family and calling, and the forethoughts of heaven, are not fitter matters to delight a sober mind than cards or stage-plays? And what can it be but an empty and sinful mind that should make these trifles so pleasant to you, and the thoughts of God and heaven so unpleasant?

*Question 2.* Does not your conscience tell you that it is not to fit you for your calling or God's service that you use these amusements, but only to delight a carnal imagination? Does not conscience tell you that it is more the pleasure than the benefit of it to your soul or body that draws you to it? Do you work so hard or study so hard all the rest of the day as to need so much recreation to refresh you?

*Question 3.* Does not your conscience tell you that if your pleasure-loving imagination were only cured, it would be a more profitable recreation to your body or mind to use some sober exercise for your body, which is confined to its proper limits of time; or to turn to a variety of labor, or studies, than to sit about these idle games?

*Question 4.* Do you think that either Christ or his apostles used stage-plays, cards, or dice; or ever approved such a temper of mind as is addicted to them? Or was not David as wise as you, that took his pleasure in the word of God, and his melodious praise?

*Question 5.* Does not your conscience tell you that your delight is more in your plays and games than it is in God? And that these amusements in no way increase your delight in God at all, but more unfit and indispose you? And yet every blessed man "finds pleasure in obeying the Lord's commands; he meditates on his commands day and night" (Psalm 1:2). And do you do so?

*Question 6.* Do you spend as much time in praying and reading the word of God, and meditating on it, as you do in your amusements and recreations? Indeed, do you not rush through this, and put God off with a few hypocritical, heartless words, that you may be at your amusements, or something which you love better?

*Question 7.* Does not conscience tell you that this precious time might be much better spent in the works that God has appointed you to do? And that your sinful soul has need enough to spend it in far greater matters? Does it become one that has sinned so long, and is so unassured of pardon and salvation, and near another world, and so unready for it, to sit at cards or be hearing a stage-play, when he should be making ready, and getting assurance of his peace with God?

*Question 8.* Would you be found at cards or plays when death comes? If it were this day, would you not rather be found about some holy, or some profitable labor?

*Question 9.* Will it be more comfort to you when you are dying to think of the time which you spent in cards, and plays, and empty pursuits, or that which you spent in serving God, and preparing for eternity?

*Question 10.* Dare you pray to God to bless your cards, and dice, and plays, to the good of your soul or body? Would not your conscience tell you that this would be only a mocking of God, as praying for that which you do not intend, and which your pleasures are unfit for? And yet no recreation is lawful which you may not thus lawfully pray for a blessing on.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[605] Among the Ep. of Bonifac. Mog. there is a council held under Carloman, king of France, which says in the king's name, Necnon et illas venationes et sylvaticas vagationes cum canibus, servis Dei interdiximus. Similiter ut accipitres et falcones non habeant. (And we have also forbidden to the servants of God those huntings and roamings through the woods with dogs. Likewise, that they should not keep hawks and falcons.) And surely these are better than cards and dice, which yet some priests now use too much.

[606] It is one of the Roman laws, 12. tab. Prodigo bonorum suorum administratio interdicta esto. (Let the management of his own goods be forbidden to a spendthrift. *Twelve Tables*.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **Psalm 1:2** (Question 5): Baxter's quotation runs together the "blessed man" of verse 1 with verse 2: "blessed man's delight is in the law of the Lord, and in it he meditateth day and night" (King James: "his delight is in the law of the LORD; and in his law doth he meditate day and night"). We give the NET wording of verse 2 and leave "blessed man" outside the quotation marks as Baxter's framing (the NET's verse 1 has "How blessed is the one"). **The NET changes the sense he leans on:** Baxter's whole question turns on the word "delight" ("your delight is more in your plays ... than it is in God"), and the King James "his delight is in the law of the Lord" answers it word for word. The NET's "finds pleasure in obeying the Lord's commands" keeps the idea of pleasure but loses the repeated word "delight," and it turns "the law" into "obeying the ... commands."
- **calling(s)** (item 4 and Questions 1–2): Baxter's word for one's vocation or ordinary occupation; kept.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): David taking pleasure in the word of God and "his melodious praise" (Question 4), probably the Psalms generally (for example Psalm 119); "seared their consciences" (compare 1 Timothy 4:2); "the bar of God" (the judgment seat).
- **Question 1:** Baxter's sentence has a double negative ("Dost thou not think ... that thy Maker ... are not fitter matters ... than cards"). His meaning is plainly "are fitter matters"; we keep his wording rather than fix his grammar.
- **The question before footnote 605** ("Has God given you such a world of lawful pleasures, ... (which therefore are unlawful):"): Baxter's question ends in a colon, not a question mark; kept as printed.
- **papists:** Baxter's word for Roman Catholics, kept as his. **theologians:** his "divines." **gamblers:** his "gamesters."
- **Reignolds; Albericus Gentilis; Voetius; Amesius; Gataker** (item 3): our identifications, probably right but not checked against Baxter's other works: "Dr. J. Reignolds" is probably John Rainolds of Oxford, whose book against stage-plays came out of a dispute with the civil lawyer Alberico Gentili ("Albericus Gentilis"); Voetius is probably Gisbertus Voetius, the Dutch Reformed theologian; Amesius is probably William Ames; Mr. Gataker is probably Thomas Gataker, who wrote a treatise on lots defending the lawfulness of using lots in play. We have not confirmed the exact title Baxter means by *Treatise against Stage-plays*; it is italicized as a title as he gives it.
- **lusory lots:** kept. "Lusory" means "for play": lots (chance) used in games, as distinct from lots used to divide property or to seek a divine decision. Baxter says even writers who allow lusory lots in principle lay down further conditions that condemn ordinary card and dice playing.
- ***spectacula*, spectacles:** Baxter's Latin with his own English gloss, kept; he means public shows (theater, games), not eyeglasses.
- **The oldest canons of the Church of England, laws of religious princes, the fathers and councils:** Baxter's own claims, kept as his; we have not checked which canons and laws he means.
- **Council under Carloman** (note 605): "Ep. of Bonifac. Mog." is the letters of Boniface of Mainz ("Moguntinus"). The council is probably one of the Frankish reforming councils of the 740s held under Carloman; Baxter calls him "king of France," which is his description (we have not checked the council's exact name or Carloman's title in it).
- **12. tab.** (note 606): the Twelve Tables, the early code of Roman law. Baxter uses the rule that a spendthrift may be barred from managing his own goods to support his point about costly hunting.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): labour, labor; joineth, joins; holy conference, holy conversation; have you not (profitable history, etc.), do you not have; serve your turns, meet your needs; sport(s), amusement(s); conceits, notions; humorous, whimsical; singular divine, eccentric theologian; reputed, considered; abundance of, a great many of; learnedst, most learned; confessedly, admittedly; account (them sinful), consider; extremity of pride, extreme pride; sensual (conceits, fantasy), pleasure-loving; sensual (delight), physical; ponderous, weighty; read but, just read; requisites, requirements; have no mind of, have no mind for; backward to, reluctant toward; insomuch that, so much that; desperate, reckless; never so lawful, ever so lawful; odious, hateful; a sport and mock, a joke and mockery; covetousness, greed; cast (of dice), throw; ofttimes / oft, often; charge (expense), cost; sportful, amusement-loving; vanities / vanity, empty pursuits; vain, empty; toys, trifles; fantasy, imagination; all the day besides, all the rest of the day; countenanced, approved; took up his pleasure, took his pleasure; no way, in no way; undispose, indispose; (delight) on God, (delight) in God; bestow (time), spend; nay, indeed; shuffle this over, rush through this; wouldst thou be, would you be; hadst thou not rather, would you not rather; spentest, spent; darest thou, dare you; this were but, this would be only.
- **Kept as still current:** stage-plays; gallants (fashionable young men); anchorites (religious hermits who lived shut away); railing (abusive talk); wantonness; licentious; impenitent; unstudied (unlearned); unassured; forethoughts; temper of mind; estates.
