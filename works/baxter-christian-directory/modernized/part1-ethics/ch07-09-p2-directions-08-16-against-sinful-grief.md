# Chapter 7: Title 9, Directions 8–16 against sinful grief and trouble

*This range continues Title 9 (against sinful grief and trouble), beginning at the start of Direction 8.*

**Direction 8. Remember your causes of joy as well as your cause of sorrow, that each may have their due, and your joy and sorrow may both be suited to their causes: to which end you must labor for the most exact acquaintance with your own condition that possibly you can attain to. [361]**

If you are yet ungodly, your sorrow must be greater than your joy, or else it will be irrational joy, and pernicious to your souls, and increase your after-sorrow. And you must not overlook so much cause of comfort as is afforded you in God's patience, and the offers of a Savior, and of pardon, and grace, and life in him. If you are truly godly, you must so mourn for sin, and weakness, and needs, and hardships, and afflictions of yourselves and others, as never to forget the invaluable mercies which you have already received, your part in Christ and life eternal, your beginnings of grace, and your reconciliation with God, which allow and command you greatly to rejoice: and remember that no humiliations will excuse you from the observation and acknowledgment of all these mercies.

**Direction 9. Read over all the commands of Scripture that make it your duty to rejoice in the Lord, and exceedingly to rejoice; and make them as much a matter of conscience as other commands of God.**

The same God commands you to rejoice who commands you to hear, and pray, and repent. See Psalm 33:1; Philippians 3:1; 4:4; Romans 5:2; Philippians 3:3; 1 Thessalonians 5:16; 1 Peter 1:6–8; 4:13; Hebrews 3:6; 2 Corinthians 6:10; Romans 12:12; Psalm 32:11; 132:9, 16; Romans 14:17; Psalm 5:11.

**Direction 10. Do not befriend your own excessive sorrows by thinking them to be your duty, nor suspect lawful mirth and joy as if it were a sin, or a thing unbecoming you.**

For if you take your sin for your duty, and plead for it, and your duty for your sin, and plead against it, you are far from the way of amendment and recovery. And yet it is common with an afflicted, weak, impatient soul to fall into liking (though not in love) with their inordinate sorrows, and to justify them, and think that it is their duty continually to mourn. If these sorrows were of God, we would be more reluctant toward them: and if our comfort were not more pleasing to God, our natures would not be so reluctant toward them as they are.

**Direction 11. Love no creature too much, and do not let it grow too sweet and pleasant to you: otherwise you are preparing for sorrow from the creature.**

Love it less, and you will sorrow less. All your grief for hardships and losses, in goods, estate, in children and friends, in reputation, liberty, health, and life, comes from your over-loving them. Value them only as they deserve, and you may easily bear the loss of them. He that makes them his idol or happiness will grieve for the lack of them, or the loss of them, as a man ruined that cannot live without them. But he that has placed his happiness and hopes in God, and values the world no further than it tends to his ultimate end, will no further grieve for the lack of it than as he misses it for that end. "The love of money and coveting after it pierces men through with many sorrows" (1 Timothy 6:10). Notice what you find your heart too much set upon and pleased in, or hoping after, and take it off quickly if you love your peace.

**Direction 12. Learn to be pleased and satisfied in the will of God.**

Trust your heavenly Father, who knows what you need. It is some rebellion or contrariness of our wills to the will of God which causes our inordinate griefs and trouble. Because we cannot bring our wills to his will, nor make our reason stoop to his wisdom, nor think well of his providence unless he will suit it to our notions, and interests, and lusts, therefore so far as we are carnal we are ordinarily displeased and grieved at his ways. If we might have had our own wills about our estates, or names, or children, or friends, or health, or life, we would not have been troubled (for the present). But because it is not our way, but God's way, that is taken, nor our will, but God's will, that is done, therefore we are grieved and discontented, as if his way and will were worse than ours, and God had needed his foolish children to be his counselors, or they could have chosen better for themselves!

**Direction 13. Afflict yourselves no further than God or man afflicts you; but remember, if you think that you have too much already against your wills, how foolish and self-contradicting it is to lay a great deal more willfully upon yourselves. [362]**

Is it slanders or reproach that men afflict you with? Let it be so; that does not touch the heart. Is it poverty, hardships, or losses that God afflicts you with? Let it be so; that does not touch the heart either. Is it loss of children and friends; or is it pain and sickness? I confess these are severe; but yet they do not touch the heart. If they come there it is your doing; and (though there they should come moderately) if they are immoderate, it is your own sinful doing. It is you that grieve, and make the heart ache; God and man only made the flesh ache. If others hurt your bodies, will you therefore vex your minds? Will you pierce through your hearts because they touch your name or goods? If so, remember which part of your sorrow is of their making, and which is of your own: and can you for shame go beg of God or man to ease the grief which you yourselves are causing, and willfully continue it while you pray against it? And why do you lament that which you cause and choose? It is a shame to be willfully your own tormentors.

**Direction 14. Abhor all that tends to take down the power and government of reason (that is, all feebleness and cowardice of mind, and a melancholy, a peevish, emotional disposition): and labor to keep up the authority of reason, and to keep all your passions subject to your wills; which must be done by Christian faith and fortitude.**

If you once come to that childish or distracted pass as to grieve and say, I cannot help it; I know it is sinful and immoderate, but I cannot choose; if you say true, you are out of the reach of counsel, advice, or comfort. You are not to be preached to, nor talked to, nor to be written for: we do not write directions to teach men how to touch the stars, or explain the roughnesses or irregularities of the moon, or the opaque parts of Saturn, or to govern the orbs, or rule the chariot of the sun. If it has become a natural impossibility to you, doctrine can give you no remedy; but if the impossibility is only moral, in the weakness of reason and lack of consideration, it may by doctrine, consideration, and resolution be overcome. You can do more if you will than you think you can. [363] How did you come to lose the command of your passions? Did not God make you a rational creature that has an understanding and will to rule all passions? How do you come to have lost the ruling power of reason and will? You would take it for a disparagement to be told that you have lost the use of your reason; and is it not a principal use of it to rule the passions, and all other inferior, subject powers? You say you cannot choose but grieve! But if one could give you that creature which you lack or desire, then you could choose: you could rejoice if one could restore to you that child, that friend, that estate which you have lost. But God, and Christ, and heaven, it seems, are not enough to cure you: if you must have only them you cannot choose but grieve! And what hearts do you have then, that are thus affected? Should not those hearts be rather grieved for? God will some day make you see that you had more power than you used.

**Direction 15. Observe the harms of excessive sorrow, that you may feel what reason you have to avoid it.**

While you do not know what hurt is in it, you will be the more remiss in your resisting it: I shall briefly name for you some of its unhappy fruits.

*In the margin: The ill effects of sinful grief.*

1. It is a continual pain and sickness of the mind. (This you know by feeling.) 2. It is a destroyer of bodily health and life. For "worldly sadness brings about death" (2 Corinthians 7:10). "A cheerful heart brings good healing, but a crushed spirit dries up the bones" (Proverbs 17:22). 3. It puts the soul out of relish with its mercies, and so causes us to undervalue them, and consequently to be unthankful for them, and not to make good use of them. 4. It destroys the sense of the love of God, and lamentably unfits the soul to love him: and therefore should be abhorred by us were it only for that one effect. 5. It destroys the joy in the Holy Spirit, and unfits us to obey that command of God, Rejoice continually. 6. It contradicts a heavenly mind and conduct, and hinders us from all foretastes of the everlasting joys. [364] 7. It unfits us for the excellent work of praise: who can ascend in the praises of God while grief oppresses and holds the soul captive? 8. It destroys the sweetness of all God's ordinances, hearing, reading, prayer, sacraments; we may force ourselves to use them, but shall have no delight in them. 9. It hinders the exercise of faith, and raises distrust, and sinful doubts and fears, within us. 10. It causes sinful discontents and grumblings at God and man. 11. It makes us impatient, peevish, contrary, angry, and hard to please. 12. It weakens the soul for all that is good, and destroys its fortitude and strength: for it is "the joy of the Lord" that is our strength (Nehemiah 8:10). 13. It hinders us in the duties of our callings: who can do them as they should be done under the dead weight of a disquieted mind? 14. It makes us a grief and burden to our friends, and robs them of the comfort which they should have in and by us. 15. It makes us unprofitable to others, and hinders us from doing the good we might; when we should be instructing, exhorting, and praying for poor sinners, or attending to the church of God, we are all taken up at home about our own afflictions. 16. It makes us a stumbling block and scandal to the ungodly, and hinders their conversion, while the devil sets us before the church doors to keep away the ungodly from a holy life, as men set scarecrows in their fields and gardens to frighten away the birds. 17. It dishonors religion by making men believe that it is a melancholy, vexatious, self-tormenting life. 18. It obscures the glory of the gospel, and thwarts the work of Christ, his Spirit, and ministers, who all come with a message of great joy to all nations; and proclaim good news to the worst of sinners; much more to the sons of God, and heirs of life. 19. It misrepresents God himself, as if we would persuade men that he is a hard and cruel master, that none can please, though they do all through a Mediator upon a covenant of grace; and that it is worse with us since we served him than before; and that he delights in our grief and misery, and is against our peace and joy; and as if there were no joy nor pleasure in his service. Such hideous doctrine do our lives preach of God, when those that profess to fear and seek him live in such immoderate grief and trouble. 20. And it too much pleases the devil, who is glad to torment us here if he may not do it in hell; and especially to make ourselves the executioners upon ourselves when he is restrained; when he can boast and say, Though I may not vex you, I will persuade you to vex yourself. These are the fruits of sinful sorrows.

**Direction 16. Govern your thoughts, and do not allow them to muse and feed on those objects which cause your grief.**

No wonder if your sore is always smarting, when you are always rubbing on it in your thoughts. Of this I spoke more fully just now.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[361] Acts 8:8.

[362] Libenter feras quod necesse est: dolor patientia vincitur. Martin. Dumiens. de Morib. (Bear willingly what is necessary: pain is conquered by patience. Martin of Dumium, *On Morals*.) Tristitiam sin potes, ne admiseris: sin minus: ne ostenderis. Id. ib. (Sorrow, if you can, do not admit; if not, do not show it. The same, same place.)

[363] See Mr. Fenner's book of *Willful Impenitency*.

[364] Even Anaxagoras, a philosopher, could say to one that asked him, Nullane tibi patriæ cura est? Mihi quidem patriæ cura est, et quidem summa: digitum in cœlum intendens. Laert. p. 85. (Do you have no care for your country? "I do indeed care for my country, and that supremely," pointing his finger to heaven. Laertius, p. 85.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). Verses quoted below were fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Title:** Baxter divides Chapter 7 into ten "Titles" (his abbreviation "Tit."), that is, headed divisions, mostly one for each passion. This file continues Title 9, against sinful grief and trouble; it begins at the start of Direction 8 (Directions 1–7 are in the previous file) and ends with Direction 16 (the Title continues after this range). **passions:** Baxter's word for the emotions and affections; kept. **melancholy:** kept (see GLOSSARY.md).
- **Acts 8:8** (note 361): cited only. The NET reads "So there was great joy in that city"; Baxter attaches it to "causes of joy."
- **2 Corinthians 7:10** (Direction 15, item 2): NET wording. The King James "worldly sorrow worketh death" becomes "worldly sadness brings about death." Baxter's whole Title is about "sorrow"; the NET's "sadness" names the same thing, but the verbal link to his word is lost. Flagged.
- **Reference placement:** Baxter prints the references to Proverbs 17:22 (Direction 15, item 2) and 1 Timothy 6:10 (Direction 11) before the quotations; following project convention, they are moved into parentheses after the quotations.
- **Proverbs 17:22** (item 2): NET wording. The King James "A merry heart doth good like a medicine: but a broken spirit drieth the bones" becomes "A cheerful heart brings good healing, but a crushed spirit dries up the bones." Baxter's point (sorrow harms bodily health) still stands; the image of a medicine is lost.
- **Nehemiah 8:10** (item 12): Baxter writes "the joy of the Lord that is our strength," adapting the King James "your strength" to "our." We quote the NET phrase "the joy of the Lord" and keep Baxter's "that is our strength" outside the quotation marks. The NET verse reads "the joy of the Lord is your strength." The source prints "Neh. viii. 10, 13."; the "13." is the next item number, not a verse.
- **1 Timothy 6:10** (Direction 11): Baxter's quotation, "The love of money and coveting after it, doth pierce men through with many sorrows," is his paraphrase of the King James ("the love of money is the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows"). We keep his wording, modernized, in his quotation marks. The NET reads: "For the love of money is the root of all evils. Some people in reaching for it have strayed from the faith and stabbed themselves with many pains." The NET's "pains" for "sorrows" removes the word Baxter leans on for this Title. Flagged.
- **"Rejoice continually"** (item 5): Baxter gives this command without quotation marks or reference; we keep his words. It probably alludes to 1 Thessalonians 5:16 (NET "Always rejoice") or Philippians 4:4 (NET "Rejoice in the Lord always"); this reference is not in Baxter's text.
- **Direction 9 reference list:** converted to modern form; Baxter gives only references. "Psal. cxxxii. 9, 16" is rendered Psalm 132:9, 16.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording; references not in his text): "your heavenly Father, who knows what you need" (Matthew 6:8, 32); "joy in the Holy Spirit" (Romans 14:17); "a message of great joy to all nations" (Luke 2:10); "a hard and cruel master" (compare Matthew 25:24); God needing "his foolish children to be his counselors" (compare Romans 11:34).
- **Word swaps (Baxter's word, then ours):** wants → needs; crosses → hardships; labour → labor; exactest → most exact; make as much conscience of them as of → make them as much a matter of conscience as; befriend not → do not befriend; backward (reluctant) → reluctant toward; still (continually) → continually; else → otherwise; felicity → happiness; want (lack) → lack; man undone → man ruined; mark → notice; crossness → contrariness; conceits → notions; at the present → for the present; wanted (needed) → needed; discontent → discontented; sore (severe) → severe; thither → there; did but make → only made; passionate → emotional (per GLOSSARY.md); asperities or inequalities → roughnesses or irregularities; opacous → opaque; but (only) → only; want of → lack of; sometime → some day; mischiefs → harms; improve (put to good use) → make good use of; undisposeth → unfits; captivate → hold captive; murmurings → grumblings; froward → contrary; clog (a weight that hinders movement) → dead weight; disquiet → disquieted; minding → attending to; stumblingblock → stumbling block; crosseth → thwarts; come upon a message → come with a message; glad tidings → good news; conversation (conduct) → conduct; Holy Ghost → Holy Spirit; suffer → allow; spake → spoke; even now → just now; thee/thyself → you/yourself.
- **Kept:** pernicious, after-sorrow, inordinate, lusts, carnal (unspiritual, ruled by the flesh), ordinances (the appointed means of worship: hearing, reading, prayer, sacraments), doctrine, remiss, disparagement, "come to that pass," "out of relish with" (no longer finding its mercies pleasant), "the orbs" (the heavenly spheres of older astronomy), "the chariot of the sun" (a classical image).
- **Direction 10, last sentence:** Baxter writes that our natures would not be "so backward to them," where "them" seems to refer back to "our comfort" (or perhaps to comforts generally). We keep his "them"; the plural may be a slip.
- **Direction 13:** "(though thither they should come moderately)" means "though they ought to come there [to the heart] only in moderation." Rendered "(though there they should come moderately)," keeping his order.
- **Direction 14:** a "moral" impossibility rests in the will and reason (and so can be overcome), as opposed to a "natural" impossibility, which cannot. Baxter's "If it be become" is rendered "If it has become."
- **Direction 15, item 20:** the source reads "and especially do make ourselves the executioners," which seems to mean "and [is glad] especially to make ourselves the executioners." We render "especially to make"; "do" may be a misprint for "to."
- **Direction 15, item 19:** "since we served him" kept as written (since we began serving him).
- **Direction 16:** "Of this I spoke more fully just now" refers back to an earlier Direction in this Title (probably Direction 7 or earlier, in the previous file); not checked.
- **Martin of Dumium** (note 362): Baxter's "Martin. Dumiens. de Morib." is probably Martin of Braga (also called Martin of Dumium, sixth century), to whom a short moral work *De moribus* was attributed; we have not confirmed the attribution or the passage.
- **Mr. Fenner** (note 363): probably the Puritan William Fenner, who wrote on willful impenitency; we have not confirmed the title or edition.
- **Anaxagoras** (note 364): the Greek philosopher, quoted from Diogenes Laertius ("Laert."), who reports that when asked whether he had no care for his country, he pointed to the sky. Baxter uses it to illustrate a heavenly mind.
