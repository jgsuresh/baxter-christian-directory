# Chapter 2: Direction 5 (part 1 of 2)

**Direction 5. Be very thankful for the great mercy of your conversion: but yet do not overvalue your first degrees of knowledge or holiness, but remember that you are yet but in your infancy, and must expect your growth and ripeness as the consequence of time and diligence.**

You have great reason to be more glad and thankful for the least measure of true grace than if you had been made the rulers of the earth; it being of a far more excellent nature, and entitling you to more than all the kingdoms of the world. See my sermon called *Right Rejoicing*, on those words of Christ, "do not rejoice that the spirits submit to you, but rejoice that your names stand written in heaven" (Luke 10:20). Christ will warrant you to rejoice, though enemies envy you, and complain both at your victory and triumph. If there is "joy in the presence of God's angels" at your conversion (Luke 15:10), there is great reason you should be glad yourselves. If the prodigal's father will insist on having the best robe and ring brought forth, and the fatted calf killed, and the music to attend the feast, that they may eat and be merry (Luke 15:23), there is great reason that the prodigal son himself should have no small share of joy; though his brother complains.

*In the margin: Fear is a cautious, preserving grace.*

But yet take care lest you think the measure of your first endowments to be greater than it is. [52] Grace imitates nature, in beginning, usually, with small degrees, and growing up to maturity by leisurely proceeding. We are not newborn in a state of manhood, as Adam was created. Though those texts that liken the kingdom of God to a grain of mustard seed, and to a little leaven (Matthew 13:31, 33), are principally meant of the small beginnings and great increase of the church or kingdom of Christ in the world; yet it is true also of his grace or kingdom in the soul. Our first stature is but to "yearn like newborn infants for pure, spiritual milk, so that by it you may grow up to salvation" (1 Peter 2:2). Note here that the new birth brings forth but babes, but growth is by degrees, by feeding on the word. The word is received by the heart, as seed into the ground (Matthew 13). And seed does not usually bring forth the blade and fruit to ripeness in a day.

Yet I do not deny but that some men (as Paul) may have more grace at their first conversion than many others have at their full growth. For God is free in the giving of his own, and may give more or less as pleases himself. But yet in Paul himself that greater measure is but his smallest measure, and he himself is capable of increase to the last. And so great a measure at first is as rare as his greater measure at last, in its full growth, is rare, and scarcely to be expected now.

And if God should give a great measure of holiness at first to any now, as possibly he may, yet their measure of gifts is never great at first, unless they had acquired or received them before conversion. If grace finds a man of great parts and understanding, which by study and other helps he had attained before, no wonder if that man, when his parts are sanctified, is able in knowledge the first day; for he had it before, though he had not a heart to use it. But if grace finds a man ignorant, unlearned, and of modest abilities, he must not expect to be suddenly lifted up to great understanding and high degrees of knowledge by grace. For this knowledge is not given now by sudden infusion, as gifts were, extraordinarily, in the primitive church. You need no other proof of this but experience, to stop the mouth of any contradictor. Look about you, and observe whether those that are men of knowledge obtained it by infusion, in a moment? or whether they did not obtain it by diligent study, by slow degrees? though I know God blesses some men's studies more than others. Name one man that ever was brought to great understanding but by means and labor, and slow degrees; or that knows any truth, in nature or divinity, but what he read, or heard, or studied for, as the result of what he read or heard. The person that is proudest of his knowledge must confess that he came to it in this way himself.

*In the margin: How the Spirit illuminates.*

But you will ask, What then is the illumination of the Spirit, and enlightening of the mind, which the Scripture ascribes to the Holy Spirit? Does not our understanding need the Spirit for light, as well as the heart or will for life?

*Answer.* Yes, no doubt; and it is a great and wonderful mercy: and I will tell you what it is. 1. The Holy Spirit, by immediate inspiration, revealed to the apostles the doctrine of Christ, and caused them infallibly to write the Scriptures. But this is not that way of ordinary illumination now. 2. The Holy Spirit assists us in our hearing, reading, and studying the Scripture, that we may come, by diligence, to the true understanding of it; but does not give us that understanding without hearing, reading, or study. "Faith comes from what is heard" (Romans 10:17). It blesses the use of means to us, but blesses us not in the neglect of means. 3. The Holy Spirit opens the eyes and heart of a sinner who has heard, and understood only in theory, the substance of the gospel, so that he may know in a piercing, effective, practical way what before he knew only in theory and without effect; so that the knowledge of the same truth is now become powerful, and, as it were, of another kind. And this is the Spirit's sanctifying of the mind, and principal work of saving illumination; not by causing us to know anything of God, or Christ, or heaven without means; but by opening the heart, that, through the means, it may take in that knowledge deeply, which others have but in theory, and in a dead opinion; and, by making our knowledge clear, and quick, and powerful, to affect the heart, and rule the life. 4. The Holy Spirit sanctifies all that theoretical knowledge which men had before their renewal. All their learning and parts are now made subservient to Christ, and to the right end, and turned into their proper channel. 5. And the Holy Spirit, by sanctifying the heart, possesses it with such a love to God, and heaven, and holiness, and truth as is a wonderful advantage to us in our studies for the attaining of further knowledge. Experience tells us how great a help it is to knowledge to have a constant love, delight, and desire for the thing which we would know. In all these ways the Spirit is the enlightener of believers.

Not observing this direction will have dreadful effects; which I will name, that you may see the necessity of avoiding them.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows the Latin passage in parentheses; it was prepared for this project and is not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check it before relying on fine points.

[52] Laert. says of Cleanthes, Cum aliquando probo illi daretur, quod esset timidus, at ideo inquit, parum pecco. (Laertius says of Cleanthes: when once he was reproached with being timid, he said, "For that very reason I sin little.")

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **Luke 10:20; Luke 15:10:** NET wording. Baxter's "Rejoice not that the spirits are subject to you" is "do not rejoice that the spirits submit to you," and "joy in heaven in the presence of the angels" is "joy in the presence of God's angels."
- **Luke 15:23** (cited only): the prodigal son's feast.
- **"should have no small share of joy"** (paragraph 1): the source reads "should not have the smallest share of joy," which cannot be what Baxter means. His argument is that if the father insists on celebrating, the prodigal son has good reason to share the joy. We have rendered the sense Baxter must mean and flag the slip in the source.
- **1 Peter 2:2 (important):** Baxter quotes the King James, "new-born babes desiring the sincere milk of the word, that ye may grow thereby." The NET reads "yearn like newborn infants for pure, spiritual milk, so that by it you may grow up to salvation." The NET drops "of the word," which Baxter relies on in the next sentence ("growth is by degrees, by feeding on the word"). This is the same issue as in Direction 16 of Part 1. Flagged.
- **Romans 10:17:** NET wording. Baxter's "Faith cometh by hearing" is "faith comes from what is heard."
- **Matthew 13:31, 33** (the mustard seed and the leaven) and **Matthew 13** (the sower): cited only.
- **Unmarked allusion:** "the prodigal's father … the best robe and ring" (Luke 15:22–23).
- **cautious:** Baxter's "cautelous." **margin note:** a heading Baxter placed beside the paragraph: fear is a preserving grace.
- **parts** (paragraph 4): natural abilities and talents. **modest abilities:** Baxter's "mean abilities." **contradictor:** Baxter's "gainsayer," someone who contradicts.
- **infusion** (paragraph 4): the idea that knowledge is poured directly into the mind. Baxter says it is not given that way now. **primitive church:** the earliest church.
- **the illumination of the Spirit** (margin note): Baxter gives five ways the Spirit enlightens: revelation to the apostles, help in study, opening the heart to know truth in a practical way, sanctifying earlier knowledge, and giving love for truth that helps further study.
- **in theory:** Baxter's "notionally." **write:** Baxter's "indite." **piercing:** left as written. **effective, ineffective:** Baxter's "effectual, ineffectual." **renewal:** his "renovation."
- **subservient:** serving as a means, subordinate. **divinity** (paragraph 4): theology, as in Direction 3.
- **that the prodigal's … though his brother complains:** Baxter's "though his brother repine" refers to the elder brother who grumbled (Luke 15:28–30).
- **Cleanthes** (note 52): a Stoic philosopher. The saying is Baxter's illustration that a little fear keeps a person from sin.
- **Right Rejoicing** (paragraph 1): Baxter's own sermon.
