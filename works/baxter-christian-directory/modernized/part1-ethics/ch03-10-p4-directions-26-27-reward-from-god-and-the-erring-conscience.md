# Chapter 3: Grand Direction X (part 4 of 6)

*This section begins at Direction 26 of Grand Direction 10 and breaks off partway through Direction 27 (after the answer to Question 8); the next file continues it.*

**Direction 26. Expect your reward from God alone, and look for ingratitude and abuse from men, or do not be surprised if it happens to you.**

If you are not the servants of men, but of God, expect your payment from him you serve. You do not serve God, indeed, if his reward alone will not content you, unless you also have man's reward. "They have their reward," if, with the hypocrite, you work for man's approval (Matthew 6:2, 5). Expect, especially if you are ministers or others who labor directly for the good of souls, that many will prove your enemies for your telling them the truth; and that if you were as good as Paul, and as tireless in seeking people's salvation, yet the more you love, the less you will (by many) be loved: and those whom he could have wished himself accursed from Christ to save, did hate him, and persecute him, as if he had been the most accursed wretch: a pestilent fellow, and a stirrer-up of sedition among the people, and one who turned the world upside down, were the names they gave him; and wherever he came, "bonds and imprisonment did attend him;" and slander, and reviling, and whipping, and stocks, and vowing his death, are the thanks and requital that he has from those for whose salvation he spared no pains, but did spend and was spent. If you cannot do good on such terms as these, and for those who will requite you like this, and be content to expect a reward in heaven, you are not fit to follow Christ, who was treated worse than all this, by those to whom he showed more love than any of his servants have to show. "Take up your cross, and do good to the ungrateful, and bless those who curse you, and love those who hate you, and pray for those who spitefully use you and persecute you, if you will be the children of God" (Matthew 5).

**Direction 27. Do not make your own judgments or consciences your law, or the maker of your duty; which is but the discerner of the law of God, and of the duty which he makes you, and of your own obedience or disobedience to him.**

There is a dangerous error grown too common in the world, that a man is bound to do everything that his conscience tells him is the will of God; and that every man must obey his conscience, as if it were the lawgiver of the world; whereas, indeed, it is not ourselves, but God, who is our lawgiver. And conscience is not appointed or authorized to make us any duty that God has not made us; but only to discern the law of God, and call on us to observe it: and an erring conscience is not to be obeyed, but to be better informed, and brought to a righter performance of its office.

In pursuing this direction, I shall here answer several cases about doubting.

*Question 1.* What if I doubt whether a thing is a duty and good work or not? must I do it while I doubt? Nay, what if I am uncertain whether it is duty or sin?

*Answer 1.* In all these cases about an erring or doubting conscience, do not forget to distinguish between the being of a duty and the knowledge of a duty: and remember that the first question is, Whether this is my duty? and the next, How I may discern it to be my duty? And that God gives it the being by his law, and conscience is only to know and use it: and that God does not change his law, and our duty, as often as our opinions change about it. The obligation of the law is still the same, though our consciences err in understanding it otherwise. Therefore, if God commands you a duty, and your opinion is that he does not command it, or that he forbids it, and so that it is no duty, or that it is a sin, it does not follow that God does not in fact command it because you think so: otherwise it would be no error in you; nor would it be possible to err, if the thing became true because you think it to be true. God commands you to love him, and to worship him, and to nourish your children, and to obey the higher powers, etc. And do you think you will be released from all these duties, and allowed to be profane, or physical in your indulgence, or to resist authority, or to starve your children, if you can only be blind enough to think that God would have it so? 2. Your error is a sin in itself: and do you think that one sin must authorize another? or that sin can release you from your duty, and cancel the law? 3. You are a subject to God, and not a king to yourself; and therefore you must obey his laws, and not make new ones.

*Question 2.* But is it not every man's duty to obey his conscience?

*Answer.* No: it is no man's duty to obey his conscience in an error, when it contradicts the command of God. Conscience is only a discerner of God's command, and is not at all to be obeyed strictly as a commander; but it is to be obeyed in a larger sense, that is, to be followed wherever it truly discerns the command of God. It is our duty to put aside our error, and seek the cure of it, until we attain it, and not to obey it.

*Question 3.* But is it not a sin for a man to go against his conscience?

*Answer.* Yes: not because conscience has any authority to make laws for you; but because by implication you go against God. For you are bound to obey God in all things; and when you think that God commands you something, and yet you will not do it, you disobey formally, though not materially. The matter of obedience is the thing commanded: the form of obedience is our doing the thing because it is commanded; when the authority of the commander causes us to do it. Now you reject the authority of God, when you reject that which you think he commands, though he did not.

*Question 4.* Since the form of obedience is the being of it, and gives it its name, which the matter does not do without the form, and there can be no sin that is not against the authority of God, which is the formal cause of obedience, is it not then my duty to follow my conscience?

*Answer.* 1. There must be an integrity of causes, or concurrence of all necessaries, to make up obedience, though the lack of any one will make a sin. If you will be called obedient, you must have the matter and form, because the true form is found in no other matter; you must do the thing commanded, because of the authority of him who commands it. If it may be called really and formally obedience when you err, yet it is not that obedience which is acceptable; for it is not any kind of obedience, but obedience in the thing commanded, that God requires. 2. But indeed as long as you err sinfully, you are also lacking in the form as well as the matter of your obedience, though you intend obedience in the particular act. It is not only a willful opposing, and positive rejecting of the authority of the commander, that is formal disobedience; but it is any privation of due subjection to it; when his authority is not so regarded as it ought to be; and does not so powerfully and effectually move us to our duty as it ought. Now this formal disobedience is found in your erring conscience; for if God's authority had moved you as it should have done, to diligent inquiry and use of all appointed means, and to the avoiding of all the causes of error, you would never have erred about your duty. For if the error had been perfectly involuntary and blameless, the thing could not have been your particular duty, which you could not possibly come to know.

*Question 5.* But if it is a sin to go against my conscience, must I not avoid that sin by obeying it? Would you have me sin?

*Answer.* You must avoid the sin by changing your judgment, and not by obeying it; for that is only to avoid one sin by committing another. An erring judgment is neither obeyed nor disobeyed without sin; it can make you sin, though it cannot make you duty; it ensnares, though it does not oblige. If you follow it, you break the law of God in doing what he forbids you. If you forsake it and go against it, you reject the authority of God, in doing what you think he forbids you. So there is no attaining to innocence any other way, but by coming first to know your duty, and then to do it. If you command your servant to weed your corn, and he mistakes you, and verily thinks that you told him to pull up the corn, and not the weeds; what now should he do? Shall he follow his judgment, or go against it? Neither, but change it, and then follow it; and to that end inquire further of your mind, until he is better informed: and no other way will serve the purpose.

*Question 6.* Since no man who errs knows or thinks that he errs, (for that is a contradiction,) how can I put aside that opinion, or strive against it, which I take to be the truth?

*Answer.* It is your sin that you take a falsehood to be a truth. God has appointed means for the cure of blindness and error, as well as other sins; or else the world would be in a miserable case. Come into the light, with due self-suspicion, and impartiality, and diligently use all God's means, and avoid the causes of deceit and error, and the light of truth will at once show you the truth, and show you that before you erred. In the meantime sin will be sin, though you take it to be duty, or no sin.

*Question 7.* But since he who knows his master's will and does not do it shall be beaten with many stripes; and he who does not know it, with few; is it not my duty chiefly to avoid the many stripes, by avoiding sinning against my conscience or knowledge?

*Answer.* 1. Your duty is to avoid both; and if both were not sinful, they would not both be punished with stripes. 2. Your conscience is not your knowledge when you err, but your ignorance. Conscience, as it signifies the faculty of knowing, may be said to be conscience when it errs; as reason is reason, in the faculty, when we err. And conscience, as to an erring act, may be called conscience, so far as there is any true knowledge in the act: as a man is said to see, when he misjudges of colors, or to reason, when he argues amiss. But, so far as it errs, it is no conscience in act at all; for conscience is science, and not nescience. You sin against your knowledge when you sin against a well-informed conscience, but you sin in ignorance when you sin against an erring conscience. 3. And if the question is not, what is your duty, but, which is the smaller sin, then it is true that, *cæteris paribus*, it is a greater sin to go against your judgment than to follow it. But yet, other differences in matter and circumstances may be an exception against this rule.

*Question 8.* But it is not possible for every man at once to know all his duty, and to avoid all error about his duty. Knowledge must be got in time. All men are ignorant in many things: should I not then in the meantime follow my conscience?

*Answer.* 1. Your ignorance is culpable, or not culpable. If it is not culpable, the thing which you are ignorant of is not your duty. If culpable, (which is the case supposed,) as you brought yourself to that difficulty of knowing, so it will remain your sin until it is cured; and one sin will not authorize another. And all that time you are under a double command; the one is, to know, and use the means of knowledge; and the other is, to do the thing commanded. So that however long you remain in error you remain in sin, and are not under an obligation to follow your error, but first to know, and then to do the contrary duty. 2. And as long as you keep yourselves in a necessity, or way of sinning, you must call it sin as it is, and not call it duty. It is not your duty to choose a lesser sin before a greater; but to refuse and avoid both the lesser and the greater. And if you say you cannot, yet remember that it is only your sin that is your inability, or your inability is sinful. But it is true that you are most obliged to avoid the greatest sin: therefore all that remains in the resolving of all such cases is but to know, of two sins, which is the greatest.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). All verses below were fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Start of range:** the range opens at the heading of Direction 26 (in the source, "Direct. XXVI.") and ends at the end of the answer to Question 8 on Direction 27; Direction 27 continues in the next file. The Direction headings' first sentences are Baxter's bold run-in headings, set off as bold headings per the style guide (the source joins them to the text with "--").
- **Matthew 6:2, 5:** Baxter quotes "Verily you have your reward." The NET has "I tell you the truth, they have their reward!" (6:2) and "Truly I say to you, they have their reward!" (6:5). We quote the NET phrase, "They have their reward." The NET's third person ("they") replaces Baxter's "you," which he applies directly to the reader; the sense is the same.
- **"Bonds and imprisonment did attend him":** quoted by Baxter without a reference. It is probably his rendering of Acts 20:23 (not in Baxter's text; we have not confirmed it as his source). The NET there reads "imprisonment and persecutions are waiting for me." Baxter's wording is kept, modernized, in his quotation marks.
- **Unmarked allusions in the Paul passage (kept in Baxter's wording):** "wished himself accursed from Christ" (Romans 9:3; NET: "I could wish that I myself were accursed—cut off from Christ"); "a pestilent fellow, and a mover of sedition" (Acts 24:5; NET: "troublemaker, one who stirs up riots"); "turned the world upside down" (Acts 17:6; the NET has "stirred up trouble throughout the world," so the "upside down" image is lost); "spend and be spent" (2 Corinthians 12:15); "the more you love, the less you will be loved" (2 Corinthians 12:15, NET: "If I love you more, am I to be loved less?"). "Whipping, stocks" probably refer to Paul's sufferings in Acts and 2 Corinthians (we have not confirmed which passages); Baxter gives no references.
- **Matthew 5 (end of Direction 26):** Baxter's quotation is a paraphrase and blend, with only "Matt. v." as his reference; it appears to draw on Matthew 5:44-45, and perhaps Luke 6:27-28, 35. We kept his wording, modernized, in his quotation marks. The NET of Matthew 5:44-45 reads "love your enemy and pray for those who persecute you, so that you may be like your Father in heaven." Luke 6:27-28 (NET): "Love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, bless those who curse you, pray for those who mistreat you." The point Baxter leans on, "be the children of God," differs from the NET's "be like your Father in heaven" (Matthew 5:45); the KJV has "children of your Father." Flagged. The NET at Luke 6:35 has "you will be sons of the Most High."
- **Luke 12:47-48 (Question 7):** Baxter's "knoweth his master's will and doth it not, shall be beaten with many stripes; and he that knoweth it not, with few" is an unmarked allusion to this passage. The NET reads "will receive a severe beating" and "a light beating." Baxter's "many stripes" and "few" (KJV) turn on counting the blows; the NET speaks of severity instead. His argument in Question 7 uses only the contrast between more and less punishment, so the sense holds, but his wording is kept as his paraphrase.
- **Word choices (Baxter's word, then ours):** recompence to payment; unthankful/unthankfulness to ungrateful/ingratitude; approbation to approval; despitefully to spitefully; "unwearied" to tireless; "mover of sedition" to stirrer-up of sedition; "worse used" to treated worse; "whom" for "that" (those whom he could have wished); maketh/telleth etc. to modern verbs; insnare to ensnare; "interpretatively" to "by implication"; "impotency" to "inability"; "apprehending" to "understanding"; "imparities" to "differences"; "warrant" (to justify) to "authorize"; "discharged" to "released"; "disannul" to "cancel"; "sensual" to "physical in your indulgence"; "famish" to "starve"; "presently" to "at once"; "serve the turn" to "serve the purpose"; "denominateth" to "gives it its name"; "lay by" to "put aside"; "&c." to "etc."; "how long soever" to "however long."
- **Kept as Baxter's words:** "integrity of causes," "concurrence," "privation," "effectually," "formally / materially," and "righter" are kept (here "integrity" means completeness, and "privation" means the lack of something due). "Nay" in Question 1 is kept as Baxter's word ("No, rather, what if I am uncertain..." is its sense).
- **Technical terms:** *formally / materially* (in form / in matter), and "the formal cause of obedience," are scholastic terms (Aristotelian: the form is what makes a thing what it is, the matter is what it is made of). Baxter uses them in Questions 3 and 4. We keep "form" and "matter," and "formally" and "materially" as he has them, with "formal" in "formal cause" and "formal disobedience." "Cases about doubting" is the old "cases of conscience" idea (practical questions of duty).
- **"Science, and not nescience" (Question 7):** *science* here means knowledge (Latin *scientia*), and *nescience* means lack of knowledge. Baxter's play on conscience (*con-scientia*, "with knowledge") is kept in English.
- **Latin:** *cæteris paribus* (English: all other things being equal.) Kept as printed.
- **"Corn" (Question 5):** kept; in British usage of Baxter's day it means grain generally.
- **Grammar:** "Quest. I." and "Answ. I." are rendered *Question 1* and *Answer 1*; Baxter numbers only the first answer. Numerals inside Answers (1., 2., 3.) are Baxter's own and are kept inline.
- **Direction 26 heading:** Baxter's text reads "or wonder not if it befall you"; we render "or do not be surprised if it happens to you."
