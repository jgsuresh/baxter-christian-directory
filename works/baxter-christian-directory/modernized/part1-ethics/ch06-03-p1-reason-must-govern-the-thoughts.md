# Chapter 6: Title 3, Directions 1-11 (reason's rule over the thoughts)

**Title 3. Directions how to make good thoughts effectual: or, general directions for meditation.**

Here some directions are preparatory, and some about the work itself.

**Direction 1. Be sure that reason maintains its authority in the command and government of your thoughts; and that they are not left masterless to fancy, and passion, and objects, to carry them whichever way they please.**

Diseased, melancholy, and crazed persons have almost no power over their own thoughts. They cannot command them to what they would have them exercised about, nor call them off from anything they run out upon; but they are like an unruly horse that has a weak rider, or has thrown the rider; or like a masterless dog, that will not go or come at your command. Whereas our thoughts should be at the direction of our reason, and the command of the will, to go and come off as soon as they are told. As you see a student can rule his thoughts all day; he can assign them what they shall meditate on, and in what order, and how long; so can a lawyer, a physician, and all sorts of men about the matters of their arts and callings. And so it should be with a Christian about the matters of his soul. All rules of direction are to little purpose with them whose reason has lost its power in governing their thoughts. If I tell a man that is deeply melancholy, "Thus and thus you must order your thoughts," he will tell me that he cannot; his thoughts are not in his power. If you would give ever so much he is not able to refrain from thinking of that which is his disturbance, nor to command his thoughts to that which you direct him, nor to think but as he does, even as his disease and trouble moves him. And what good will precepts do to such? Grace, and doctrine, and exhortation work by reason and the commanding will. If a holy person could manage his practical, heart-raising meditations just as orderly, and constantly, and easily as a carnal, greedy preacher can manage his thoughts in studying the same things, for carnal ends (to make a gain of them or to win applause), how happily would our work go on! And is it not sad to think that carnal ends should do so much more than spiritual, about the same things?

**Direction 2. Carefully avoid the disease of melancholy; for that dethrones reason, and disables it from ruling the thoughts.**

Madness wholly disables; but melancholy disables only in part, according to the measure of its strength; and therefore leaves some room for advice.

**Direction 3. Beware of sloth and negligence of the will, whereby the directions of reason will be unexecuted, for lack of resolution and command; and so every temptation will carry away the thoughts.**

A lazy coachman will let the horses go whichever way they please, because he will not strive with them; and will break his neck to save his labor. If, when you feel unclean or worldly thoughts invade your minds, you will not give your wills the alarm, and rise up against them, and resolutely command them out; you will be like a lazy person that lies in bed while he sees thieves robbing his house, and will let all go rather than he will rise and make resistance (a sign that he has no great riches to lose, or else he would stir for it). And if you see your duty, on what your thoughts should be employed, and will not resolutely call them up, and command them to their work, you will be like a sluggard that will let all his servants lie in bed, as well as he, because he will not speak to call them. You see by daily experience that a man's thoughts are much in the power of his will, and made to obey it. If money and honor, or the delight of knowing, can cause a wicked preacher to command his own thoughts on good things, as said above; you may command yours to the same things, if you will just as resolutely exercise your authority over them.

**Direction 4. Do not accustom your thoughts to take their liberty and be ungoverned; for habit will make them headstrong, and not regard the voice of reason; and it will make reason careless and remiss.**

Habit and custom have great power on our minds; where we are used to going, our path is plain; but where there is no habit, there is no way. Where the water usually runs there is a channel. It is hard ruling those that are used to being unruly. If habit will do so much with the tongue (as we find in some that are used to cursing, and swearing, and speaking emptily, and in others that are used to speaking soberly and religiously; in some that by practice can speak well in discussion, preaching or praying many hours together, when others that do not practice it can do almost nothing that way), why may it not have much power with the thoughts?

**Direction 5. Be careful lest the senses and appetite grow too strong, and master reason; for if they do, they will at once dispossess it of the government of the thoughts, and will brutishly usurp the power themselves.**

As, when a rebellious army deposes a king, they do not only cast off the yoke of subjection themselves, but dissolve the government as to all other subjects, and usually usurp it themselves, and make themselves governors. If once you are servants to your fleshly appetites and sense, your thoughts will have other work to do, and another way to go, when you call them to holy and necessary things; especially when the enticing objects are at hand. You may as well expect a clod to ascend like fire, or a pig to delight in temperance, as a glutton, or drunkard, or fornicator, to delight in holy contemplation. Reason and flesh cannot both be the governors.

**Direction 6. Keep passions down, that they do not depose reason from the government of your thoughts.**

I told you before how they cause evil thoughts; and as much will they hinder good. Four passions are special enemies to meditation: 1. Anger. 2. Perplexing grief. 3. Disturbing fear. 4. But above all, excess of pleasure in any worldly, fleshly thing. Who can think that the mind is fit for holy contemplation when it flames with wrath, or is distracted with grief and care, or trembles with fear, or is drunk with pleasure? Grief and fear are the most harmless of the four; yet all hinder reason from governing the thoughts.

**Direction 7. Evil habits are another great hindrance of reason's command over our thoughts; labor therefore diligently for the cure of this disease.**

Though habits do not necessitate, they strongly incline; and when every good thought must go against a strong and constant inclination, it will weary reason to drive on the soul, and you can expect only small success.

**Direction 8. Urgent and oppressing business does almost compel the thoughts; therefore avoid as much as you can such urgencies, when you would be free for meditation.**

Let your thoughts have as little diverting matter as possible, at those times when you would have them entire and free for God.

**Direction 9. Crowds and bad company are no friends to meditation; choose therefore the quietness of solitude when you would do much in this.**

As it is bad studying in a crowd, and unseasonable before a multitude to be at secret prayer (except some short quick prayers); so is it as unfit a season for holy meditation. The mind that is fixedly employed with God, or about things spiritual, needs all possible freedom and peace, to retire into itself, and withdraw itself from unrelated things, and seriously attend to its greater work.

**Direction 10. Above all, beware of sinful interests and designs; for these are the garrison of Satan, and must be battered down before any holy thoughts can take hold.**

He that is set upon a design of rising, or of growing rich, has something else to do than to entertain those sober thoughts of things eternal, which are destructive of his carnal design.

**Direction 11. The impediments of reason's authority being thus removed, distinguish between your occasional and your set, ordinary course of thoughts.**

And as your hands have their ordinary, set course of labor, and every day has its employment which you expect beforehand, so let your thoughts know where their proper channel is, and their every day's work; and let holy prudence assign proportionate time and service for them. What a life will that man live that has no known course of labor, but only such as he is by chance called to! His work must necessarily be uncertain, various, unprofitable, and comfortless, and next to none. And he that has not a set course of employment for his thoughts will have them do him little service. Consider first how much of the day is usually to be spent in common business; and then consider whether it is such as takes up your thoughts as well as your hands, or such as leaves your thoughts at liberty: as a lawyer, a physician, a merchant, and most tradesmen must employ their thoughts to the well-doing of their work; and these must be the more desirous of a seasonable, free hour for meditation, because their thoughts must be otherwise employed all the rest of the day. But a weaver, a tailor, and some other tradesmen, and day-laborers may do their work well, and yet have their thoughts free for better things a great part of the day; these must contrive an ordinary way of employment for their thoughts when their work does not require them; and they need no other time for meditation. The rest must entertain some short, occasional meditations, intermixed with their business; but they cannot then have time for more solemn meditation (which differs from the other as a set prayer from a short quick prayer; or a sermon from an occasional short talk). They that have more time for their thoughts must beforehand prudently consider how much time it is best to spend in meditation, for the increase of knowledge, and how much for the exercise of holy affections, and on what subject, and in what order; and so to know their ordinary work.

## Translator's notes

- **Title:** Baxter's "_Tit._" is his abbreviation for "Title," meaning a heading or main division. Chapter 6 has six such Titles; this file begins Title 3 (Directions 1–11). The next file continues with Direction 12.
- **No Scripture quotations** fall in this range, so no NET text was used here.
- **Direction headings:** in each Direction, Baxter's first sentence is set in bold as the heading, with the rest following in the same paragraph as Baxter printed it (as in the other Chapter 6 files). No words were moved between sentences.
- **melancholy** (Directions 1–2): kept as Baxter's word (see GLOSSARY.md): a lasting low, fearful, or brooding state, understood in his day as partly bodily. It is not swapped for a modern diagnosis.
- **crazed** (Direction 1): kept. In Baxter's English it means mentally broken or disordered, not wildly excited as it often does now.
- **Madness** (Direction 2): Baxter's "Distraction," which in his day meant insanity, not inattention. He contrasts it, as total loss of reason's rule, with melancholy, which takes away that rule only in part.
- **strength** (Direction 2): Baxter's "prevalency." **for lack of:** his "for want of." **Beware of / Be careful lest:** his "Take heed of / Take heed lest."
- **thrown** (Direction 1): his "cast." **told:** his "bid." **assign:** his "appoint" (Directions 1 and 11; in 11 "appoint out"). **refrain from:** his "forbear." **ever so much:** his "never so much" (that is, however much). **just as orderly:** his "but as orderly" (where "but" means "only" or "even"); likewise **just as resolutely** in Direction 3 for "but as resolutely."
- **arts and callings** (Direction 1): kept; "arts" here means skilled crafts and professions, and "calling" is Baxter's word for a person's vocation or occupation.
- **greedy** (Direction 1): his "covetous," per the project conventions. **carnal:** kept (unspiritual, ruled by the flesh).
- **as said above** (Direction 3): his "as aforesaid," pointing back to the "carnal, covetous preacher" of Direction 1.
- **Direction 4:** Baxter plays on "use" in its old sense of habit or practice ("Use not your thoughts," "use will make them," "where we use to go," "the water useth to run," "that use it not"). We render it as "Do not accustom," "habit," "are used to," "usually," and "practice" by context; the wordplay on "use" is lost in modern English. **speaking emptily:** his "speak vainly." **discussion:** his "conference." **have much power with:** his "much prevail with" (the conventions render "prevail" by "powerful").
- **pig** (Direction 5): his "swine." **brutishly:** kept, meaning like a brute beast. **a clod to ascend like fire:** in the older physics Baxter assumes, earth naturally falls and fire naturally rises.
- **Keep passions down** (Direction 6): his "Keep under passions." **Perplexing grief:** kept; "perplexing" here means distressing or troubling, not merely puzzling. **I told you before:** a cross-reference to Baxter's earlier treatment of how passions cause evil thoughts (presumably Title 1 of this chapter; we have not checked the exact place).
- **compel** (Direction 8): his "necessitate" ("doth almost necessitate the thoughts," that is, nearly forces where the thoughts go). In Direction 7 "necessitate" is kept. **as possible:** his "as may be."
- **bad company; bad studying** (Direction 9): his "ill company; ill studying." **unfit:** his "unmeet." **needs:** his "had need of." **withdraw itself from unrelated things:** his "abstract itself from alien things." **attend to:** his "intend" (direct itself to).
- **short quick prayers** (Directions 9 and 11): Baxter's "ejaculations," his term for brief prayers darted up to God in the middle of other business.
- **holy thoughts can take hold** (Direction 10): his "holy cogitations can take place," where "take place" means gain ground or take effect, not "happen." **rising:** kept; rising in the world, advancement in rank or position. **designs:** kept, meaning schemes or plans.
- **set** (Direction 11): his "stated," meaning fixed or regular. **expect beforehand:** his "fore-expect." **by chance:** his "accidentally." **must necessarily:** his "must needs." **comfortless:** his "uncomfortable," meaning yielding no comfort. **free hour:** his "vacant hour." **talk:** his "discourse."
