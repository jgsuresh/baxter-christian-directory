# Chapter 4: Directions against Pride, and for Humility (Direction 1, the nature of pride and humility)

**Directions against Pride, and for Humility.** [196]

Pride, being reputed the great sin of the devil, by which he fell, is, in the name and general notion of it, infamous and odious with almost all; but the nature of it is so much unknown, and the sin so undiscerned by the most, that it is commonly cherished while it is commonly spoken against. Therefore the chief directions for the conquering of it are those that are for the full discovery of it; for when it is seen it is shamed, and to shame it is to destroy it.

**Direction 1. Understand aright the nature of pride, that you may neither ignorantly retain it, nor oppose your duty as supposed to be pride.**

Here I shall tell you, 1. What pride is, and what commandment it is against; and what humility is, which is its contrary. 2. Some seemings or appearances like pride, which may make men censured as proud for that which is not pride. 3. The counterfeits of humility, which may make a proud man seem to himself or others to be humble.

*In the margin: What pride is.*

I. Pride is an inordinate self-exalting, or a lifting up ourselves above the state or degree appointed us. It is called ὑπερηφανια, because it is an appearing to ourselves, and a desire to appear to others, above what we are, or above others of our quality. It is a branch of selfishness, and contains man-pleasing as before described, and produces hypocrisy, and is its origin and life. It contains in it these following acts or parts: 1. A will to be higher or greater than God would have us be. 2. An overvaluing of ourselves, or esteeming ourselves to be greater, wiser, or better than indeed we are. 3. A desire that others should think of us, and speak of us, and use us, as greater, or wiser, or better than we are. 4. An effort or seeking to rise above our appointed place, or to be overvalued by others. 5. An ostentation of our inordinate self-esteem in outward signs of speech or action. Every one of these is an act of pride. The three first are the inward acts of it in the mind and will, and the two last are its external acts.

*In the margin: Against what commandment.*

As the love of God and man are the comprehensive duties of the Decalogue, expressed most in the first and last commandment, but yet extending themselves to all the rest; so selfishness and pride (which is a principal part of it) are the opposite sins, forbidden principally in the first and last commandment, as contrary to the love of God and man, but so as it is contrary to the rest. They are sins against the very relation itself, that God and man stand in to us, and not only against a particular law: they are against the very constitution of the kingdom of God, and not only against the administration: it is treason or idolatry against God, and a setting up ourselves in some part of his prerogative: and it is a monstrous exuberance in the body, and a rising of one member above and so against the rest, either superiors (and so against the fifth commandment) or equals (against the rest).

*In the margin: What humility is.*

Humility is contrary to pride; and therefore consists, 1. In a contentedness with that degree and state which God has assigned us. 2. In low thoughts of ourselves, esteeming ourselves no greater, wiser, or better than we are. 3. In a willingness and desire that others should not think of us, or speak of us, or use us, as greater, or wiser, or better than we are; that they should give us no more honor, praise, or love than is our due; the excess being but a deceit or lie, and an abuse of us and them. 4. In the avoiding of all inordinate aspiring efforts, and a contented exercise of our assigned offices, and doing the lowliest works of our own places. 5. In the avoiding of all ostentation or appearance of that greatness, wisdom, or goodness which we do not have; and fitting our speeches, clothing, provisions, furnishings, and all our deportment and behavior to the lowliness of our abilities, and place, and worth. This is the very nature of humility. The more particular signs I shall open afterwards.

*In the margin: The inward seemings of pride that are not it.*

II. Pride, lying in the heart, is often misjudged of by others, that see but the outward appearances, and sometimes by the person himself, that does not understand the nature of it. The inward appearances that are mistaken for pride, and are not it, are such as these: 1. When a man in power and government has a spirit suitable to his place and work: this is not pride, but virtue. 2. When natural strength and vigor of spirits expels faintheartedness; especially when faith, beholding God, expels all inordinate respect to men, and fear of all that they can do, this is not pride, but Christian magnanimity and fortitude; and the contrary is not humility, but weakness, and faintheartedness, and cowardice. 3. When a wise man knows in what measure he is wise, and in what measure other men are ignorant, or erroneous; and when he is conscious of his knowledge, and delighted and pleased in it through the love of truth, and thankful to God for revealing it to him, and blessing so far his studies and efforts; all this is mercy and duty, and not pride. For truth is lovable and delightful in itself. And he that knows must needs know that he knows; as he that sees perceives by seeing that he sees. And if it is a fault to know that I know, it must be a fault to know at all. But some knowledge is necessary and irresistible, and we cannot avoid it: and that which is good must be valued, and we must be thankful for it. Humility does no more require that a wise man think his knowledge equal with a fool's, or ignorant man's, than that a healthy man take himself to be sick. 4. When a wise man values the useful knowledge which God has given him, above all the glory and empty things of the world, which are indeed of lower worth, this is not pride, but a due estimation of things. [197] 5. When a wise man desires that others were of his mind, for their own good, and the propagating of the truth, this is not pride, but charity and love of truth: else preachers were the proudest men, and Paul had done ill in laboring so much for men's conversion, and saying to Agrippa, "I pray to God that whether in a short or a long time not only you but also all those who are listening to me today could become such as I am, except for these chains" (Acts 26:29). 6. When an innocent man is conscious of his innocence, and a holy person is conscious of his holiness, and assured of his state in grace, and rejoices in it, and is thankful for it, this is not pride, but an excellent privilege and duty. If angels rejoice at the conversion of a sinner, (Luke 15) the sinner has reason to rejoice himself: and if it is a sin to be ungrateful for our daily bread, much more for grace and the hope of glory. 7. When we value our good name, and the honor that is indeed our due, as we do other outward common mercies, not for themselves, but so far as they honor God, or tend to the good of others, or the promoting of truth or piety among men, desiring no more than is indeed our due, nor overvaluing it as that which we cannot spare, but submitting it to the will of God, as that which we can be without; this is not pride, but a right estimation of the thing.

*In the margin: The outward appearances of pride that are not it.*

The outward seemings which are often mistaken for the signs and fruits of pride by others, are such as these: 1. When a magistrate or other governor maintains the honor of his place, which is necessary to his successful government, and lives according to his degree. When princes, and rulers, and masters, and parents, keep that distance from their subjects, and servants, and students, and children, which is fitting and needed to their good, it is usually misjudged to be their pride.

2. When a sinner is convinced of the necessity of holiness in a time and place where it is rare, and unbelief or profaneness and ungodliness is the common way, the necessary singularity of such a one in giving up himself to the will of God, is commonly charged on him as his pride; as if he were proud that cannot be contented to be damned in hell for company with the most; or to despise salvation if most despise it, and to forsake his God when most forsake him, and to serve the devil when most men serve him. If you will not swear, and be drunk, and gamble, and spend your time, even the Lord's day, in worthlessness and indulgence in physical pleasure, as if you were afraid of being saved, and as if it were your business to work out your damnation, the world will call you proud and singular, and "they are astonished when you do not rush with them into the same flood of wickedness, and they vilify you" (1 Peter 4:4). [198] You shall quickly hear them say, What! will you be wiser than all the town? What a saint, what a holy precisian is this! When Lot was grieved for the filthiness of Sodom, they scorn him as a proud critic: "This man came to live here as a foreigner, and now he dares to judge us!" (Genesis 19:9). And what did they think of Noah that walked with God in so great singularity, when all the world was drowned in (and for) their wickedness? When David "humbled his soul with fasting," they turned it to his "reproach" (Psalm 69:10; 35:13). Especially when any of the servants of Christ do press towards the highest degree of holiness, they shall be sure to be accounted proud and hypocrites. And yet they do not accuse that child or servant of pride who excels all the rest in pleasing them, and doing their work; nor do they take a sick man to be proud, if he is more careful than others to recover his health. But he that will do most for heaven, and most carefully avoids sin and hell, and is most serious in his religion, and most industrious to please his God, this man shall be accounted proud.

3. He that will not forsake his God and betray the truth, and wound his conscience by willful sin, but will do as Daniel and the three confessors did (Daniel 6:3), and answer as they answered, will be accounted proud. But it is no pride to prefer God before men, and to fear damnation more than imprisonment or death. The army of martyrs did not in pride prefer their own judgments before their superiors that condemned them; but they did it in obedience to God and truth, when that was revealed to babes, which was hidden from the wise, and prudent, and great, and noble of the world.

4. When those that are faithful to the honor of Christ's sovereignty, dare not approve of papal usurpations, against his laws, and over his church, and the consciences of his subjects, they shall by the popish usurpers be called proud and despisers of government; as if a usurper of the kingly power should call us proud, because we dare not consent to his pride; or call us traitors, for not being traitors as he is himself.

5. When a man that has the sense of the matters of God and men's salvation upon his heart, is zealous and diligent to teach them to others, and if he is a minister, is fervent and laborious in his ministry, he is called proud, as one that must needs have all men of his mind: though compassion to souls, and ability to teach, and preaching urgently in season and out of season, is his necessary duty required of God. And what is the ministry for, but to change men's minds, and bring them to the full obedience of the truth?

## Baxter's notes

The Latin below is reproduced as in the source. A draft English translation follows it in parentheses; the translation was prepared for this project and is not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check it before relying on fine points.

[196] Of this subject read the preface to my book *Of Self-denial*, and chapters 41 to 51.

[197] Duplex est humilitas: una lucida solum et non fervida: quæ ex ratione potius quam ex charitate exercetur.--Altera quæ lucida fervidaque simul est, ex charitate magis quam ex ratione exercetur; non tamen citra rationem.--Humilitas enim (ut et reliquæ virtutes) opus est voluntatis. Nam sicut virtutes per rationem cognoscimus, ita per dilectionem nobis sapiunt. Thauler. flor. c. 7, p. 103, 104. (English: Humility is twofold: one is bright only and not fervent, which is exercised from reason rather than from charity.--The other, which is bright and fervent together, is exercised from charity more than from reason; yet not apart from reason.--For humility (as also the other virtues) is a work of the will. For as we know the virtues through reason, so through love they are savory to us. Tauler [as the source spells it, "Thauler"], *flor.* chapter 7, pp. 103, 104.)

[198] See Plutarch Tract. How a man may praise himself without incurring blame? He that is blamed and suffers reproach for well-doing, is justifiable if he praises himself, etc. p. 304.

## Translator's notes

- **Heading and structure:** "Directions against Pride, and for Humility" is the heading of a new section of Chapter 4; the first Direction's heading sentence is set in bold, and the "Here I shall tell you..." sentence that follows it is kept as body text. Baxter's "I." and "II." (the first two of his three heads) stay as numerals.
- **Margin notes:** Baxter's "Pride what." and "Humility what." are rendered "What pride is." and "What humility is."; the other margin notes follow the source wording. The two reworded ones change word order slightly, in the margin only.
- **Greek (ὑπερηφανια):** kept as printed; transliterated *hyperēphania*, the Greek word for pride, formed from "above" and "appear/show." Baxter's point is its sense of "appearing above."
- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible, fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Acts 26:29:** NET wording. Baxter's own quotation of the verse in this passage was the King James phrase "both almost and altogether such as I am, except these bonds." The NET has "whether in a short or a long time ... could become such as I am, except for these chains." The NET reads "short or long time," so Baxter's "almost and altogether" is not in it; his point (Paul wanted others to share his state for their good) is unchanged.
- **Luke 15:** Baxter gives only the chapter and paraphrases (angels rejoice at a sinner's conversion); no quotation. NET Luke 15:7, 10 speak of "joy in heaven" and "in the presence of God's angels" over "one sinner who repents." Baxter's "conversion" is kept.
- **1 Peter 4:4:** NET wording ("they are astonished when you do not rush with them into the same flood of wickedness, and they vilify you"). Baxter had the King James-style phrases "think it strange that you run not with them to excess of riot, speaking evil of you." The sense (the world is surprised and abusive at your not joining) is the same.
- **Genesis 19:9:** NET wording. Baxter's "This one fellow came in to sojourn, and he will needs be a judge" is the King James; "sojourn" becomes "came to live here as a foreigner." Baxter's "controller" is rendered "critic" here (one who finds fault and judges).
- **Psalm 69:10; 35:13 (David's fasting):** Baxter's words "humbled his soul with fasting" and "reproach" are the King James wording and are **not** in the NET. NET Psalm 69:10 reads "I weep and refrain from eating food, which causes others to insult me," and NET Psalm 35:13 reads "When they were sick, I wore sackcloth, and refrained from eating food." In keeping with the project rule, Baxter's wording stays, modernized, in his quotation marks. The NET has no "humbled my soul" phrase, which Baxter leans on (fasting as humbling).
- **Daniel 6:3:** Baxter's reference is kept. NET Daniel 6:3 says that Daniel distinguished himself above the other supervisors; it does not clearly match "Daniel and the three confessors" answering as they did. The confessors' story (Daniel 3) and Daniel's own (Daniel 6:10 and following) might be meant. We have not confirmed which Baxter intended and keep his number.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "walked with God" (Genesis 5:24; 6:9); Noah and the flood (Genesis 6-7); "revealed to babes, which was hidden from the wise and prudent" (Matthew 11:25; Luke 10:21); "in season and out of season" (2 Timothy 4:2); "work out your damnation" echoes "work out your salvation" (Philippians 2:12). These are not marked in Baxter's text.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): "spoke against" to "spoken against"; "original" (of hypocrisy) to "origin"; "endeavour" to "effort"; "mean thoughts" to "low thoughts"; "meanest works" to "lowliest works"; "meanness of our parts" to "lowliness of our abilities"; "apparel" to "clothing"; "furniture" to "furnishings"; "redundancy" to "excess"; "exuberancy" to "exuberance"; "command" to "commandment" (fifth); "pusillanimity" to "faintheartedness"; "amiable and delectable" to "lovable and delightful"; "unresistible" to "irresistible"; "sound man" to "healthy man"; "unthankful" to "ungrateful"; "innocency" to "innocence"; "scholars" to "students"; "meet and needful" to "fitting and needed"; "infidelity" to "unbelief" (the project's decision for "infidel"); "game" (verb) to "gamble"; "vanity" (the empty pursuits) to "worthlessness"; "sensuality" to "indulgence in physical pleasure"; "instantly" to "urgently"; "aptness to teach" to "ability to teach"; "common road" to "common way"; "wilful" to "willful."
- **Technical or kept terms:** *inordinate* (excessive or out of proper order); *man-pleasing* (seeking to please people; Baxter says "as before described" in an earlier section we have not checked); *ostentation* (showy display); *prerogative* (a ruler's special right); *magnanimity* (greatness of spirit); *singularity* (standing out from the crowd); *precisian* (a scornful word for a strict, scrupulous person, here for a Puritan); *confessors* (those who confess their faith at risk of suffering); *usurpation* (taking power not one's own); *Decalogue* (the Ten Commandments). Baxter's "first and last commandment" (love to God, and not coveting) is his reading of the ten.
- **"popish usurpers" and "papal usurpations":** Baxter's polemic against Rome, kept as he wrote it.
- **"hypocrisy ... is its original and life":** the "its" refers to hypocrisy's source being pride. The sentence is kept as written.
- **"oppose your duty as supposed to be pride":** kept as written; it means "oppose a duty because it is mistaken for pride."
- **"damned in hell for company with the most":** kept as written ("for company" means "to keep company with").
- **"men's conversion":** conversion in Baxter's sense, turning to God.
- **"Lord's day":** Sunday.
- **Footnote 196:** *Of Self-denial* is Baxter's own book; we have not checked the chapters.
- **Footnote 197:** the author is probably the Tauler whom Baxter names, but we have not identified him or the edition; "flor." is not expanded. The Latin is as printed; "Thauler" is Baxter's spelling. The translation is rendered loosely at "sapiunt" (literally "taste").
- **Footnote 198:** Plutarch is probably cited for his essay on praising oneself without giving offense; we have not confirmed the title or page.
