# Chapter 1, Part 1: Direction 2

**Direction 2. Especially labor first to understand the true nature of a state of sin and a state of grace.**

It is likely you will say that all are sinners; and that Christ died for sinners; and that you were regenerated in your baptism; and that for the sins that you have committed since then, you have repented of them, and therefore you hope they are forgiven. [18]

But wait a moment, man, and understand the matter well as you go; for it is your salvation that lies at stake. It is very true that all are sinners: but it is as true that some are in a state of sin, and some in a state of grace; some are converted sinners, and some unconverted sinners; some live in sins inconsistent with holiness (which therefore may be called mortal), others have none but infirmities which are consistent with spiritual life (which in this sense may be called venial); some hate their sin, and long to be perfectly delivered from it, and others so love it that they are loath to leave it. And is there no difference, do you think, between these?

It is as true also that Christ died for sinners (or else where would our hope be?); but it is true also that he died to "save his people from their sins," [19] (Matthew 1:21), and to bring them from "darkness to light and from the power of Satan to God" (Acts 26:18), and "to set us free from every kind of lawlessness and to purify for himself a people who are truly his, who are eager to do good" (Titus 2:14), and "that unless a man is born again, and converted, and becomes like a little child (in humility and beginning the world anew), he cannot enter into the kingdom of heaven" (John 3:3, 5; Matthew 18:3), and that even he that died for sinners will at last condemn the workers of iniquity, and say, "Depart from me, you accursed, into the eternal fire" (Matthew 25:41), "I never knew you" (Matthew 7:23).

It is very true that you were sacramentally regenerated in baptism, and that he that believes and is baptized shall be saved, and all that are the children of promise, and have that promise sealed to them by baptism, are regenerate. The ancients taught that baptism puts men into a state of grace; that is, that all that sincerely renounce the world, the devil, and the flesh, and are sincerely given up to God the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, according to the covenant of grace, and profess and seal this by their baptism, shall be pardoned, and made the heirs of life. But as it is true that baptism thus saves, so it is as true, [20] that it is not "the washing off of physical dirt" that will suffice, but "the pledge of a good conscience to God" (1 Peter 3:21); and that "unless a person is born of water and spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God" (John 3:5); and that Simon Magus and many another have had the water of baptism that never had the Spirit, but still remain "bitterly envious and in bondage to sin," having "no share or part in this matter," their hearts not being "right before God" (Acts 8:13, 21, 23). And nothing is more sure than that, for all his baptism, "if anyone does not have the Spirit of Christ, this person does not belong to him" (Romans 8:9); and that if you have his Spirit, you "do not walk according to the flesh but according to the Spirit"; and have your "outlook shaped by the things of the Spirit," not by the things of the flesh; and are "alive to God," and as "dead to the world" (Romans 8:1, 5–8, 10, 13, 14). Whether all that were baptized are such as these, when they come of age, judge for yourselves.

It is true also that if you truly repent, you are forgiven: but it is as true that true repentance is the very conversion of the soul from sin to God, and does not leave any man in the power of sin. It is not for a man, when he has had all the pleasure that sin will yield him, to wish then that he had not committed it (which he may do then at little cost), and yet to keep the rest that are still pleasant and profitable to his flesh; like a man that throws away the bottle which he has drunk empty, but keeps the one which is full; or as men sell off their barren cows, and buy milk cows in their place: this kind of repentance is a mockery, and not a cure for the soul. If you have true repentance, it has so far turned your heart from sin that you would not commit it if it were to be done again, though you had all the same temptations; and it has so far turned your heart to God and holiness that you would live a holy life if it were all to be done again, though you had the same temptations as before against it (because you do not have the same heart). This is the nature of true repentance; such a repentance indeed is never too late to save; but I am sure it never comes too soon.

Note now, I beg you, what a state of sin, and what a state of holiness is.

He that is in a state of sin has habitually and predominantly a greater love for some pleasures, or profits, or honors of this world than he has for God, and for the glory which he has promised; he prefers, and seeks, and holds (if he can) his fleshly prosperity in this world above the favor of God and the happiness of the world to come. His heart is turned from God to the creature, and is principally set on things on earth. Thus his sin is the blindness, and madness, and faithlessness, and idolatry of his soul, and his forsaking of God, and his salvation, for a worthless thing. It is to his soul what poison, and death, and sickness, and lameness, and blindness are to his body: it is such dealing with God as that man is guilty of toward his dearest friend or father, who would hate him and his company, and love the company of a dog or toad much better than his; and obey his enemy against him: and it is like a madman's dealing with his physician, who seeks to kill him as his enemy because he crosses his appetite or will in order to cure him. Think of this well, and then tell me whether this is a state to be continued in. This state of sin is something worse than a mere thoughtless act of sin in one that otherwise lives an obedient, holy life.

On the other side, a state of holiness is nothing else but the habitual and predominant devotion and dedication of soul, and body, and life, and all that we have, to God; [21] and esteeming, and loving, and serving, and seeking him above all the pleasures and prosperity of the flesh; making his favor, and everlasting happiness in heaven, our end, and Jesus Christ our way, and referring all things in the world to that end, and making this the aim, purpose, and business of our lives. It is a turning from a deceitful world to God; and preferring the Creator to the creature, and heaven to earth, and eternity to an inch of time, and our souls to our perishable bodies, and the authority and laws of God, the universal Governor of the world, to the word or will of any man, however great; and a subjecting our sensory faculties to our reason, and advancing this reason by divine revelation; and living by faith, and not by sight: in a word, it is a laying up our treasure in heaven, and setting our hearts there, and living in a heavenly way of life, setting our affections on the things above, and not on the things that are on earth; and a rejoicing in hope of the glory to come, when pleasure-seekers have nothing but transitory, brutish pleasures to rejoice in.

This is a state and life of holiness: when we persuade you to be holy, we persuade you to no worse than this; when we commend a life of godliness to your choice, this is the life that we mean, and that we commend to you. And can you understand this well, and yet be unwilling to have it? It cannot be. Do but know well what godliness and ungodliness, what grace and sin are, and the work is almost done.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced exactly as in the source. A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[18] Pœnitenti optimus est portus, mutatio consilii. Cic. Phil. 12. (For one who repents, the best harbor is a change of purpose. Cicero, *Philippics*, 12.)

[19] Bonum gratiæ unius hominis majus est quam bonum naturæ totius universi. Aquin. 12. q. 113. art. 9. (The good of grace in one man is greater than the good of the nature of the whole universe. Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae*, I–II, question 113, article 9.)

[20] Quicquid Deo gratum dignumque offertur, de bono thesauro cordis defertur. Intra nos quippe est quod Deo offerimus, omne viz. acceptabile munus: Ibi timor Dei----ibi confessio, ibi largitas, ibi sobrietas, ibi paupertas spiritus, ibi compassio, &c. Potho Prumiens. de Domo Dei, 1. 2. (Whatever is offered to God as pleasing and worthy is brought out of the good treasury of the heart. For what we offer to God is within us, namely every acceptable gift: there is the fear of God ---- there confession, there generosity, there sobriety, there poverty of spirit, there compassion, etc. Potho of Prüm, *On the House of God*, book 2.) De regno Dei quod intra nos est meditamur vanitates et insanias falsas, dum interioribus animæ virtutibus, in quibus regnum Dei consistit, privati, ad exteriora quædam studia ducimur, et circa corporales exercitationes quæ ad modicum utiles esse videntur, occupamur, fructus spiritus, qui sunt charitas, pax, gaudium, &c. intus minime possidemus, et exterius quarundum consuetudinum observantias sectamur; in exercitiis tantum corporalibus quæ sunt jejunia, vigiliæ, asperitas seu vilitas vestis, &c. regulam nobis vivendi quasi perfectam statuentes. Idem ibid. (Concerning the kingdom of God, which is within us, we dwell on empty and false follies, while, deprived of the inner virtues of the soul in which the kingdom of God consists, we are led to certain outward pursuits and are busied with bodily exercises that seem useful only a little; the fruits of the Spirit, which are love, peace, joy, etc., we hardly possess within, and outwardly we pursue the observance of certain customs; setting up for ourselves, in bodily exercises only, such as fasts, vigils, and rough or cheap clothing, etc., a rule of living as if it were perfect. The same, same place.)

[21] Nulla religio vera est, nisi quæ virtute et justitia constat. Id. ibid. (No religion is true except that which consists in virtue and justice. The same, same place.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **Composite paraphrase** (paragraph 3): the quotation "that except a man be born again, and converted, and become as a little child … he cannot enter into the kingdom of heaven" is Baxter's blend of John 3:3, 5 and Matthew 18:3, not a quotation of either verse. We kept his wording, modernized, and cited both.
- **"gall of bitterness, and bond of iniquity"** (paragraph 4): Baxter's King James wording of Acts 8:23. The NET reads "bitterly envious and in bondage to sin."
- **"alive to God" and "dead to the world"** (paragraph 4): these two phrases are Baxter's summary, not quotations from Romans 8. Compare Romans 6:11 and Galatians 6:14. The other quoted phrases in that sentence are from Romans 8:4–5 in the NET.
- **1 Peter 3:21:** the 1846 edition prints "1 Pet. ii. 21," a misprint. The words Baxter quotes about "the answer of a good conscience toward God" are from 3:21, so we corrected the reference.
- **regenerated … in baptism** (paragraph 1): Baxter, writing as an Anglican-trained Puritan, treats baptism as the ordinary sign and seal of new birth, but not as a guarantee of it. He spells out his own qualifications in paragraph 4.
- **mortal / venial** (paragraph 2): Baxter borrows these terms from the Catholic tradition and redefines them. "Mortal" sins are those inconsistent with holiness; "venial" are the infirmities of a living believer.
- **it is likely** (paragraph 1): Baxter's "it is like." **lies at stake:** Baxter's "lieth at the stake." **loath:** his "loth."
- **faithlessness** (paragraph 7): Baxter's "perfidiousness," meaning betrayal of trust.
- **sacramentally** (paragraph 4): kept; it means "by means of the sacrament (baptism)."
- **sensory faculties** (paragraph 8): Baxter's "sensitive faculties" (see the note on "sensory appetite" in the presuppositions).
- **a heavenly way of life** (paragraph 8): Baxter's "heavenly conversation," meaning conduct.
- **pleasure-seekers** (paragraph 8): Baxter's "sensualists."
