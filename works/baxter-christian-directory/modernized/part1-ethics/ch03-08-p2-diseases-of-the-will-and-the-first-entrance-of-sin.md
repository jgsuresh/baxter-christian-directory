# Chapter 3: Grand Direction VIII (part 2 of 5)

*This section begins partway through the second point of Baxter's Direction II ("Know what sin is, in its nature or intrinsic evil"), in the middle of Baxter's list of how sin diseases each faculty of the soul, just after his treatment of the diseases of the understanding. It ends partway through the same list; the next file continues it.*

*In the margin: Romans 8:5-7.*

But as the goodness is of chief regard in the object, so the discerning of the truth about good and evil is the chief office of the understanding. And therefore its low esteem of God, and glory, and grace, and its mistaken esteem of physical pleasure, and worldly prosperity, wealth, and honor, is the principal malady of the mind.

(2.) The diseases of the will are in its inclination and its acts. 1. An inordinate inclination to the pleasing of the physical appetite and imagination, and to all carnal baits and temporal things that tend to please it, and inordinate acts of desire accordingly. 2. An irrational backwardness to God, and grace, and spiritual good, and a refusal or *nolition* in act accordingly. These are in the will, 1. Because it has become much subject to the sensory appetite, and has debased itself, and contracted, by its sinful acts, an inclination to physical pleasure, the flesh having the dominion in a corrupted soul. 2. Because the intellect, being also corrupted, often misleads it by overvaluing transient things. 3. Because the will has become destitute (in its corrupted state) of the power of divine love, or an inclination to God and holy things, which should countermand the seduction of carnal objects. 4. And the understanding is much destitute of the light that should lead them higher. 5. Because the rage of the corrupted appetite is still seducing it. Mark therefore, for the right understanding of this, our greatest malady:

1. That the will never desires evil as evil, but as a carnal or a seeming good. 2. Nor does it hate good as good, but as a seeming evil, because God and grace do seem to be his enemies, and to hurt him, by hindering him from the good of carnal pleasure which he now prefers. 3. Indeed, at the same time that he loves evil as it pleases the flesh, he has naturally, as a man, some averseness to it, so far as he perceives it to be evil: and when he hates God and holiness as evil, for hindering him from his carnal pleasure, he naturally loves them, so far as he perceives them to be good. So that there is some love to God and good, and some hatred to evil, in the ungodly; for while man is man, he will have naturally an inclination to good as good, and against evil as evil. 4. But the perception of sensory good is the strongest in him, and the perception of spiritual good is weakest; and therefore the will, receiving a greater impression from the carnal appetite and mind than from the weak perceptions of spiritual good, is more inclined to that which indeed is worst; and so carnal things have gained the dominion, or chief commanding interest, in the soul. 5. Note also that sin receives its formal character, or moral evil, first in the will, and not in the intellect or sensory appetite: for it is not sin until it is positively or privately, immediately or mediately, voluntary. But the first motions toward sin are not in the will, but in the sensory appetite; though there, at first, it is not formally sin. 6. Note that neither intellect, object, appetite, nor sense necessitates naturally the will to sin, but it remains the first in the sin and guilt.

It is a matter of great difficulty to understand how sin first entered into the innocent soul; and it is of great importance, because an error here is of dangerous consequence. Two sorts seem to me to make God so much the necessitating cause of Adam's first sin (and so of all sin) that it was as naturally impossible for Adam to have refrained from it, according to their doctrine, as to have conquered God: 1. Those who assert the Dominican, immediate, physical, predetermining pre-motion (which no created power can resist). 2. And those who say the will acts as necessitated by the intellect in all its acts (and so is necessitated in all its omissions); and that the intellect is necessitated by objects (as, no doubt it is, unless as its acts are *sub imperio voluntatis*); and all those objects are caused and disposed of by God. But it is certain that God is not the cause of sin; and therefore this certainty overrules the case against these tenets.

At present it seems to me that sin entered in this way: 1. Sense perceived the forbidden thing. 2. The appetite desired it. 3. The imagination thought on its desirableness yet further. 4. The intellect conceived of it (truly) as good, by a simple apprehension. 5. The will accordingly willed it by a simple complacency or volition. Thus far there was no sin. But, 6. The will here adhered to it too much, and took in it an excess of complacency, when it had power to do otherwise: and here sin began. 7. And so when the thoughts should have been called off; 8. And the intellect should have thought of God and his command, and proceeded from a simple apprehension to the comparing act, and said, The favor of God is better, and his will should rule, it omitted all these acts, because the will omitted to command them; indeed, and hindered them. 9. And so the intellect was next guilty of a *non-renuo*,--I will not forbid or hinder it (and the will accordingly). 10. And next of a positive deception, and the will of consent to the sin, and so it being "full grown, it gives birth to death" (James 1:15).

If you say that the will's first sinful adhesion in the sixth instance could not be unless the intellect first directed it to do so, I deny that, because the will is the first principle in men's actions *quoad exercitium*, though the intellect is the first as to specification. And therefore the will could suspend its exercise and its stirring up of the mind. In all this I go upon common principles: but I leave it to further inquiry; 1. How far the sensory appetite may move the faculty of bodily motion without the will's command, while the will does not forbid? And whether reason is not given to man as the rider to the horse, not to enable him to move, but to rule his motion: so that as the horse can go if the rider does not hinder, so the sensory appetite can cause the actions of eating, drinking, thinking, speaking in a pleasure-loving way, if reason does but drop asleep, or not hinder. 2. And so whether in the first sin (and ordinarily) the sensory appetite, imagination, and passion are not the active mover, and the rational powers first guilty only by omitting their restraining government, which they were able to have exercised? 3. And so, whether sin is not (ordinarily) a brutish motion, or a voluntary unmanning of ourselves, the rational powers in the beginning being guilty only of omission or lack of restraint; but afterward brought over to serve the sensory appetite actively? 4. And so, whether the will, which is the *principium actus quoad exercitium*, was not the first in the omission? The intellect having before said, This must be further considered, the will did not command that further consideration, when it could and should?

However, if it is too hard for us to trace our own souls in all their motions, it is certain that the will of man is the first subject of moral good and evil; and uncertainties must not make us deny what is certain.

The reader who understands the importance and consequence of these points, I am sure, will pardon me for this interruption with these difficult controverted points (which I purposely avoid where I judge them not very needful in order to the defense or clearing of the plainer common truths): and as for others, I must bear their censure.

The degree of sinfulness in the will lies in a stiffness and obstinacy, a tenaciousness of deceitful temporal good, and an eagerness after it; and stubborn averseness to spiritual good, as it is against that temporal fleshly good. This is the will's disease. [99]

(3.) The sinfulness of the memory is in its retentiveness of evil, or things hurtful and prohibited; and its looseness and neglect of better, spiritual, necessary things. If this were only because things present have the natural advantage of making a deeper impression on the imagination, and things unseen and absent have the disadvantage, it would then be but a natural, innocent infirmity; or if in sickness, age, or weakness, all kind of memory equally decay. But it is plain that if the Bible is open before our eyes, and preaching is in our ears, and unseen things have the advantage of their infinite greatness, and excellence, and concern to us, yet our memories are like walls of stone to anything that is spiritual, and like walls of wax, on which you may write anything, of that which is secular or evil. Note here also that the faultiness of the memory is only sinful so far as it is voluntary: it is the will where the sin is as in its throne, or chief subject. Because men love carnal things, and do not love spiritual things, therefore it is that they think about, and understand, and remember the one, and not the other. So that it is but as imperate, and participatively, that the memory is capable of sin.

(4.) The sinfulness of the imagination consists in its readiness to think of evil, and of common earthly things, and its unaptness to think of anything that is holy and good; and when we do force ourselves to holy thoughts, they are disorderly, confused, unskillfully managed, with great averseness.--Here also voluntariness is the life of the sin.

(5.) The sin of the affections, or passions, consists in this:--That they are too easily and violently moved by the sensory interest and appetite; and are habitually prone to such carnal, inordinate motions, running before the understanding and will (some of them), and soliciting and urging them to evil; and resisting and disobeying the commands of reason and the will: but dull and backward to things spiritually good, and to execute the right dictates of the mind and will.

(6.) The sin of the sensory appetite consists in its inordinate rage or immoderateness toward its object, which causes it to disobey the commands of reason, and to become the great inciter of rebellion in the soul; violently urging the mind and will to consent to its desires. Materially this depends much on the temper of the body; but formally this also is sinful so far as (positively or privately, mediately or immediately) it is voluntary. To have an appetite simply for the object of appetite is no sin; but to have a diseased, inordinate, unruly appetite is a sin, not primarily in itself considered, but as it is voluntary, as it is the appetite of a rational free agent who has thus disordered the frame of his own nature.

(7.) The sin of the exterior parts, tongue, hand, eyes, feet, etc., is only in act, and not in habit, or at least the habits are weak and subject to the will. And it is in the execution of the sinful desires of the flesh, and commands of the will, that it consists. These parts also are not the primary subject of the guilt, but the will, which either positively puts them upon evil, or does not restrain them when it ought; and so they are guilty but participatively and secondarily, as the other imperate faculties are: it is not good or evil merely as it is the act of tongue or hand, but as it is the act of the tongue, or hand, of a rational free agent (agreeable or disagreeable to the law). If a madman should speak blasphemy, or should kill, or steal, it would be no further sin than as he had voluntarily contracted the ill disposition which caused it while he had the use of reason. If a man's hand were held and forced by another to do mischief utterly against his will, it is the sin of the chief agent, and not of the involuntary instrument. But no force totally excuses us from guilt that leaves the act to our rational choice. He who says, Take this oath, or I will kill you, or torment you, uses force as a temptation which may be resisted, but does not constrain a man to swear: for he leaves it to his choice whether he will swear, or die, or be tormented; and he may and ought to choose death rather than the smallest sin. The will may be tempted, but not constrained.

## Translator's notes

- **Range begins mid-list:** the first paragraph finishes Baxter's point (1.) on the diseases of the understanding; the section's first sentence ("But as the goodness...") continues from the previous file.
- **James 1:15:** Baxter quotes the King James, "finished, brought forth death." The NET reads "Then when desire conceives, it gives birth to sin, and when sin is full grown, it gives birth to death." We use the NET for the extent he quotes ("full grown, it gives birth to death"). Baxter's reference is not printed in the source; the reference is ours and not in his text. NET flag: the sense is close, but "full grown" (NET) is not quite "finished" (KJV), and his ten-step account of sin's entrance leans on the image of a process reaching completion.
- **In the margin, Romans 8:5-7:** Baxter's sidenote is a reference only. We have not quoted the verses.
- **sensory, sensitive (appetite):** Baxter's "sensitive," meaning belonging to the senses. **physical pleasure / indulgence in physical pleasure:** Baxter's "fleshly pleasure," "sensual inclination" (his "sensual inclination" becomes "inclination to physical pleasure"); "speaking sensually" becomes "speaking in a pleasure-loving way."
- **fantasy:** Baxter's "fantasy" (the faculty that forms and holds images; we have rendered it "imagination," which he also uses as the separate item (4.) "imagination"; in his psychology the two are close, and we have not confirmed whether he means them to be distinct here). The phrase "the sensory appetite, imagination, and passion" renders his "sensitive appetite, fantasy, and passion."
- **nolition:** kept. The act of the will refusing, the opposite of volition. Baxter glosses it himself ("a refusal ... in act").
- **formality (Baxter), formal character (ours):** in scholastic language, what makes a thing the kind of thing it is. "Formally" and "materially" (items 5 and (6.)) are kept as his technical contrasts: the "matter" is the bodily temper, the "form" is the voluntariness that makes it sin.
- **complacency:** kept. In his usage, "simple complacency" is a plain liking or approval, not smugness.
- **mind (verb):** in (3.), "they mind, and understand, and remember" is kept as Baxter wrote it; "mind" means pay attention to.
- **intellect, understanding, will, appetite:** his faculty-psychology terms, kept.
- **Dominican, immediate, physical, predetermining pre-motion:** Baxter's description of a view he argues against, held (he says) by the Dominicans; "physical" here means acting on the will as a real cause, not "bodily." We make no claim of our own about who held it; this is Baxter's characterization.
- **imperate, participatively:** Baxter's scholastic terms, kept as he wrote them in items (3.) and (7.). "Imperate" describes a faculty that acts at the command of the will; "participatively" means by sharing in the will's guilt rather than being the primary subject of it. **voluntariness:** kept.
- **Latin kept in the text** (draft translations): *sub imperio voluntatis* (under the command of the will); *non-renuo* (I do not refuse; Baxter glosses it "I will not forbid or hinder it"); *quoad exercitium* (as to exercise, that is, as to whether and when an act is done); *principium actus quoad exercitium* (the principle of the act as to its exercise). "As to specification" is his English phrase for what the intellect settles: which kind of act it will be.
- **unmanning:** left as written (making ourselves less than human or rational).
- **brutish:** like an animal's.
- **The madman and the forced oath** (item (7.)): left as Baxter has them; they are his illustrations of voluntariness.
- **Misprint in the source:** footnote 99 prints "nee" for "nec" in the first line (kept as printed). Cicero's reference is Baxter's.
- **Adam's first sin:** Baxter says plainly this is his tentative view ("at present it seems to me") and invites further inquiry; we keep that hedging as written.
- **Other swaps:** inclination "to the pleasing of the fleshly appetite and fantasy" becomes "physical appetite and imagination"; "perceiveth" and "apprehendeth" become "perceives"; "apprehension" is kept as a technical term in "simple apprehension" and becomes "perception" in item 4 of the first list ("the apprehension of sensitive good"); "misesteem" becomes "mistaken esteem"; "disesteem" becomes "low esteem"; "cogitations" becomes "thoughts"; "minded God" becomes "thought of God"; "locomotive faculty" becomes "faculty of bodily motion"; "privation" (of restraint) becomes "lack"; "subserve" becomes "serve"; "interposition" becomes "interruption"; "defence" becomes "defense"; "tenets," "overvaluing," "infirmity," "secular," "execution," and "exterior" are kept.
