# Chapter 9: Laying the hand on the Bible and kissing it in oaths (objections answered)

*This file begins partway through Baxter's answer to the question whether it is lawful to lay the hand on the Bible and kiss it when taking an oath (under Title 2, against profane swearing), in the middle of his answers to the first objection.*

*Object.* But Pareus, etc., on Genesis 24:2, says, *Non absque superstitione fit cum super crucifixum aut codicem Evangelii digitis impositis juratur, ut fit in Papatu*. *Answer.* 1. But that same act which *in Papatu* is superstitious because of superstitious notions and ends, is not so in all others that have none such. 2. It is no new thing to be quick in accusing our adversaries: but Pareus does not add a syllable of proof; and if he had, it must have been such as did not touch us, or else invalid.

*Object.* Some good men have had scruples about it. *Answer.* 1. Ten thousand to one such have not had scruples about it. 2. They are not our gods nor law. 3. The Quakers and the old Anabaptists (and they say Origen) had scruples about, indeed, condemned all swearing, or all imposed oaths. And if we avoid all as sin that some good men have had scruples about, we shall make superstition a great part of our religion: and when on the same grounds we have only practiced all as duty that some good men have taken for duty, we shall quite outdo the papists. He that reads Bede, Boniface, and an abundance of such pious writers, will soon see that godly or fanatical religious persons, dreams, visions, strict opinions, confident assertions, and credulous believing of one another, with the hope of turning such things to account against pagans and Jews, for Christianity, brought in almost all the legends and superstitions of the papists.

II. *Object.* Our common-law commissions, that give authority to examine persons, direct it to be done *supra sacramenta sua per sancta Dei evangelia fideliter præstanda*: and in the form of administrations in ecclesiastical courts the words are, *Ad sancta Dei evangelia rite et legitime jurati*: whether these forms do not imply that in their first use (at least) persons either swore by the evangelists or offended in that mode of swearing; and our common law calls it a corporal oath, from touching the book.

*Answer.* 1. To know the sense of our present law it is not necessary that we know the sense of the first users of the form. For the law is not now the law of the king that first made it (he has no law that has no government), but the law of the king that now reigns, and bears his sense. 2. To justify our obedience to a law, it is not necessary that we prove every phrase in that law to be fitly expressed. 3. But examine it well, and test whether it is not also fit and laudable.

1. There are three things joined together in the oaths in question: 1. An assertory testimony, or a promise. 2. An oath. 3. An imprecation. The assertory testimony here is the first thing intended; and the oath and imprecation are only as a means to make that testimony or promise valid. 2. The published doctrine of England, in the Thirty-nine Articles, the book of ordination, etc., is that the holy Scriptures contain all things necessary to salvation, as being God's law or rule of our faith and life. All our duty to God is there commanded; all the promises on which we hope are there contained; all the punishments which the perjured or any sinner must feel and should fear, are there threatened. Therefore, 3. The laying on of the hand and kissing the book is an action directly related to the imprecation, and not to the oath, but only by consequence, as the imprecation is subservient to the oath, as the oath is to the assertion. So that this is the plain paraphrase of the whole: I do believe that God the Ruler of all the world is the Judge of secrets which are above man's judgment, the Searcher of hearts, and the hater and avenger of perjury, according to this his holy word by which he governs us; and to this God I appeal as to the truth of this my testimony, consenting myself to lose all the benefit of his promises to be just, and to bear all the punishment here threatened to the perjured, if I lie.

And what could be said more fitly, 1. To acknowledge the Protestant doctrine that the Scripture is God's perfect word; that the evil to be feared, and the good to be hoped for, is all there contained, and is all the fulfilling of that word? 2. And to put the word in its due subordination to God? And our ordinary form of swearing shows this, So help you God, and the contents of this book. Whether you will call this swearing upon or by the gospel, or call it a corporal oath, or a spiritual oath, is only *de nomine*, and is nothing to the matter thus truly described. *Sacramentum* signifies the oath itself, and *Ad sancta evangelia* is a fit phrase: or if *super sacramenta* signifies the two sacraments of the gospel, it can mean no more than, As one that by the reception of the sacrament does profess to believe this gospel to be true, I do renounce the benefits of it, if I lie; and in this sense it has been some men's custom to receive the sacrament when they would solemnly swear.

III. *Object.* Some seem to object against kissing the book, as having the greater appearance of giving too much to it, or putting some adoration on it; and because this ceremony of kissing is held to be of later date than laying on the hand.

*Answer.* The ceremony signifies that I love and approve the gospel, and place the hope of my salvation in it. And the public doctrine of the kingdom cited before shows us a full exposition of what we ascribe to it. But as some scrupulous brethren in Scotland gratify the papists by rejecting the oath of supremacy, which is the most thorny hedge against them, and this while they cry out against popery; so others would gratify the papists by suggesting that we give too much to the Bible, and adore it; when the very sum of England's Protestantism is their just ascribing to the holy Scriptures its sufficiency as to all things necessary to salvation. Thus Satan undoes still by overdoing.

IV. *Object.* Laying on the hand, and kissing the book, seem of the same nature with the cross in baptism, and other significant ceremonies; and an oath is part of the worship of God; therefore it is not to be taken with these ceremonies, or else it will seem to justify the other.

*Answer.* 1. Significant words, gestures, or actions are not therefore evil because they are significant (unless brutishness is a virtue); nor because any call them by the name of ceremonies (else that name might be put on anything by an enemy to deprive us of our liberty). Therefore I can judge of no ceremony by that general name alone, until it is named itself *in specie*. 2. Of the cross in baptism, see my *Disputations of Church Government*, "Of Ceremonies," written long ago. There are these well-known differences in the case: 1. The cross is an image used in God's worship; though not a permanent, yet a transient image, and used as an image of the cross of Christ, though only in water or oil. And God has more specially forbidden images used in his worship than he has done a professing significant word, gesture, or action, which is no image, nor used as such. 2. The cross seems to be a third sacrament of the covenant of grace, while it is used as a symbol of Christianity, and a dedicating sign (as the canon calls it) by which, before the church, there is made a solemn self-obligation, as sacramentally, to renounce the devil, the world, and the flesh, and manfully to fight under Christ's banner, as his faithful servants and soldiers, to our life's end; implying our trust and hope in Christ crucified for the benefits of his death. So that if it is not a complete third sacrament, it has so much of that which is proper to a sacrament (like the *sacramentum militare*, from which the name came into the church) that for my part, I dare not use it, though I do not presume to censure those that do, nor to condemn all other uses of the cross, which the ancients abounded in, as sudden, particular, professing signs, much below this solemn covenanting use. And as I think the king would not take it well, when he has made the star the badge of the knights of the Garter, if any subject will presume to make another *symbolum ordinis*, though yet many a significant gesture or act may be used without offense; so I fear Christ would not take it well of me if I presume to make or use another symbol or *tessera* of Christianity, especially with so much of a covenanting sacramental nature. But what is this to things or gestures significant of no such kind? You see then the difference of these cases.

But if you were able to prove the cross as harmless as the swearing ceremony, I would be for the cross, and not against the laying of the hand on the book, and kissing it. For, 1. I am not of their mind that form their judgment of other particulars to suit with their preconceived opinions of things of the same rank or quality; nor do I make the interest of my former conceptions to be the measure of my later judging. 2. Nor do I think it so great an honor to be strict in my opinions as a dishonor to be superstitious, and to add to God's law by saying that he forbids what he does not, or to be affectedly singular in denying lawful things, with a "Do not handle! Do not taste! Do not touch!" etc. Nor do I esteem him to be the wisest, best, or holiest person who is narrowest or strictest in his opinions, but who is rightest; nor him that makes most things to be sins, but him that commits least sin, which is such indeed; nor him that makes most laws to himself and others, but him that best obeys God's laws.

*Question.* 1. May one that has scruples about thus swearing himself, yet, commissioned, give an oath thus to another that has no scruples about it?

*Answer.* 1. If the thing is, as is proved, lawful, his scruple will not make him innocent in neglecting the duty of his place. 2. If the substance of the oath were lawful, and only the mode or ceremony were sinful, as suspected, then, (1.) If the commissioner must himself particularly command that mode, it would be unlawful for him to do it. (2.) But if he only commands, and gives the oath as an oath, leaving the mode, without his approval or command, to the taker and the law, he may so give the oath: and thus Christians in all ages have taken it for lawful to make covenants even with unbelievers and idolaters, and to take a Turk's oath by Muhammad, when it is only the oath that we demand, and the mode is his own, which we would rather be without, and give no approval of. And if a king may thus demand an unbeliever's or idolater's oath (as God himself does men's duty, when he knows that they will sin in doing it), much more may one do so in case of a doubtful ceremony, which he is neither the author nor approver of. But I think this in question is lawful, fit, and laudable.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **Title:** Chapter 9 ("Directions for the Government of the Tongue") is divided into six "Titles" (Baxter's "_Tit._"), that is, headed divisions. This passage falls under Title 2, special directions against profane swearing.
- **Where this file sits:** Baxter has asked, "Is it lawful to lay hands on the book and kiss it in swearing, as is done in England?" and answered that it is. Under his point III he answers objections numbered I to IV. This file opens inside objection I ("It savors of the Romish superstition"): the two unnumbered objections at the start (Pareus; "some good men") continue that first objection. Objections II, III, and IV follow, then a further *Question*.
- **Latin** (draft translations prepared for this project; a Latinist should check them):
  - *Non absque superstitione fit cum super crucifixum aut codicem Evangelii digitis impositis juratur, ut fit in Papatu* (English: It is not done without superstition when one swears with the fingers laid on a crucifix or on the book of the Gospel, as is done in the Papacy.) *in Papatu*: "in the Papacy," that is, under Rome.
  - *supra sacramenta sua per sancta Dei evangelia fideliter præstanda* (English: upon their oaths, to be faithfully taken by the holy Gospels of God.)
  - *Ad sancta Dei evangelia rite et legitime jurati* (English: duly and lawfully sworn upon the holy Gospels of God.) *Ad sancta evangelia*: "upon the holy Gospels."
  - *de nomine*: "about the name," that is, a dispute about words only.
  - *Sacramentum*: in Latin both "oath" (originally a soldier's oath) and "sacrament"; Baxter's argument turns on this double sense. *super sacramenta*: "upon the sacraments" (or "upon oaths").
  - *in specie*: "in its particular kind," that is, specifically, by its own name.
  - *sacramentum militare*: "the military oath." Baxter says the church's word "sacrament" came from it.
  - *symbolum ordinis*: "badge (emblem) of the order." *tessera*: a token or badge of identity, such as a soldier's watchword tablet.
- **Genesis 24:2** is cited, not quoted; for reference, the NET has Abraham say, "Put your hand under my thigh."
- **Colossians 2:21:** Baxter quotes the King James "touch not, taste not, handle not." The NET reads "Do not handle! Do not taste! Do not touch!" in the reverse order of the three verbs; the sense Baxter uses (man-made prohibitions) is unchanged. His "&c." is rendered "etc." The reference is not in Baxter's text.
- **Pareus:** probably David Pareus, the Heidelberg Reformed commentator on Genesis, whom Baxter cited earlier in this question (Baxter spells it "Paræus"). We have not checked the quotation against Pareus's commentary.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): conceits → notions; addeth not → does not add; touched not → did not touch; scrupled → had scruples about (several times); yea → indeed; out-go → outdo; improving (making use of) → turning to account; infer → imply; try → test; conjoined → joined together; testimony assertory → assertory testimony (an "assertory" testimony is one that asserts a fact, as distinct from a promise); but as (only as) → only as; own (acknowledge) → acknowledge; before cited → cited before; notorious (well known, obvious) → well-known; whence → from which; presume not → do not presume; after judging → later judging; approbation → approval; infidels → unbelievers; Mahomet → Muhammad; had rather → would rather.
- **Kept words:** laudable (praiseworthy); imprecation (calling down a curse on oneself if one lies); corporal oath (an oath taken with a bodily act, here touching the book); significant (in the sense "signifying, carrying a meaning," as in "significant ceremonies"); gratify (please, serve the interest of); affectedly singular (deliberately odd or nonconforming); professing (making a profession of faith); papists, popery (Baxter's words for Roman Catholics and their religion; his polemic, kept as he wrote it); Turk (the period word for a Muslim, especially a subject of the Ottoman Empire).
- **"his promises to be just"** (paragraph beginning "1. There are three things"): printed so in the source. It may be a misprint for "his promises to the just," but we have kept the printed wording.
- **"Ten thousand to one such have not had scruples about it":** Baxter means that for every good man who has had scruples, ten thousand have had none.
- **Quakers and old Anabaptists; Origen:** Baxter's report that these refused all oaths. The Origen claim he himself gives as hearsay ("they say").
- **Bede, Boniface:** early medieval English churchmen and writers. Baxter's claim that pious credulity brought in the legends of Rome is his polemic.
- **Thirty-nine Articles; book of ordination:** the Church of England's doctrinal articles and its ordination service. The teaching that Scripture contains all things necessary to salvation is, we believe, Article 6, but we have not checked the exact text.
- **oath of supremacy:** the oath acknowledging the monarch as supreme governor of the church in his realm. Baxter's remark about "scrupulous brethren in Scotland" refusing it is his own report.
- **the cross in baptism; "a dedicating sign (as the canon calls it)":** the Church of England's practice of signing the baptized child with the cross, a point Puritans and nonconformists objected to. Baxter refers to a canon of the Church of England, probably among the canons of 1604, but we have not confirmed which one or its exact words.
- ***Disputations of Church Government*** ("Of Ceremonies"): Baxter's own earlier work, title kept in italics.
- **the star the badge of the knights of the Garter:** the star worn by knights of the Order of the Garter, an English order of chivalry; Baxter's comparison is that no subject may invent another badge of the order.
- **"as God himself does men's duty":** that is, as God himself demands men's duty. The verb is understood from the clause before; kept as Baxter wrote it.
