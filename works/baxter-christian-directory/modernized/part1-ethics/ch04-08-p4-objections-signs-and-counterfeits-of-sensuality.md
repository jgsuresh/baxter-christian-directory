# Chapter 4: Objections of the flesh-pleasers, signs of sensuality, and its counterfeits

*This range begins partway through the Direction "Directions against the Master Sin; Sensuality, Flesh-pleasing," at its numbered point IV, and ends partway through point VI. The numbered points I through III come in the previous file; point VI continues in the next. No new Direction heading is given here.*

*In the margin: The plea of flesh-pleasers.*

IV. *Objection 1.* What hurt is it to God, or anyone else, that I please my flesh? I will not believe that a thing so harmless will displease him. *Answer.* Merely as it is pleasure, it has no hurt in it: but as it is excessive or immoderate pleasure; or as it is over-loved, and preferred before God and your salvation; or as it is greater than your delight in God; or as it lacks its proper end, and is loved merely for itself, and not used as a means to higher things; and as it is made a hindrance to the soul, and to spiritual pleasure, and the service of God; and as it is the brutish delight of an ungoverned, rebellious appetite, that masters reason, and is not under obedience to God. Though sin can do God no hurt, it can do you hurt, and it can do him wrong. I think I have shown you what hurt and poison is in it already. It is the very rebellion of corrupted nature; the turning of all things upside down; the taking down God, and heaven, and reason, and destroying the use of all the creatures, and setting up flesh-pleasing instead of all, and making a brute your god and governor. And do you ask what harm there is in this? So will your child do, when he desires any play, or pleasure; and the sick, when they desire to please their appetite. But your father, and physician, and reason, and not brutish appetite, must be judge.

*Objection 2.* But I feel it is natural to me, and therefore can be no sin. *Answer.* 1. The excessive, violent, unruly appetite is no otherwise natural to you, than as a leprosy is to a leprous generation. And will you love your disease, because it is natural? It is no otherwise natural, than it is to be malicious, and revengeful, and to disobey your governors, and abuse your neighbors; and yet I think they will not judge you innocent, for rebellion or abuse, because it is natural to you. 2. Though the appetite is natural, is not reason to rule it as natural to you? And is not the subjection of the appetite to reason natural? If it is not, you have lost the nature of man, and are metamorphosed into the nature of a beast. God gave you a higher nature to govern your appetite and lower nature: and though reason cannot take away your appetite, it can rule it, and keep you from fulfilling it, in anything or measure that is improper.

*Objection 3.* But it appears by the case of Eve, that the appetite was the same in innocence; therefore it is no sin. *Answer.* You must not forget the difference between, 1. The appetite itself. 2. The violence and unruly disposition of the appetite. 3. And the actual obeying and pleasing of the appetite. The first (the appetite itself) was in innocence, and is yet no sin. But the other two (the violence of it, and the obeying it) were not in innocence, and are both sinful.

*Objection 4.* But why would God give innocent man an appetite that must be crossed by reason? and that desired that which reason must forbid? *Answer.* The sensory nature comes in order of generation before the rational: and reason and God's laws do not make sense to be no sense. You may as well ask, why God would make beasts, which must be restrained and ruled by men; and therefore have a desire to that which man must restrain them from? You only ask, Why God made us men and not angels? Why he placed our souls in flesh? He owes you no account of his creation. But you may see it is fitting that obedience should have some trial by difficulties and opposition, before it has its commendation and reward. He gave you a body that was subject to the soul, as the horse to the rider; and you should admire his wisdom, and thank him for the governing power of reason; and not murmur at him, because the horse will not go as well without the guidance of the rider, or because he does not make you able to go as fast and as well on foot. So much for the pleasure-seeker's objections.

*In the margin: Signs of indulgence in physical pleasure.*

V. The signs of a flesh-pleaser or pleasure-seeker are these (which may be gathered from what is said already): 1. When a man in desire to please his appetite, does not refer it (actually or habitually) to a higher end, namely, fitting himself to the service of God; but sticks only in the delight. 2. When he looks more eagerly and industriously after the prosperity of his body, than of his soul. 3. When he will not part with or forbear his pleasures, when God forbids them, or when they hurt his soul, or when the necessities of his soul call him more loudly another way; but he must have his delight whatever it costs him, and is so set upon it, that he cannot deny it to himself. 4. When the pleasures of his flesh exceed his delights in God, and his holy word and ways, and the forethoughts of endless pleasure; and this not only in the passion, but in the estimation, choice, and pursuit. When he had rather be at a play, or feast, or gambling, or getting good bargains or profits in the world, than to live in the life of faith, and love, a holy and heavenly conduct. 5. When men set their minds to contrive and study to make provision for the pleasures of the flesh; and this is first and sweetest in their thoughts. 6. When they had rather talk, or hear, or read of fleshly pleasures, than of spiritual and heavenly delights. 7. When they love the company of merry pleasure-seekers, better than the communion of saints, in which they may be exercised in the praises of their Maker. 8. When they account that the best calling, and condition, and place for them to live in, where they have the pleasure of the flesh, where they have ease, and fare well, and want nothing for the body, rather than that where they have far better help and provision for the soul, though the flesh is pinched for it. 9. When he will be at more cost to please his flesh, than to please God. 10. When he will believe or like no doctrine but libertinism, and hates mortification as too strict preciseness. By these, and such other signs, indulgence in physical pleasure may easily be known; indeed, by the main bent of the life.

*In the margin: Counterfeits of mortification.*

VI. Many flesh-pleasers flatter themselves with better titles, being deceived by such means as these: 1. Because they are against the doctrine of libertinism, and hold as strict opinions as any. But flesh-pleasing may stand with the doctrine of mortification, and the strictest opinions, as long as they are not put in practice.

2. Because they do not live in any gross, disgraced vice. They do not go to stage-plays, or at the wrong times to alehouses or taverns; they are not drunken, nor gamblers, nor spend their hours in unnecessary recreations or pastimes; they are no fornicators, nor wallow in wealth. But the flesh may be pleased and served in a way that has no disgrace accompanying it in the world. May not a man make his ease, or his prosperity, or the pleasing of his appetite, without any infamous excesses, to be as much his happiness and highest end, and that which practically he takes for his best, as well as if he did it in a shameful way? Is not many a man a gluttonous flesh-pleaser, who makes his delight the highest end of all his eating and drinking; and pleases his appetite without any restraint, but what his health and reputation put upon him, though he does not eat till he vomits or is sick? Even the flesh itself may forbid a pleasure-seeker to be drunk, or to eat till he is sick; for sickness and shame are displeasing to the flesh. Many a man covers a life of indulgence in physical pleasure, not only with a seeming temperance, unreproved by men, but also with a seeming strictness and austerity. But conscience might tell them, where they have their good things (Luke 16:25).

3. Some think they are no pleasure-loving flesh-pleasers, because they live in constant misery, in poverty and want, laboring hard for their daily bread; and therefore they hope that they are the Lazaruses that have their sufferings here. But is not all this against your will? Would you not fare as well as the rich, and live as idly, and take your pleasure, if you had as much as they? What you would do, that you do in God's account. It is your will that you shall be judged by. A thief does not become an honest man when the prison or stocks hinder him from stealing, but when a changed heart hinders him.

4. Others think that they are no flesh-pleasers, because their wealth, and places, and degrees of honor allow them to live high in diet and delights. It is like the rich man, Luke 16, who "dressed in purple and fine linen and who feasted sumptuously every day," lived upon his own, and as he thought agreeably to his rank and place; and the fool, Luke 12:19-20, who said, "relax, eat, drink, celebrate!" intended to please himself but with his own, which God had given him as a blessing on his land and labor. But no man's riches allow him to be voluptuous. The commands of taming and mortifying the flesh, and not living after it, nor making provision for it, to satisfy its lusts, belong as much to the rich as to the poor. Though you are not to live in the same garb with the poor, you are as much bound to mortification and self-denial as the poorest. If you are richer than others, you have more to serve God with, but not more than others to serve the flesh with. If poverty denies them anything which might better enable their bodies or minds to serve God, you may so far go beyond them, and use with thankfulness the mercies given you; but you must no more be flesh-pleasers than they.

5. And some deceive themselves by interposing sometimes a formal fast, as the fleshly Pharisee, who "fasted twice a week," Luke 18:12, and then they think that they are no pleasure-seekers. I do not speak of the popish fasting with fish and delicacies (this is not so much as a show of mortification). But what if you really fast as often as the Pharisees did, and quarrel with Christ's disciples for not fasting? Matthew 9:14-15. Will not a pleasure-seeker do as much as this, if his physician requires it for his health? If the scope of your lives is fleshly, it is not the interruption of a formal fast that will acquit you; which perhaps does but quicken your appetite to the next meal.

## Translator's notes

- **Structure:** Baxter's roman numerals IV, V, VI are numbered points within the Direction against sensuality, and the objections are his "Object. I-IV"; we render them as "Objection 1-4" and keep his numbers. His sub-points 1-5 under VI stay numbered. The italic "In the margin" lines are his margin summaries.
- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible, fetched from labs.bible.org for this file; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **Luke 16:19** (cited by Baxter as "Luke xvi." only; we added no verse number in the text; "clothed with purple and fine linen, and fared sumptuously every day" in Baxter): NET wording, "dressed in purple and fine linen and who feasted sumptuously every day." Same sense.
- **Luke 12:19-20 (flagged):** Baxter quotes the King James, "Soul, take thine ease, eat, drink, and be merry." The NET reads "relax, eat, drink, celebrate!" and has no "Soul": it has the man say "And I will say to myself, 'You have plenty of goods stored up for many years; relax, eat, drink, celebrate!'" Baxter's "take your ease" is what he calls "intending to please himself," and the NET's "relax" still carries that sense; the "Soul" address is simply not in the NET wording. Baxter cites "xii. 19, 20"; we keep "Luke 12:19-20" as he gave it. The quoted words are in verse 19.
- **Luke 18:12 (flagged):** Baxter writes that the Pharisee "fasted twice a week." This is his own adaptation, in the third person and past tense, of the Pharisee's words. The NET has the Pharisee speak: "I fast twice a week; I give a tenth of everything I get." We kept Baxter's wording in his quotation marks and did not alter it to the NET's first-person present.
- **Luke 16:25:** Baxter only cites it, after "where they have their good things." The NET reads "in your lifetime you received your good things and Lazarus likewise bad things." Baxter's sentence is left as written (a clause without a main verb), with the reference placed in parentheses.
- **Matthew 9:14-15:** cited only. In the NET it is John's disciples who ask why they and the Pharisees fast but Jesus' disciples do not.
- **Unmarked allusions:** "the Lazaruses that have their sufferings here" (Luke 16:19-25); "the Pharisees ... quarrel with Christ's disciples for not fasting" (Matthew 9:14-15); "the taming and mortifying of the flesh, and not making provision for it, to satisfy its lusts" (compare Romans 13:14 and Romans 8:13; not marked by Baxter); "the horse unto the rider" and the body subject to the soul are his own images.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): hurt, showed and the like kept or lightly respelled; sensual, sensualist, sensuality to pleasure-loving / pleasure-seeker / indulgence in physical pleasure (by context; "flesh-pleaser" and "flesh-pleasing" are his own terms and are kept); sensitive (nature) to sensory; inordinate to excessive; hinderance to hindrance; wants (lacks) to lacks; "You do but ask" to "You only ask"; desirously to eagerly; unmeet to improper; meet (adj.) to fitting; felicity to happiness; conversation (conduct) to conduct; gaming to gambling; gamesters to gamblers; delicates to delicacies; unseasonably to "at the wrong times"; prosecution (of pleasures) to pursuit; honour to honor; "a true man" (as opposed to a thief) to "an honest man"; "so set upon it" and "fare well" left as written.
- **Objection 2, "a leprosy ... to a leprous generation":** left as written; Baxter's comparison is that a disease that runs in a family is "natural" in the same way.
- **Objection 4:** "the sensory nature is in order of generation before the rational" means, as we read it, that the bodily and appetitive nature develops in a person before reason does. "Reason and God's laws do not make sense to be no sense" means reason and law do not turn the senses into non-senses (that is, they do not abolish the appetite). We have left Baxter's wording and give this reading only as ours; he may mean something slightly different.
- **libertinism:** Baxter's word for the view that a Christian is free of the law's restraint in matters of the flesh. Kept as his term. **preciseness:** kept; he means (as his opponents' charge) overstrict scrupulosity. **mortification** stays (see GLOSSARY.md).
- **popish fasting** (point VI.5): Baxter's polemic against Roman Catholic fasting with fish and delicacies; kept as his, not our claim.
- **"the fool, Luke xii."** and **"the rich man, Luke xvi."** are Baxter's labels for the parable characters; the NET calls neither by those names in the quoted verses (the second is "a rich man"; the first is "You fool!" in verse 20).
- **"Luke xvi. 25"** after "where they have their good things" is printed in the source as a reference without the verse text; kept as a reference only.
- **Source oddity:** the source's "unseasonably to alehouses or taverns" is rendered "at the wrong times to alehouses or taverns"; the sense is going at improper hours or seasons.
