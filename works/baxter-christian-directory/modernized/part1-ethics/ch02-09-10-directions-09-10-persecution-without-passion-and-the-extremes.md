# Chapter 2: Directions 9 and 10

**Direction 9. Be careful that no persecution or wrong from others provokes you to any unjustifiable passions and practices, and deprives you of the charity, meekness, and innocence of a Christian; or makes you go beyond your bounds, in censuring, reviling, or resisting your rulers, who are the officers of God.**

Persecution and wrongs are called temptations in Scripture, because they test you, to see whether you will hold to your integrity. [64] As many fall in such trials, through the fear of men, and the love of the world, and their prosperity; so when you seem most firmly set against any sinful compliance, there is a snare laid for you on the other side, to draw you into passions and practices that are unjustifiable.

Those who are tainted with pride, uncharitableness, and schism will itch to be persecuting those who do not comply with them in their way; and yet, while they do it, they will most cry out against pride, uncharitableness, and schism themselves. This is, and has been, and will be too common in the world. You may think that schism should be far from them, who seem to do all for order and unity. But never expect to see this generally cured, when you have said and done the best you can: you must, therefore, resolve, not only to flee from church division yourselves, but also to undergo the persecutions or wrongs of proud or zealous church dividers. It is great weakness in you, to think such treatment strange: do you not know that enmity is put, from the beginning, between the woman's and the serpent's seed? And do you think the name or lifeless profession of Christianity does extinguish the enmity in the serpent's seed? Do you think to find more kindness from proud, ungodly Christians, than Abel might have expected from his brother Cain? [65] Do you not know that the Pharisees (by their zeal for their pre-eminence, and traditions, and ceremonies, and the expectation of worldly dignity and rule from the Messiah) were more zealous enemies of Christ than the heathens were? and that the carnal members of the church are often the greatest persecutors of the spiritual members? "But just as at that time the one born by natural descent persecuted the one born according to the Spirit, so it is now," (and will be,) (Galatians 4:29). It is enough for you, that you shall have the inheritance, when the sons of the bondwoman shall be cast out. It is your taking the ordinary case of the godly for a strange thing, that makes you so disturbed and passionate, when you suffer: and reason is down, when passion is up. It is by overwhelming reason with passion and discontent, that "oppression can turn a wise person into a fool" (Ecclesiastes 7:7); for passion is a short, imperfect madness. You will think in your passion, that you do well, when you do ill; and you will not perceive the force of reason, when it is ever so plain and full against you. Remember, therefore, that the great motive that causes the devil to persecute you is not to hurt your bodies, but to tempt your souls to impatience and sin: and if it may be said of you as of Job, "In all this Job did not sin" (Job 1:22), you have got the victory, and "have complete victory" (Romans 8:37-39).

Does it seem strange to you, that "few rich men are saved," when Christ tells you it is "so hard," as to be "impossible for mere humans" (Luke 18:27; Mark 10:27)? Or is it strange, that rich men should be the usual rulers of the earth? Or is it strange, that the wicked should hate the godly, and the world hate those who are "chosen out of the world"? What of all this should seem strange? Expect it as the common lot of the faithful, and you will be better prepared for it.

See therefore that you "do not resist the evildoer" (by any revengeful, disorderly violence) (Matthew 5:39). "Let every person be subject to the governing authorities ... those who resist will incur judgment" (Romans 13:1-3). Imitate your Lord, that "when he was maligned, he did not answer back; when he suffered, he threatened no retaliation, but committed himself to God who judges justly; leaving an example for you to follow in his steps" (1 Peter 2:21, 23). An angry zeal against those who cross and hurt us is so easily kindled and so hard to suppress, that it appears there is more in it of corrupted nature than of God. We are very ready to think that we may "call fire to come down from heaven" upon the enemies of the gospel; but "you do not know what kind of spirit you are of then" (Luke 9:55). But Christ says to you, "Love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who treat you spitefully and persecute you; that you may be the children of your Father who is in heaven" (Matthew 5:44-45). You find no such prohibition against patiently suffering wrong from anyone. Be careful about giving way to secret wishes of harm to your adversaries, or to reproachful words against them: be careful about hurting yourself by passion or sin, because others hurt you by slanders or persecutions. Keep yourselves in the way of your duty, and leave your names and lives to God. Be careful that you keep your innocence, and in your patience possess your souls, and God will keep you from any harm from enemies, except what he will cause to work for your good. Read Psalm 37. "Commit your future to the Lord. Trust in him, and he will act on your behalf. He will vindicate you in broad daylight, and publicly defend your just cause. Wait patiently for the Lord! Wait confidently for him! Do not fret over the apparent success of a sinner, a man who carries out wicked schemes. Do not be angry and frustrated. Do not fret. That only leads to trouble" (Psalm 37:5-8).

**Direction 10. When you are repenting of or avoiding any extreme, do not do it without sufficient fear and caution about the contrary extreme.**

*In the margin: Extremes in religion.*

In the esteem and love of God, your ultimate goal, you need not fear overdoing: nor anywhere, when impediments, and backwardness or powerlessness, do tell you that you can never do too much. But sin lies on both sides of the rule and way: and nothing is more common, than to turn from one sin to another, under the name of duty or reform. Especially this is common in matters of opinion. Some will first believe, that God is nothing but mercy, and afterward take notice of nothing but his justice. First, they believe that almost all are saved, and afterward, that almost none: first, that every profession is credible, and next, that none is credible without some greater testimony: first, that Christ satisfied for none at all who will not be saved, and next, that he died for all alike: first, that none are now partakers of the Holy Spirit; and next, that all saints have the Spirit, not only to illuminate and sanctify them, by transcribing the written word upon their hearts; but also to inspire them with new revelations, instead of Scripture: first, they think that all that papists hold and do must be avoided; and afterward, that there was no need of reformation at all. Now they are for legal bondage, and soon after for libertinism: today, for a liberty in religion to none who do not agree with them in every circumstance; and tomorrow, for a liberty for all: this year, all things are lawful to them; and the next year, nothing is lawful, but they have scruples about all that they say or do. At one time, they are all for a worship of mere show and ceremony; and at another time, against the determination of mere circumstances of order and decency, by man. At one time, they praise nothing but free grace; and at another time, nothing but free will. At one time, they are for a discipline stricter than the rule; and at another time, for no discipline at all. First, for fearful compliance with evil; and afterward, for boisterous contempt of government. Abundance of such instances we might give you.

The remedy against this disease is to proceed deliberately, and receive nothing and do nothing rashly and unadvisedly in religion. For, when you have found out your first error, you will be frightened from that into the contrary error. See that you look round about you; as well to the error that you may run into on the other side, as into that which you have run into already. Consult also with wise, experienced men; and mark the misfortune of those who have fallen on both sides; and do not wait to know evil by sad experience. True mediocrity is the only way that is safe; though negligence and lukewarmness are odious, even when cloaked with that name.

*In the margin: For modesty in your first opinions.*

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). All NET quotations in this file were fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Galatians 4:29:** Baxter's King James wording was "he that was born after the flesh ... him that was born after the Spirit." The NET has "the one born by natural descent." The word "flesh" is gone, and Baxter's next sentences about "carnal members of the church" lean on the flesh-and-Spirit contrast. The sense of the verse is the same, but the verbal link is lost. The reference was printed "Gal. iv. 29."
- **Ecclesiastes 7:7:** Baxter (King James) has "oppression maketh a wise man mad." The NET has "oppression can turn a wise person into a fool." Baxter's very next clause ("passion is a short, imperfect madness") builds on "mad," which the NET does not use. His "some" before "wise men" is absorbed by the NET's "can."
- **Job 1:22:** NET "did not sin" for "sinned not"; the NET adds, after the part Baxter quotes, "nor did he charge God with moral impropriety." Only Baxter's extent is quoted.
- **Romans 8:37-39:** Baxter's "more than conquerors" is the King James; the NET has "we have complete victory through him who loved us." Baxter leans on the comparative ("more than"), which the NET does not carry, though "complete victory" is close to his point that the sufferer has won. We quote only the NET phrase "have complete victory" (NET has "we have"), fitted to Baxter's "you." Verses 38-39 are cited by Baxter but not quoted.
- **Luke 18:27; Mark 10:27:** "few rich men are saved" and "so hard" are in Baxter's quotation marks but are not verbatim from either verse; we treat them as his paraphrase of the teaching on the rich (compare "How hard it is for the rich to enter the kingdom of God," Luke 18:24 NET), and left them in his wording. "Impossible with men" is the King James; NET has "impossible for mere humans," used here. Baxter's sentence reads the camel teaching as "so hard" that it is "impossible"; in the NET context the "impossible" is Jesus' answer to "Then who can be saved?" and is followed by "possible for God." Baxter does not quote that second half.
- **"chosen out of the world":** unmarked allusion in Baxter's wording (compare John 15:19, NET: "I chose you out of the world"). Not in the text with a reference, since he gave none.
- **Matthew 5:39:** Baxter's "resist not evil" is the King James; the NET has "do not resist the evildoer." The NET makes the object a person; the King James can be read as evil in general. Baxter's own gloss, "by any revengeful, irregular violence," fits either reading. **irregular:** his word, rendered "disorderly" (outside lawful order).
- **Romans 13:1-3:** Baxter's quotation blends verses 1 and 2 in the King James: "Let every soul be subject to the higher powers, and not resist, lest they receive damnation." The NET has "Let every person be subject to the governing authorities" (verse 1) and "those who resist will incur judgment" (verse 2); we give those two pieces with an ellipsis. Differences that matter: "higher powers" becomes "governing authorities"; and "damnation" (Baxter leans on its severity) becomes "judgment," a milder-sounding word in English. The NET also adds, "there is no authority except by God's appointment," which Baxter does not quote but which supports his phrase "officers of God" in the Direction.
- **1 Peter 2:21, 23:** Baxter cites verses 21 and 23 in that order, as we do. NET "committed himself to God who judges justly" where Baxter (King James) has "committed all to him that judgeth righteously"; Baxter's "all" (his whole cause) is narrower in the NET's "himself." Baxter also has "leaving us an ensample"; the NET has "leaving an example for you." **Imitate your Lord, that...:** Baxter's "that" means roughly "seeing that"; left as is.
- **Luke 9:55:** Baxter cites only verse 55, so the reference is given as he printed it (the NET fetch for 9:54-55 was used to check the wording). His "call for fire from heaven" is the disciples' request in verse 54, which he does not cite; the NET has "call fire to come down from heaven." The words "you know not what manner of spirit ye are of" are in the King James; the NET text of verse 55 as fetched stops at "But Jesus turned and rebuked them." Because those words are not in the NET text, we kept Baxter's wording (modernized) for them. Note the word order he gives, "ye are then of."
- **Matthew 5:44-45:** Baxter's quotation has "bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them that despitefully use you." The NET text of verse 44 is shorter: "love your enemy and pray for those who persecute you." Since much of what Baxter quotes is not in the NET, we kept his wording, modernized, rather than substitute. Also, Baxter has "that ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven" and the NET has "so that you may be like your Father in heaven." Baxter's "children" (sonship) differs from "like" (likeness). "despitefully use" is rendered "treat spitefully."
- **"possess your souls":** unmarked allusion in Baxter's wording (compare Luke 21:19; NET "By your endurance you will gain your lives"). The King James "possess your souls" and "patience" differ from the NET's "gain your lives" and "endurance." Baxter's emphasis on the soul would be lost in the NET.
- **Psalm 37:5-8:** NET wording, with spaces added where the API ran lines together. Baxter's King James text has "Rest in the Lord" at the start of verse 7 (the NET opens "Wait patiently for the Lord!"), "forsake wrath," and "fret not thyself in any wise to do evil." The NET verse 8 ends "That only leads to trouble," not "to do evil." Baxter, who is warning about sin through anger, loses a little force: in the NET, fretting leads to "trouble," not explicitly to "evil." The NET's "apparent success of a sinner" also adds "apparent." Baxter's King James also has "as the light" and "as the noon-day" where the NET has "in broad daylight," and "thy judgment" where the NET has "your just cause." Baxter's verse numbers were printed "ver. 5-8."
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "enmity is put, from the beginning, between the woman's and the serpent's seed" (Genesis 3:15); Abel and Cain (Genesis 4); "the sons of the bondwoman shall be cast out" and "you shall have the inheritance" (compare Galatians 4:30). We have not checked these against the NET.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): *Take heed* to *Be careful / Watch out / Beware*; *unwarrantable* to *unjustifiable*; *innocency* to *innocence*; *hath* to *has*; *ordinary* (common) to *common* (in "too ordinary in the world") and to *usual* (in "ordinary rulers"); *hardly suppressed* (suppressed with difficulty) to *so hard to suppress*; *Keep you* to *Keep yourselves*; *oft* to *often*; *usage* to *treatment*; *never so* to *ever so*; *anon* to *soon after*; *one while* to *at one time*; *cry up* to *praise*; *timorous* to *fearful*; *affrighted* to *frightened*; *impotency* to *powerlessness*; *amendment* to *reform*; *ultimate end* to *ultimate goal*; *unhappiness* to *misfortune*; *scruple* to *have scruples about*; *christian* to *Christian*.
- **Kept as Baxter's terms:** *compliance* (yielding to demands, here to the persecutors'); *profession* (outward claim of faith); *carnal* (here, ruled by the flesh; as in the glossary); *passion* (strong emotion that overrides reason); *legal bondage* (bondage to the law as a way of salvation); *libertinism* (rejection of moral restraint); *satisfied for* (made satisfaction for sins, a doctrine of the atonement); *illuminate*; *discipline* (church discipline); *papists* (his word for Roman Catholics, and his polemic); *true mediocrity* (the middle way between extremes, not "average").
- **Polemic:** the lists in Direction 10 ("papists," those who "inspire ... new revelations, instead of Scripture") are Baxter's own characterizations of rival positions, kept as written.
- **Margin note at the end of the range:** the line "For modesty in your first opinions" is the last line in the source range. In the source it stands after the final paragraph, so it may belong to the paragraph that follows the range (we have not checked). We give it as it stands.
- **Victor Utic.; Hunnerychus** (note 64): Baxter's "Hunnerychus" is probably Huneric, the Vandal king who persecuted Catholic Christians in North Africa, and "Victor. Utic." probably refers to Victor of Vita's history of that persecution. We have not confirmed either identification or the page numbers.
- **Gildas; Neander** (note 65): Gildas: the title is printed "De Ex cid. Britan." in the source, apparently a misprint for *De Excidio Britanniae*; we have not confirmed the work or its author beyond that. "Neander Chron." is probably Michael Neander's chronicle; not confirmed. The Latin is kept exactly as printed, including "Ex cid." and "Lege et quæ."
