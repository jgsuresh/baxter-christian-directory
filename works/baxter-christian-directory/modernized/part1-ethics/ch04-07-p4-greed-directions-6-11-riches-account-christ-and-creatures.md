# Chapter 4: Directions against Greed, Directions 6-11

*This range begins at Direction 6 of the Directions against Greed (Baxter's "Covetousness"), after earlier Directions in the previous file, and ends at the end of Direction 11; the next file continues with Direction 12.*

**Direction 6. Remember that riches do make it much harder for a man to be saved; and the love of this world is the commonest cause of men's damnation.**

This is certainly true, for all that poverty also has its temptations; and for all that the poor are far more numerous than the rich. For even the poor may be undone by the love of that wealth and plenty which they never get; and those may perish for over-loving the world, that yet never prospered in the world. [260] And if you believe Christ, the point is out of controversy: for he says, "How hard it is for the rich to enter the kingdom of God! In fact, it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter the kingdom of God. Those who heard this said, 'Then who can be saved?' He replied, 'What is impossible for mere humans is possible for God'" (Luke 18:24-27). So, "But woe to you who are rich, for you have received your comfort already. Woe to you who are well satisfied with food now, for you will be hungry" (Luke 6:24-25). Make but sense of these and many such like texts, and you can gather no less than this from them, that riches make the way to heaven much harder, and the salvation of the rich to be more difficult and rare, proportionally, than of other men. And Paul says, "Not many were wise by human standards, not many were powerful, not many were born to a privileged position" (1 Corinthians 1:26). And the lovers of riches, though they are poor, must remember that it is said, "the love of money is the root of all evils" (1 Timothy 6:10). And, "Do not love the world or the things in the world. If anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him" (1 John 2:15). Do you believe that here lies the danger of your souls? and yet can you so love, and choose, and seek it? Would you have your salvation more difficult, and doubtful, and impossible with men? You had rather choose to live where few die young, than where most die young; and where sicknesses are rare, than where they are common. If you were sick, you had rather have the physician, and medicines, and diet which cure most, than those which few are cured by. If the country were beset with thieves, you had rather go the way that most escape in, than that few escape in. And yet, so it may but please your flesh, you will choose that way to heaven, that fewest escape in; and you will choose that state of life, which will make your salvation to be most hard and doubtful. Does your conscience say that is wisely done? I know that if God put riches into your hand, by your birth, or his blessing on your honest labors, you must not cast away your Master's talents, because he is austere; but by a holy improvement of them, you may further his service and your salvation. But this is no reason why you should over-love them, or desire and seek so great a danger. Believe Christ heartily, and it will quench your love of riches.

**Direction 7. Remember that the more you have, the more you have to give account for.**

And if the day of judgment be dreadful to you, you should not make it more dreadful by increasing your own accounts. If you desired riches but for the service of your Lord, and have used them for him, and can truly give in this account, that you laid them not out for the needless pleasure or pride of the flesh, but to furnish yourselves, and families, and others, for his service, and as near as you could, employ them according to his will, and for his use, then you may expect the reward of good and faithful servants; but if you desired and used them for the pride and pleasure of yourselves while you lived, and your posterity or kindred when you are dead, dropping some insignificant crumbs for God, you will then find that Mammon was an unprofitable master, and godliness, with content, would have been greater gain. [261]

**Direction 8. Remember how dear it costs men, thus to hinder their salvation, and increase their danger and accounts.**

What a lot of precious time is lost upon the world, by the lovers of it, which might have been improved to the getting of wisdom and grace, and making their calling and election sure! [262] If you had believed that the gain of holy wisdom had been so much better than the gaining of gold, as Solomon says (Proverbs 3:14), you would have laid out much of that time in laboring to understand the Scriptures, and preparing for your endless life. How many unnecessary thoughts have you cast away upon the world, which might better have been laid out on your greater concerns! How many cares, and vexations, and passions does it cost men, to overload themselves with worldly provisions! Like a foolish traveler, who having a day's journey to go, does spend all the day in gathering together a load of food, and clothes, and money, more than he can carry, for fear of wanting by the way: or like a foolish runner, that has a race to run for his life, and spends the time in which he should be running, in gathering a burden of supposed necessities. [263] You have all the while God's work to do, and your souls to mind, and judgment to prepare for, and you are tiring and vexing yourselves for unnecessary things, as if it were the top of your ambition to be able to say, in hell, that you died rich. "Godliness combined with contentment brings great profit. For we have brought nothing into this world and so we cannot take a single thing out either. But if we have food and shelter, we will be satisfied with that. Those who long to be rich, however, stumble into temptation and a trap and many senseless and harmful desires that plunge people into ruin and destruction. For the love of money is the root of all evils. Some people in reaching for it have strayed (or been seduced) from the faith and stabbed themselves with many pains" (1 Timothy 6:6-10). Piercing sorrows here, and damnation hereafter, are a very dear price to give for money. [264] For Christ himself says, "For what benefit is it for a person to gain the whole world, yet forfeit his life? What can a person give in exchange for his life?" (Mark 8:36-37); that is, What money or price will recover it, if for the love of gain he lose it? "The one who is greedy for gain troubles his household, but whoever hates bribes will live" (Proverbs 15:27). Do you not know that a godly man contented with his daily bread, has a far sweeter and quieter life and death than a self-troubling worldling? You may easily perceive it. "Better is little with the fear of the Lord than great wealth and turmoil with it" (Proverbs 15:16).

**Direction 9. Look much on the life of Christ on earth, and see how strangely he condemns worldliness by his example.**

Did he choose to be a prince or lord, or to have great possessions, lands, or money, or sumptuous buildings, or splendid attendance, and plentiful provisions? His housing you may read of: "Foxes have dens and the birds in the sky have nests, but the Son of Man has no place to lay his head" (Matthew 8:20; Luke 9:58). His clothing you may read of at his crucifying, when they divided it. As for money, he had to send Peter to a fish for some to pay their tribute. If Christ did scrape and care for riches, then so do you: if he thought it the happiest life, think so too. But if he despised it, despise it: if his whole life was directed to give you the most perfect example of the contempt of all the prosperity of this world, then learn of his example, if you take him for your Savior, and if you love yourself. "Although he was rich, he became poor for your sakes, so that you by his poverty could become rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9).

**Direction 10. Think on the example of the early Christians, even the best of Christ's servants, and see how it condemns worldliness.**

They that by miracle in the name of Christ could give limbs to the lame, yet tell him, "I have no silver or gold" (Acts 3:6). Those that had possessions sold them, and laid the money at the apostles' feet, and they had all things common, to show that faith overcomes the world, by despising it, and subjecting it to charity, and devoting it entirely to God. Read whether the apostles did live in sumptuous houses, with great attendance, and worldly plenty and prosperity? And so of the rest. [265]

**Direction 11. Remember to what ends all worldly things were made and given you, and what a happy advantage you may make of them by renouncing them, as they would be provision for your lusts, and by devoting yourselves and them to God.**

[266]

The use of their sweetness is, to draw your souls to taste by faith the heavenly sweetness. They are the looking-glass of souls in flesh, that are not yet admitted to see these things spiritual face to face. They are the provender of our bodies; our traveling furniture and helps; our inns, and comforting company in the way; they are some of God's love-tokens, some of the lesser pieces of his coin, and bear his image and inscription. They are drops from the rivers of the eternal pleasures; to tell the mind by the way of the senses how good the Donor is, and how lovable; and what higher delights there are for souls; and to point us to the better things which these foretell. They are messengers from heaven, to testify our Father's care and love, and to call for our thankfulness, love, and duty; and to bear witness against sin, and bind us faster to obedience. They are the first volume of the word of God; the first book that man was set to read, to acquaint him fully with his Maker. As the word which we read and hear is the chariot of the Spirit, by which it makes its accesses to the soul; so the delights of sight, and taste, and smell, and touch, and hearing, were appointed as an ordinary way for the speedy access of heavenly love and sweetness to the heart, that upon the first perception of the goodness and sweetness of the creature, there might at once be transmitted by a due progression, a deep impression of the goodness of God upon the soul; that the creatures, being the letters of God's book, which are seen by our eye, the sense (even the love of our great Creator) might at once be perceived by the mind: and no letter might once be looked upon but for the sense; no creature ever seen, or tasted, or heard, or felt in any delightful quality, without a sense of the love of God; that as the touch of the hand upon the strings of the lute does cause the melody, so God's touch by his mercies upon our hearts, might at once tune them into love, and gratitude, and praise. They are the tools by which we must do much of our Master's work. They are means by which we may refresh our brothers and sisters, and express our love to one another, and our love to our Lord and Master in his servants. They are our Master's stock, which we must trade with, by the improvement of which, no less than the reward of endless happiness may be attained. These are the uses to which God gives us outward mercies. Love them thus, and delight in them, and use them thus, and do not hold back; yes, seek them thus, and be thankful for them. But when the creatures are given for so excellent a use, will you debase them all by making them only the fuel of your lusts, and the provisions for your flesh? And will you love them, and dote on them in these base respects; while you utterly neglect their noblest use? You are just like children that cry for books, and can never have enough; but it is only to play with them because they are fine; but when they are set to learn and read them, they cry as much because they love it not: or like one that should spend his life and labor in getting the finest clothes, to dress his dogs and horses with, but himself goes naked and will not wear them. [267]

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[260] Socrates dixit, Opes et nobilitates, non solum nihil in se habere honestatis, verum et omne malum ex eis oboriri. Laert in Socrat. (English: Socrates said that riches and noble birth not only have nothing of honor in themselves, but that every evil arises from them. Laertius, in the life of Socrates.)

[261] Proverbs 3:14; 1 Timothy 6:5-6.

[262] Lege Petrarchæ lepidam historiam de avaro filio et liberali patre. Dial. 13. li. 2. (English: Read Petrarch's charming story of the greedy son and the generous father. Dialogue 13, book 2.)

[263] Plutarch says, de tranquillit. anim. Alexander wept because he was not lord of the world; when Crates, having but a wallet and a threadbare cloak, spent his whole life in mirth and joy, as if it had been a continual festival holiday.

[264] Psalm 37:16; Proverbs 16:8.

[265] Chrysostom says that his enemies charged him with many crimes, but never with greed or wantonness. And so it was with Christ and his enemies.

[266] Et sicut in patria Deus est speculum in quo relucent creaturæ; sic è converso in via, creaturæ sunt speculum quo creator videtur. Paul. Scaliger in Ep. Cath. 1. 14. Thess. 123. p. 689. (English: And as in the homeland God is the mirror in which the creatures shine back, so conversely on the way the creatures are the mirror in which the Creator is seen. Paul. Scaliger, on the Catholic Epistles [rendered loosely; the reference "1. 14. Thess. 123. p. 689" is kept as printed].)

[267] Even Dionysius the tyrant was bountiful to philosophers. To Plato he gave more than eighty talents, Laert. in Platone (English: Laertius, in the life of Plato), and much to Aristippus and many more, and he offered much to many philosophers that refused it. And so did Croesus.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). Every verse in quotation marks was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Section heading:** Baxter's "Covetousness" and "covetous" are rendered "greed" and "greedy" (decided practice). The section's heading itself appeared in an earlier file; it is not repeated.
- **Direction headings:** Baxter's printed text runs the Direction's first sentence(s) straight into the body. We set the first sentence (or two) in bold as the heading and begin the body after it, as in the finished files. No word has been added or dropped.
- **Luke 18:24-27:** NET wording. Baxter's "How hardly shall they that have riches enter" is "How hard it is for the rich to enter"; "the things which are impossible with men" is "What is impossible for mere humans." Baxter's "For it is easier" is "In fact, it is easier." The NET has "the eye of a needle" where Baxter has "a needle's eye." Baxter's "who then can be saved" sits inside the quotation in his text without inner marks; we add inner single quotation marks only to show the speakers.
- **Luke 6:24-25 (flag):** Baxter's "consolation" is the NET's "comfort," and his "woe unto you that are full" is the NET's "woe to you who are well satisfied with food now." "Full" in the King James is general; the NET narrows it to food. Baxter's argument (riches make salvation harder) is not changed. In the NET, a third woe ("Woe to you who laugh now") follows; Baxter does not quote it.
- **1 Corinthians 1:26 (flag):** Baxter's "Not many wise men after the flesh, not many mighty, not many noble are called" is the King James. The NET reads "Not many were wise by human standards, not many were powerful, not many were born to a privileged position," set in the context of the readers' call ("Think about the circumstances of your call"). The NET drops "are called" from the quoted words and puts the verb in the past. We follow the NET and the sentence is unchanged otherwise.
- **1 Timothy 6:10:** NET wording: "the love of money is the root of all evils" (Baxter, King James: "of all evil"). Baxter's "That" is dropped because the quotation now begins at "the love." The NET adds, in the next sentence, "Some people in reaching for it have strayed from the faith...", which Baxter quotes in Direction 8.
- **1 John 2:15:** NET wording ("the love of the Father is not in him"); Baxter's "if any man" is "if anyone."
- **1 Timothy 6:6-10 (Direction 8; flag):** NET wording of the whole passage. Differences from the King James that Baxter quotes: "food and raiment" is "food and shelter"; "they that will be rich fall into temptation and a snare" is "Those who long to be rich ... stumble into temptation and a trap"; "foolish and hurtful lusts, which drown men in destruction and perdition" is "senseless and harmful desires that plunge people into ruin and destruction"; "pierced themselves through with many sorrows" is "stabbed themselves with many pains"; "which while some coveted after" is "Some people in reaching for it." Baxter's words "(or been seduced)" are his own gloss on the King James "erred"; we kept them in parentheses after the NET's "strayed." The NET has no such alternative.
- **Mark 8:36-37 (flag):** The NET has "forfeit his life" and "in exchange for his life," where Baxter has "lose his own soul" and "in exchange for his soul." The Greek word can mean either "life" or "soul," and the King James chose "soul." Baxter's comment "that is, What money or price will recover it, if for the love of gain he lose it?" and his whole argument (damnation as the price of money) lean on "soul." With the NET wording the sense of "life" is still there, but the specific word "soul" is not. Baxter's "Or what shall a man give" is "What can a person give."
- **Proverbs 15:27 (flag):** The NET has "bribes" where Baxter has "gifts." Baxter's argument (greed troubles one's own house) is not affected, but "hates gifts" in the King James reads as wider than "hates bribes."
- **Proverbs 15:16:** NET wording: "great wealth and turmoil" for the King James "great treasure and trouble."
- **Direction 6 and others, small swaps:** "such like" kept; "hath its" to "has its"; "proportionably" to "proportionally"; "labours" to "labors." **meat** (Direction 8): "food" (Baxter's older sense). **parted** (Direction 9, of Christ's clothing): "divided."
- **1 Timothy 6:6 (Direction 8):** the NET begins "Now godliness combined with contentment"; Baxter's quotation begins at "Godliness," so "Now" is omitted.
- **Proverbs 3:14:** Cited only, in Baxter's words; the NET reads "For her benefit is more profitable than silver, and her gain is better than gold." Baxter takes it that "her" (wisdom) gain is better than gold.
- **Matthew 8:20; Luke 9:58:** NET wording from Luke 9:58 ("Foxes have dens and the birds in the sky have nests..."). Matthew 8:20 has a comma after "dens." Baxter's "holes" is "dens," "birds of the air" is "birds in the sky," and "hath not where to lay his head" is "has no place to lay his head."
- **2 Corinthians 8:9 (flag):** Baxter's printed quotation reads "for our sakes he became poor, that you through his poverty might be rich." The NET has "for your sakes"; Baxter's "our" is either his adaptation of the verse or a slip in the text (we have not confirmed which). We use the NET's "your." Baxter's "Though" is "Although."
- **Acts 3:6 (flag):** Baxter has "Silver and gold have we none" (plural, "we"), reading it as the apostles' words. In the NET it is Peter's, in the first person singular: "I have no silver or gold." Baxter's "yet tell him" (the lame man) is kept. The plural is Baxter's; the NET has one speaker.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "Master's talents ... austere" (Matthew 25:14-30; Luke 19:12-27); "good and faithful servants" (Matthew 25:21, 23); "Mammon" (Matthew 6:24; Luke 16:13); "godliness, with content, would have been greater gain" (1 Timothy 6:6, quoted in the following Direction); "calling and election sure" (2 Peter 1:10); "to send Peter to a fish ... to pay their tribute" (Matthew 17:27); "when they parted it" (Matthew 27:35; John 19:23-24); "had all things common" and the selling of possessions (Acts 2:44-45; 4:32-35); "face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12); "his image and superscription" (compare Matthew 22:20).
- **For all that** (Direction 6): kept; Baxter's "even though." **a deal of** (Direction 8): "a lot of." **greatening:** Baxter's word, rendered "increasing" (Directions 7 and 8).
- **talents** (Direction 6): kept; Baxter uses the sense of the parable, a sum of money. **austere:** kept. **improvement** (Direction 6, and "by the improvement of which," Direction 11): kept in Baxter's sense of putting to good use. **worldling:** kept. **Mammon:** kept.
- **inconsiderable** (Direction 7): Baxter's word, rendered "insignificant." **pretended necessaries** (Direction 8): "supposed necessities."
- **gallant attendance** (Direction 9): Baxter's word "gallant" meaning fine, showy; rendered "splendid." **fain** (Direction 9): "had to," in "he was fain to send Peter." **scrape and care for riches:** kept (scrape = hoard, gather); "do thou" is "do you."
- **contemned, contemn** (Directions 9 and 10): "despised, despise." **primitive Christians** (Direction 10): "early Christians" (Baxter means the first generation).
- **lusts** (Direction 11): left as Baxter has it (ordinary desires of the flesh). **provender:** kept; it means feed or provisions. **furniture:** kept; in his day it means equipment. **creature(s)** (Direction 11): kept in Baxter's sense of "created things," as in the Latin of note 266. **solacing:** "comforting." **superscription:** "inscription." **amiable:** "lovable." **bespeak:** "call for." **presently:** "at once." **delectable:** "delightful." **enow:** "enough." **brethren:** "brothers and sisters." **spare not:** "do not hold back." **yea:** "yes." **dote upon:** "dote on." **do cause the melody:** the verb is made singular ("does cause") to agree with "the touch."
- **Donor** (Direction 11): God as the giver. **a due progression:** left as written. **the sense** (Direction 11): Baxter plays on "sense" as meaning, as in letters and sense; kept.
- **Socrates, Laertius** (note 260): Baxter's "Laert" is probably Diogenes Laertius (we have not confirmed this); we have not checked the passage. **honestatis** is rendered "honor"; it might mean "honorable character."
- **Petrarch** (note 262): Baxter's "Dial. 13. li. 2." is a reference to a work of Petrarch's, which we have not identified or checked. The note in the source is in Latin.
- **Plutarch** (note 263): Baxter's "de tranquillit. anim." is kept as printed and probably refers to a Plutarch work on tranquility of mind (not confirmed); the stories of Alexander and Crates are as Baxter reports them, and we have not checked them.
- **Chrysostom** (note 265): Presumably John Chrysostom (not confirmed); the claim is Baxter's and we have not checked it.
- **Scaliger** (note 266): "Paul. Scaliger" is as printed; we have not identified the work or confirmed the reference "Ep. Cath. 1. 14. Thess. 123. p. 689" (our "Catholic Epistles" is a guess at "Ep. Cath."), and so render the Latin loosely.
- **Dionysius, Plato, Aristippus, Croesus** (note 267): Baxter's examples of rulers (Dionysius the tyrant, Croesus) who were generous to philosophers (Plato, Aristippus, and others); we make no claim about them. "Laert. in Platone" is Laertius's life of Plato.
- **Psalm 37:16; Proverbs 16:8** (note 264) and **1 Timothy 6:5-6; Proverbs 3:14** (note 261): converted from Baxter's old-style numbering; not quoted by Baxter in his notes.
