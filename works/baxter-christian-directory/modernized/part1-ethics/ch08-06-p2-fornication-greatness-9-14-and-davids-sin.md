# Chapter 8: Part 5, Title 1, the greatness of the sin of fornication (9–14) and an objection from David and Solomon

*This file begins partway through Baxter's numbered list showing the greatness of the sin of fornication (Part 5, Title 1), at point 9; points 1–8 are in the previous file. It ends with the heading of section II, whose directions continue in the next file.*

9. Yes, lust corrupts the mind of the person himself, if it is not very much restrained and moderated. It turns it from the only excellent pleasure, by the force of that brutish kind of pleasure. [450] It carries away the thoughts, and disorders the passions, and corrupts the imagination, and thereby easily corrupts the intellect and heart. Pleasure is so much of the end of man, which his nature leads him to desire, that the chief thing in the world to make a man good and happy is to engage his heart to those pleasures which are good, and make men happy. And the chief thing to make him bad and miserable is to engage him in the pleasures which make men bad and end in misery. And the principal thing by which you may know yourselves or others, what you are, is to know what your pleasures are; or at least, what you choose and desire for your pleasure. If the body rules the soul, you are brutish, and shall be destroyed: if the soul rules the body, you live according to true human nature and the ends of your creation. If the pleasures of the body are the predominant pleasures which you are most addicted to, then the body rules the soul, and you shall perish as traitors to God, that debase his image, and turn man into beast (Romans 8:13): if the pleasures of the soul are your most predominant pleasures, which you are most addicted to (though you attain as yet but little of it), then the soul rules the body, and you live like men: and this cannot well be till faith shows the soul those higher pleasures in God and everlasting glory, which may carry it above all fleshly pleasures. By all this set together you may easily perceive that the way of the devil to corrupt and damn men is to keep them from faith, that they may have no heavenly, spiritual pleasure, and to strengthen indulgence in physical pleasure, and give them their fill of fleshly pleasures, to imprison their minds that they may ascend no higher: and that the way to sanctify and save men is to help them by faith to heavenly pleasure, and to abate and keep under that fleshly pleasure that would draw down their minds. And by this you may see how to understand the doctrine of mortification, and taming the body, and abstaining from the pleasures of the flesh: and you may now understand what personal mischief lust does to the soul.

10. Your own experience and consciences will tell you that if it is not exceedingly moderated, it unfits you for every holy duty. You are unfit to meditate on God, or to pray to him, or to receive his word or sacrament: and therefore nature teaches those that meddle with holy things to be more continent than others; which Scripture also seconds (1 Samuel 21:4–5). Such physical things and sacred things do not well agree too near. [451]

11. And as by all this you see sufficient cause why God should make stricter laws for the bridling of lust than fleshly, lustful persons like; so when his laws are broken by the unclean, it is a sin that conscience (till it is quite debauched) deeply accuses the guilty for, and bears a very clear testimony against. Oh the unquietness! the horror! the despair that I have known many persons in, even for the sin of self-pollution, that never proceeded to fornication! And how many adulterers and fornicators have we known that have lived and died in despair, and some of them hanged themselves! Conscience will condemn this sin with a heavy condemnation, till custom or unbelief has utterly seared it. [452]

12. And it is also very observable that when men have once mastered conscience in this point, and reconciled it to this sin of fornication, it is a hundred to one that they are utterly hardened in all abomination, and scarcely make conscience of any other villainy whatsoever! [453] If once fornication goes for nothing, or a small matter with them, usually all other sin is with them of the same account: if they have but an equal temptation to it, lying, and swearing, and perjury, and theft, yes, and murder and treason, would seem small too: I never knew any one of these but he was reconcilable and prepared for any villainy that the devil set him upon: and if I know such a man, I would no more trust him than I would trust a man that lacks nothing but interest and opportunity to commit any heinous sin that you can name. Though I confess I have known several of the former sort, that have committed this sin under horror and despair, that have retained some good in other points, and have been recovered; yet of this latter sort, that have reconciled their consciences to fornication, I never knew one that was recovered, or that retained anything of conscience or honesty, but so much of the show of it as their pride and worldly interest commanded them: and they were malignant enemies of goodness in others, and lived according to the unclean spirit which possessed them. [454] They are terrible words (Proverbs 2:18–19), "For she has set her house by death and her paths by the place of the departed spirits. None who go in to her will return, nor will they reach the paths of life." Age keeps them from actual filthiness and lust (and so may hell, for there is no fornication); but they retain their debauched, seared consciences.

13. And it is the greater sin because it is not committed alone; but the devil takes them by couples. Lust inflames lust: and the fuel set together makes the greatest flame. You are guilty of the sin of your wretched companion, as well as of your own.

14. Lastly, the miserable effects of it, and the punishments that in this life have attended it, tell us how God accounts of the sin: it has ruined persons, families, and kingdoms; and God has borne his testimony against it by many striking judgments, which almost all histories acquaint you with. [455] As there is scarcely any sin that the New Testament more frequently and bitterly condemns (as you may see in Paul's Epistles, 2 Peter 2, Jude, etc.), so there are not many that God's providence more frequently pursues with shame and misery on earth: and in the latter end of the world, God has added one accompanying plague not known before, called commonly the *lues venerea*, the venereal pox, so that many of the most brutish sort go about branded with a mark of God's vengeance, the sign or warning of a heavier vengeance. And there are none of them all (that by great repentance are not made new creatures) but leave an infamous name and memory when they are dead (if their sin was publicly known). Let them be ever so great, and ever so gallant, victorious, successful, generous, flattered, or applauded while they lived, God orders it so that truth shall ordinarily prevail with the historians that write of them when they are dead; and with all sober men their names rot and stink, as well as their bodies. Proverbs 10:7, "The memory of the righteous is a blessing, but the reputation of the wicked will rot." So much of the greatness of the sin. Boniface, archbishop of Mainz, writing to Ethilbald, an English king that was a fornicator (Letter 19), says, Fornication is a reproach, not only among Christians, but pagans.... For in old Saxony if a virgin had thus stained her father's house, or a married woman, breaking the marriage covenant, had committed adultery; sometimes they force her to hang herself with her own hand, and over her ashes when she is burnt they hang the fornicator; sometimes they gather a band of women, they lead her about, scourging her with rods; and cutting off her clothes at the waist, and with small knives cutting and pricking all her body, they send her from village to village, thus bloody and mangled with little wounds; and so more and more, incited by a zeal for chastity, meet her and scourge her again, till they leave her either dead or scarcely alive, that others may fear adultery and lust. And the Wineds, which are the filthiest and worst sort of men, keep the love of matrimony with so great a zeal that the woman will refuse to live when her husband is dead. And after some reproofs of the fornicating king, he adds these further stories. Ceolred, your Highness' predecessor, as they witness who were present, he being splendidly banqueting with his earls, was by the evil spirit that drew him to violate God's law suddenly driven mad in his sin; so that without repentance and confession, being raging mad and talking with the devil, and abominating God's priests, he departed out of this life, no doubt to the torments of hell. And Osred (king of the Deiri and Bernicii) the spirit of lust carried in fornication and defiling the sacred virgins in the monasteries, till such time as by a vile and base kind of death he lost his glorious kingdom, together with his youthful and lustful life. Therefore, most dear son, take heed of the ditch into which you have seen others fall before you.... See *Auctarium Bibliothecae Patrum*, volume 2, pp. 55, 56.

And how great sufferings were laid on priests, monks, and nuns that had committed fornication, by several years' imprisonment and scourging, see the same work, p. 84, in an edict of Carloman, by the advice of a council of bishops.

And in Letter 85, p. 87, Boniface writes to Lullo that he was obliged to allow a priest to officiate, baptize, pray, etc., that had long ago committed fornication, because there was none but he alone to be had in all the country, and he thought it better to venture that one man's soul than let all the people perish, and desires Lullo's counsel in it. By all which we may see how heinous a sin fornication was then judged.

*Objection.* But (say the filthy ones) did not David commit the sin of adultery? Did not God permit them many wives among the Jews? How many had Solomon? Therefore this is no such great sin as you claim. Thus every filthiness a little while will plead for itself.

*Answer.* David did sin; and is the sin any the less for that? It is easier to refrain from it than undergo the tears and sorrows which David did endure for his sin! Besides the bitterness of his soul for it, his son Absalom rebels and drives him out of his kingdom, and his own wives are openly defiled; and yet God leaves it as a perpetual blot upon his name. Solomon's sin was so great that it almost ruined him and his kingdom; though experience caused him to say more against it than is said in the Old Testament by any other, yet it is a controversy among divines whether he was ever recovered and saved; and ten tribes of the twelve were therefore taken from his line, and given to Jeroboam. And is this any encouragement to you to imitate him? Christ tells you in the case of divorce that God permitted (not allowed, but forbore) some such sins in the Jews, because of their "hard hearts" (Mark 10:5); but from the beginning it was not so; but one man and one woman were joined in the original institution. And the special reason why plurality was winked at among the Jews was for the fuller peopling of the nation: they being the only covenanted people of God, and being few among encompassing enemies, and being separated from the people of the earth, their strength, and safety, and glory lay much on their increased number, and therefore some disorder was winked at for their multiplication, but never absolutely allowed and approved of. And yet fornication is punished severely, and adultery with death.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized; Scripture in the NET where Baxter quotes it).

[450] Solomon's "wives shifted his allegiance to other gods" (1 Kings 11:4). The wisdom of Solomon did not preserve him from the power of lust, and the deceit of women. 1 Peter 2:11, "fleshly desires that do battle against the soul."

[451] Revelation 14:4.

[452] Says Chrysostom, The adulterer even before damnation is most miserable: always in fear, trembling at a shadow, fearing those that know, and those that do not know: always in pain, even in the dark.

[453] 1 Timothy 6:9, "harmful desires that plunge people into ruin and destruction."

[454] When an adulterer asked Thales whether he should make a vow against his sin, he answered him, Adultery is as bad as perjury: if you dare be an adulterer, you dare perjure yourself. Laert. Herod dared behead John, who dared be incestuous.

[455] Judges 19–20. The tribe of Benjamin was almost cut off upon the occasion of an adultery or rape. See Numbers 25:8; Genesis 12:17; 2 Samuel 12:10; Luke 3:19; 1 Corinthians 5:1; John 8:2. Vid. Ælian. fol. 47. (See Aelian, folio 47.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). Every verse quoted as NET was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Labels:** this file sits in Part 5, Title 1 (against fornication and all uncleanness). "Tit." in the source is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," a heading or division within a Part. At the opening of Title 1 Baxter says he will "1. show the greatness of the sin; and, 2. give directions for the cure"; the numbered points 9–14 here belong to the first of these, and the closing heading "II. The Directions against Fornication" opens the second (the source gives no matching "I." heading).
- **Proverbs 2:18–19:** NET wording. The King James Baxter quotes has "her house inclineth unto death, and her paths unto the dead: none that go unto her return again, neither take they hold of the paths of life." The NET's "the place of the departed spirits" replaces "the dead"; the sense Baxter uses (no return from her) is unchanged.
- **Proverbs 10:7:** NET wording. Baxter's King James has "the name of the wicked shall rot"; the NET has "the reputation of the wicked will rot." His point (an infamous name after death) is if anything sharpened by "reputation," but his own word "names rot" in the sentence before echoes the King James "name."
- **Mark 10:5:** Baxter quotes "hardness of their hearts" (the King James has "hardness of your heart"). The NET reads "your hard hearts"; we quote only "hard hearts" to fit Baxter's third-person sentence.
- **Romans 8:13; 1 Samuel 21:4–5; 2 Peter 2; Jude:** cited, not quoted, so left as references. In 1 Samuel 21:4–5 the NET speaks of soldiers who "have abstained from relations with women," which is the point Baxter draws on (those handling holy things are to be continent).
- **Note 450, 1 Kings 11:4:** the King James has "his wives turned away his heart after other gods"; the NET has "his wives shifted his allegiance to other gods." Baxter's word "heart" (lust turning the heart) is replaced by "allegiance" in the NET. **1 Peter 2:11:** King James "fleshly lusts, which war against the soul"; NET "fleshly desires that do battle against the soul."
- **Note 453, 1 Timothy 6:9:** King James "hurtful lusts, which drown men in destruction and perdition"; NET "harmful desires that plunge people into ruin and destruction." In context Paul speaks of desires that come from longing to be rich; Baxter applies the phrase to lust. The NET's "desires" is less pointed than the King James "lusts" for his use.
- **Note 455, John 8:2:** as printed. The story of the woman taken in adultery begins at John 8:3 (the passage John 7:53–8:11 is bracketed as a textual variant in the NET). We keep Baxter's reference.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): yea → yes; distempers → disorders; fantasy → imagination; sensuality → indulgence in physical pleasure; sensual (things) → physical; infidelity → unbelief; villany → villainy; wants → lacks; divers → several; signal (judgments) → striking; concomitant → accompanying; stigmatized → branded; prognostic → sign; liberal → generous; Never so → ever so; girdle → waist; luxury / luxurious (in the Boniface passage, the old sense "lechery") → lust / lustful; distracted (went mad) → driven mad; fain → obliged; suffer → allow; pretend → claim; ever the less → any the less; forbear (it) → refrain from; divorcement → divorce; conjoined → joined; primitive (institution) → original; connived at → winked at (Baxter means tolerated, not secretly conspired in); inordinacy → disorder; forswear thyself → perjure yourself (note 454).
- **Kept words:** *continent* (paragraph 10), meaning sexually self-restrained; *self-pollution* (paragraph 11), Baxter's term for masturbation; *mortification* (putting sin to death); *make conscience of* (treat as a matter of conscience, scruple at); *divines* (theologians); *plurality* (having more than one wife); *debauched, seared* (of conscience, compare 1 Timothy 4:2, an unmarked allusion).
- ***lues venerea*:** Latin, "venereal plague"; Baxter glosses it himself as "the venereous pox" (here "venereal pox"). This is very probably what is now called syphilis. His claim that it was "not known before" is his own; historians still discuss its origin.
- **Unmarked allusions:** "new creatures" (2 Corinthians 5:17); "the unclean spirit" (Mark 1:23 and elsewhere); Absalom's revolt and the public defiling of David's wives (2 Samuel 15–16; 2 Samuel 12:11); Solomon's sayings against the adulteress (Proverbs 5–7); the ten tribes given to Jeroboam (1 Kings 11:31); "from the beginning it was not so" (Matthew 19:8); adultery punished with death (Leviticus 20:10). References here are ours, not in Baxter's text.
- **The Boniface passage** (paragraph 14): Baxter gives it in English, a translation or close paraphrase of a Latin letter; we have modernized his English and not compared it with the Latin. "Mentz" is Mainz. "Ethilbald" is probably Æthelbald, king of Mercia (we keep Baxter's spelling). "The Wineds" are probably the Wends, a Slavic people. "Deiri and Bernicii" are probably the peoples of the two Northumbrian kingdoms Deira and Bernicia. Ceolred and Osred are kept as printed. "Auct. Bib. Pat. tom. ii." we take to mean the *Auctarium* (supplement) to the *Bibliotheca Patrum* (a collection of the Church Fathers), volume 2; we have not confirmed which edition Baxter used. "Lullo" is probably Lull, Boniface's successor at Mainz. "Carloman" is kept as printed. The dashes (----) in the source, marking skipped text, are rendered "...."
- **Note 452, Chrysostom:** Baxter's English rendering; we have not located the passage.
- **Note 454:** "Laert." probably refers to Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of the Philosophers*, where we believe the Thales anecdote is told (not checked); kept as printed. **Word order:** in paragraph 14, "which all histories almost acquaint you with" is given as "which almost all histories acquaint you with," since Baxter's "almost" goes with "all." Herod and John: Mark 6:17–28 (unmarked).
- **Note 455:** "Vid. Ælian. fol. 47." is kept with an English rendering; it presumably refers to the Greek writer Aelian, but we have not identified the edition or passage.
- **Baxter's claims** about the despair and suicide of adulterers, and that he never knew a fornicator with a reconciled conscience recover, are his own pastoral observations, kept as his.
