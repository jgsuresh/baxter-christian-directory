# Chapter 10: Title 2, Directions 2-12 against idleness and sloth

**Direction 2. Those persons must be extraordinarily watchful against this sin of idleness whose constitutions, unhappy educations, condition of life, or company do most strongly tempt them to it.**

It is a sin that some have but little temptation to in comparison with others: and some have need of a great deal of care and resolution to escape it. 1. Those are most subject to this sin who have a phlegmatic constitution, or dullness of spirits, or other bodily indisposition to cherish it: such therefore should strive the more against it, and not give way to any sloth which they are able to resist. Though their bodies are like a dull or tired horse, they must use the rod and spur the more. Such heavy persons are more given to sleep than others are; and yet they may resist it and rise early if they will, though they have a greater sluggishness than others to overcome. So though they are more indisposed to labor than more active persons are, yet if they will do their best, they may go as far as their strength of body will enable them. And this they should all the more strive to do (unless they have a disease that labor is hurtful to), because custom does much to the increasing or decreasing of their bodily indisposition, and labor is the most effective means to cure them of that fleshly heaviness which unfits them for their labor. [579]

2. Those that have been unhappily brought up in idleness have great cause to repent of their sinful life that is past, and to be doubly diligent to overcome this sin: if their parents have so far been their enemies, they should not continue enemies to themselves. Though usually the children of the rich and proud have this for their particular original sin, and are very unhappy in their parentage and education in comparison with the children of wise, and humble, and industrious parents, yet their own understanding and willingness, by the help of grace, may overcome it. If your parents had trained you up to live by stealing, could you not leave it if you would, when you come to know that God forbids it? so though they have brought you up in idleness, and done their part to ruin you both in soul and body, to make your souls a sty for sin, and your bodies a skinful of diseases, yet if you will do your part you may be recovered, at least as to your souls; and custom may conquer the fruits of custom. You cannot do worse than to go on, and spend the rest of your life in sin. If you had been stillborn, or murdered in your infancy, it would have been no sin for you to have lain idle in the common earth; but to teach a living soul to be idle, and to train up the living to a conformity to the dead (save only that they eat, and spend, and sin, and carry their ornaments on their backs, when the dead have theirs for a standing monument), this was great cruelty and treachery in your parents: but you must not therefore be as cruel and treacherous against yourselves. [580]

3. Those that abound in wealth, and have no need to labor for any bodily provisions, should be especially watchful against this sin. Necessity is a constant spur to the poor (except those that live upon begging, who are the second rank of idle persons in the land); but the rich and proud are under a continual temptation to live idly; for they need not rise early to labor for their bread; they need not work hard for food or clothing; they do not have the cries of their hungry children to rouse them up; they have plenty for themselves and family without labor, and therefore they think they may take their ease. But it is a sad case with poor souls when the commands of God go for nothing with them; or cannot do as much to make them diligent as poverty or want could do; and when God's service seems to them unworthy of their labor, in comparison with their own. Perhaps God may bring you to a necessity of laboring for your daily bread, if you so ill repay him for your plenty. But it is better that your idleness were cured by grace than by necessity: for when you labor only for your own supplies, your own supplies are your reward; but when you labor in true obedience to God, it is God that will reward you (Colossians 3:23, 24). I do, with very much love and honor, think of the industrious lives of some lords and ladies that I know, who hate idleness and empty pursuits, and spend their time in diligent labors suitable to their places. But it is matter of very great shame and sorrow to think and speak of the lives of too great a number of our gallants: to how little purpose they live in the world! If they take a true account of their lives (as God will make them wish they had done, when he calls them to account), how many hours, do you think, will be found to have been spent in any honest labor, or diligent work that is worthy of a Christian, or a member of the commonwealth! in comparison with all the rest of their time, which is spent in bed, in dressing, in ornaments, in idle talk, in playing, in eating, in idle wanderings and visits, and in doing nothing, or much worse? [581] How much of the day does idleness consume in comparison with any profitable work! Oh that God would make such know in time how dreadful a thing it is thus to imitate Sodom that was punished with the vengeance of eternal fire (Ezekiel 16:49; Jude 7), instead of imitating Christ. As for idle beggars, they do not read books, and therefore I shall not write for them: they are in this more happy than the idle gentry, that the law compels them to work, and does not leave them to themselves.

4. Those persons that live in idle company have special cause to fear this sin; for such will entangle you in idleness, and greatly hinder you from conscientious diligence.

5. Those servants that live in great men's houses, and are kept more for pomp and state than service, having little to do, should especially take heed of the sin of idleness. Many such take it for their happiness to live idly, and take that for the best service where they have least work. But have you nothing to do for yourselves, for soul or body? If you have leisure from your master's service, you should thankfully make good use of it in God's service and your own.

**Direction 3. Settle yourselves in a lawful calling, which will keep you under a necessity of ordinary and orderly employment.**

As we cannot so easily bring our minds to a close attendance upon God on the weekdays, when we have our common business to divert us, as we can do on the Lord's day, which is purposely set apart for it, and in which we have the use of his stated ordinances to assist us; even so a man that is out of a stated course of labor cannot avoid idleness so well as he that has his ordinary time and course of business to keep him constantly at work. It is a dangerous life to live out of a calling.

**Direction 4. Take heed of excess of food, and drink, and sleep; for these drown the senses, and dull the spirits, and load you with a burden of flesh or humors, and greatly indispose the body to all diligent, useful labors: a full belly and drowsy brain are unfit for work.**

It will seem work enough to such to carry the load of flesh or phlegm which they have gathered. A pampered body is more disposed to lust and wantonness than to work.

**Direction 5. A manly resolution is an effective course against sloth.**

Resolve and it will be done. Do not give way to a slothful disposition. Be up and doing: you can do it if you only resolve. To this end, never be without God's quickening motives (mentioned before) on your minds. Think what a sin and shame it is to waste your time; to live like the dead; to bury a rational soul in flesh; to be a slave to so base a thing as sloth; to neglect all God's work while he supports and maintains you, and looks on; to live in sloth, with such miserable souls, so near to judgment and eternity. Such thoughts well set home will make you stir, when a drowsy soul makes an idle body.

**Direction 6. Take pleasure in your work, and then you will not be slothful in it.**

Your very horse will go heavily where he goes unwillingly, and will go freely when he goes there where he would be. Either your work is good or bad: if it is bad, avoid it; if it is good, why should you not take pleasure in it? It should be pleasant to do good.

**Direction 7. To this end be sure to do all your work as that which God requires of you, and that which he has promised to reward; and believe his acceptance of your lowliest labors which are done in obedience to his will.**

Is it not a delightful thing to serve so great and good a Master, and to do that which God accepts and promises to reward? This interest of God in your lowest, and hardest, and most servile labor does make it honorable, and should make it sweet.

**Direction 8. Do not allow your fancies to run after physical, empty delights; for these will make you weary of your callings.**

No wonder if foolish youths are idle, whose minds are set upon their amusements; nor is it a wonder that pleasure-loving gentlemen live idly, who glut themselves with corrupting pleasures. The idleness of such pleasure-seekers is more inexcusable than other men's, because it is not the labor itself that they are against, but only such labor as is honest and profitable: for they can bestow more labor in play, or dancing, or running, or hunting, or any empty pursuit, than their work required; and it is the folly and sickness of their minds that is the cause, and not any disability in their bodies: the busiest in evil are the most slothful to good.

**Direction 9. Mortify the flesh, and keep it in an obedient dependence on the soul, and you will not be captivated by sloth.**

For idleness is but one way of flesh-pleasing: he that is a pleasure-loving slave to his flesh will please it in the way that it most desires; one man in fornication, and another in ambition, and another in ease; but he that has overcome and mortified the flesh has mastered this with the rest of its concupiscence.

**Direction 10. Remember always that time is short, and death makes haste, and judgment will be just, and that all must be judged according to what they have done in the body; and that your souls are precious, and heaven is glorious, and hell is terrible, and work is various and great, and hindrances are many; and that it is not idleness, but labor, that is comforting in the reviews of time; and this will powerfully expel your sloth.**

**Direction 11. Call yourselves daily or frequently to account for how you spend your time, and what work you do, and how you do it.**

Do not allow one hour or moment so to pass that you cannot give your consciences a just account of it.

**Direction 12. Lastly, watch against the slothfulness of those that are under your charge as well as against your own: some persons of honor and greatness are diligent themselves, and bestow their time for the service of God, their king and country, and their souls and families (and I wish we had more such): but if, in the meantime, their wives and children and many of their servants spend most of the day and year in idleness, and they are guilty of it, for want of a thorough effort to reform it, their burden will be found greater at last than they imagined.**

In a word, though the labor and diligence of a believing saint, and not that of a greedy worldling, is what tends to save the soul, and diligence in doing evil is but a making haste to hell; yet sloth in itself is so great a nourisher of vice, and deadly an enemy to all that is good; and idleness is such a course and swarm of sin, that all your understandings, resolution, and authority should be used to cure it in yourselves and others.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). No verse is quoted in this section; Baxter only cites or alludes, so his wording is kept throughout.
- **Where this file begins:** the range opens at the start of Direction 2 of Title 2 ("Title" is Baxter's word for a heading or division of a Part; this is Title 2 of Part 1 of Chapter 10, "Directions against idleness and sloth"). Direction 1 and the Title heading are in the preceding file, which we have not checked here.
- **calling** (Directions 3 and 8): Baxter's word for one's vocation or occupation, the work God has set a person to. Kept.
- **Colossians 3:23, 24** (point 3): cited, not quoted. In the NET, verse 24 reads "you will receive your inheritance from the Lord as the reward," which supports Baxter's point that God rewards work done in obedience.
- **Ezekiel 16:49; Jude 7** (point 3): "punished with the vengeance of eternal fire" is the King James phrase of Jude 7, used without quotation marks; we keep Baxter's wording. The NET has "suffering the punishment of eternal fire." Baxter leans on Ezekiel 16:49 for Sodom's idleness: the King James there speaks of "abundance of idleness," while the NET reads "enjoyed carefree ease." The sense he draws (Sodom's ease and plenty as sin) is still present in the NET, but the word "idleness" is not.
- **Unmarked allusion** (Direction 10): "judged according to what they have done in the body" echoes 2 Corinthians 5:10 (no reference in Baxter's text).
- **phlegmatic; dullness of spirits; humors; phlegm** (Direction 2, point 1; Direction 4): terms from the older medicine of the four humors. A "phlegmatic" constitution was one in which phlegm predominated, making a person sluggish; the "spirits" were thought to be fine bodily vapors that carried life and motion. Kept as Baxter's terms; they reflect the medicine of his day.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): extraordinary watchful / extraordinarily watchful; undisposed, undisposedness / indisposed, indisposition; the rather / all the more; effectual / effective; unfitteth / unfits; bred up / brought up; peculiar / particular (meaning "belonging especially to them"); undo / ruin; still-born / stillborn; raiment / clothing; It may be / Perhaps; requite / repay; vanity / empty pursuits (Directions 2 and 8); conscionable / conscientious; improve (leisure) / make good use of; meat / food; manlike / manly; still (keep him still at work) / constantly; Remember still / Remember always; comfortable / comforting; meanest / lowliest; servilest / most servile; sensual / physical; sensual gentlemen / pleasure-loving gentlemen; sensualists / pleasure-seekers; sports / amusements; slothfullest / most slothful; sensual slave / pleasure-loving slave; Suffer not / Do not allow; I would we had / I wish we had; endeavour / effort; covetous / greedy; "is it that tends" / "is what tends."
- **custom:** kept; Baxter means habit ("custom may conquer the fruits of custom": a contrary habit can undo what habit has produced).
- **places** (point 3): stations or positions in society. **gallants:** fashionable young men of means. **pomp and state** (point 5): show and dignity. Kept.
- **stated ordinances; stated course** (Direction 3): "stated" means fixed or regularly appointed. Kept.
- **quickening** (Direction 5): enlivening, stirring to life. **(mentioned before):** Baxter refers back to motives given earlier in this Title or chapter; we have not checked which passage.
- **fancies** (Direction 8): imagination, desires. **concupiscence** (Direction 9): sinful desire, lust. Kept.
- **"the second rank of idle persons"** (point 3) and **"the law compelleth them to work"**: Baxter's own claims about beggars and the vagrancy laws of his day, kept as his.
- **"course and swarm of sin"** (last paragraph): printed so; "course" may be a misprint for "cause" or "curse," but we are not sure and keep the source.
- **"and deadly an enemy"** (last paragraph): kept as printed; the "so" of "so great a nourisher" carries over to it.
- **Note 579:** the Plato anecdote is cited from Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of the Philosophers*, book 3 (Plato). The Latin "Quam me in parvis reprehendis?" is a little odd; the translation gives two possible readings.
- **Note 580:** "Callimachus, in *Attila*" is probably the Italian humanist Filippo Buonaccorsi, called Callimachus Experiens, who wrote a work on Attila; we have not confirmed this or the edition behind "Page 353." "meat" is rendered "food."
- **Note 581:** from Erasmus's *Colloquies*; we have not identified which colloquy. "chartarius" is rendered "card-player" loosely; "decoctor" is a bankrupt or squanderer; "scabies Gallica" is the "French disease," syphilis. "chap. v." is given as "chapter 5" as printed; we have not checked which chapter or section Baxter means.
