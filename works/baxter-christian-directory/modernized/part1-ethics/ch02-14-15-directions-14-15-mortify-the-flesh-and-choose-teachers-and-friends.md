# Chapter 2: Directions 14 and 15

**Direction 14. Be very diligent in mortifying the desires and pleasures of the flesh; and keep a continual watch upon your senses, appetite, and lusts; and do not throw yourselves into temptations, occasions or opportunities of sinning, remembering that your salvation depends on your success.**

The lusts of the flesh, and the pleasures of the world, are the common enemies of God and souls, and the damnation of those souls that perish. And there is no sort more liable to temptations of this kind, than those that are in the flower of their youth and strength. When all the senses are in their vigor, and lust and appetite are in their strength and fury, how great is the danger! and how great must your diligence be if you will escape! The appetite and lust of the weak and sick, are weak and sick as well as they; and therefore they are no great temptation or danger to them. The desire and pleasure of the senses do abate, as natural strength and vigor does abate: to such there is much less need of watchfulness; and where nature has mortified the flesh, there is somewhat the less for grace to do. There needs not much grace to keep the aged and weak from fornication, uncleanness, excessive amusements and carnal mirth: and gluttony and drunkenness also are sins which youth is much more liable to. Especially some bodies that are not only young and strong, but have in their bodily temperament and constitution a special inclination to some of these, such as lust, or amusement, or foolish mirth, there is need of a great deal of diligence, resolution, and watchfulness for their preservation. Lust is not like a corrupt opinion, that surprises us through a defect of reason, and vanishes as soon as truth appears; but it is a brutish inclination, which though reason must subdue and govern, yet the most perfect reason will not root out, but there it will still dwell. And as it is constantly with you, it will be stirring when objects are presented by the sense or imagination to allure. And it is like a torrent, or a headstrong horse, that must be held in at first, and is restrained only with difficulty if it once breaks loose and gets its head. If you are brought up in temperance and modesty, where there are no great temptations to gluttony, drinking, amusements, or wantonness, you may think a while that your natures have little or none of this concupiscence, and so may walk without a guard: but when you come where baits of lust abound, where women, and plays, and feasts, and drunkards are the devil's snares, and tinder, and bellows, to inflame your lusts, you may then find to your sorrow, that you had need of watchfulness, and that all is not mortified that is asleep or quiet in you. As a man that goes with a candle among gunpowder, or near thatch, should never be careless, because he goes in continual danger; so you that are young, and have naturally eager appetites and lusts, should remember that you carry fire and gunpowder always about you, and are never out of danger while you have such an enemy to watch.

And if once you allow the fire to kindle, alas! what work may it make, before you are aware! "Each one is tempted when he is lured and enticed by his own desires. Then when desire conceives, it gives birth to sin, and when sin is full grown, it gives birth to death" (James 1:14–15). Little knows the fish, when he is catching or nibbling at the bait, that he is swallowing the hook which will at once lay him on the bank. When you are looking on the cup, or gazing on alluring beauty, or wantonly dallying and pleasing your senses with things unsafe, you little know how far beyond your intentions you may be drawn, and how deep the wound may prove, how great the pain, or how long and difficult the cure. As you love your souls, observe Paul's counsel, "keep away from youthful passions" (2 Timothy 2:22). Keep at a full distance: do not come near the bait. If you get a wound in your consciences, by any willful, heinous sin, O what a state you will be in! How heartless toward private duty! afraid of God, who should be your joy; deprived of the comforts of his presence, and all the pleasure of his ways! How miserably will you be tormented, between the tyranny of your own concupiscence, the sting of sin, the pangs of conscience, and the terrors of the Lord! How much of the life of faith, and love, and heavenly zeal, will be quenched in a moment! I am to speak more of this afterward; and therefore will only say, for now, to all young converts that care for their salvation, "Mortify the flesh," and "always watch, and avoid temptations."

**Direction 15. Be exceedingly wary, not only what teachers you commit the guidance of your souls to, but also with what company you familiarly associate; [69] that they be neither such as would corrupt your minds with error, or your hearts with viciousness, profaneness, lukewarmness, or with a feverish, factious zeal: but choose, if possible, judicious, holy, heavenly, humble, blameless, self-denying persons, to be your regular companions, and close friends; but especially for your near relations.**

It is a matter of very great importance, what teachers you choose, with a view to your salvation. [70] In this the free grace of God makes a great difference between some people and others: for, as poor heathens and unbelievers have none that know more, than what the book of nature teaches (if so much); so in the several nations of Christians, it is hard for the people to have any, but such as the sword of the magistrate forces on them, or the stream of their country's custom recommends to them. And it is a wonder, if pure truth and holiness be supported by either of these. But, when and where his mercy pleases, God sends wise and holy teachers, with compassion and diligence to seek the saving of men's souls; so that none but the malignant and obstinate are deprived of their help.

Ambitious, proud, greedy, licentious, ungodly men, are not to be chosen for your teachers, if you have your choice. In a nation where true religion is in good standing, and has the magistrate's support, or the majority vote, some graceless men may join with better, in preaching and defending the purity of doctrine and holiness of life: and they may be very serviceable to the church herein; especially in expounding and disputing for the truth. But even there, more experienced, spiritual teachers are much more desirable: they will speak most feelingly, who feel what they speak; and they are fittest to bring others to faith and love, who believe, and love God and holiness themselves. They that have life, will speak more lively than the dead. And in most places of the world, the ungodliness of such teachers makes them enemies to the truth which is according to godliness: their natures are at enmity to the life and power of the doctrine which they should preach: and they will do their worst to corrupt the magistrates, and make them of their mind: and, if they can but get the sword to favor them, they are, usually, the cruelest persecutors of the sincere. As it is notorious among the papists, that the baits of power, and honor, and wealth, have so corrupted the body of their clergy, that they conspire to uphold a worldly government and religion; and, in express contradiction to sense and reason, and to antiquity, and the judgment of the church, and to the holy Scriptures, they captivate the ignorant and pleasure-loving to their tyranny and false worship, and use the seduced magistrates and multitude, to the persecuting of those that will not follow them to sin and to perdition. Beware of proud and worldly guides.

And yet it is not everyone that claims piety and zeal, that is to be heard, or taken for a teacher. But, 1. Such as preach, ordinarily, the substantial truths which all Christians are agreed in. 2. Such as make it the drift of their preaching, to raise your souls to the love of God, and to a holy, heavenly life, and are zealous against confessed sins. 3. Such as do not contradict the essential truths, by errors of their own; nor the doctrine of godliness, by wicked, malicious applications. 4. Such as do not push any ambitious, tyrannical designs of their own, but deny themselves, and aim at your salvation. 5. Such as are not too hot in winning you over to any peculiar opinion of their own: it being the prediction of Paul to the Ephesians, "Even from among your own group men will arise, teaching perversions of the truth to draw the disciples away after them" (Acts 20:30). 6. Such as are judicious with holy zeal, and zealous with judgment. 7. Such as are of experience in the things of God, and not young beginners, or novices in religion. 8. Such as defer to the judgments of the generality of wise and godly men, and are careful of the unity of the church; and not such as would draw you into a sect or party, to the contempt of other Christians; no, not to a party that has the favor of rulers and the people, to promote them. 9. Such as are gentle, peaceable, and charitable; and not such as burn with hellish malice against their brethren, nor with an ungodly, or cruel, consuming zeal. 10. Such as do not live in physical indulgence and wickedness, contrary to the doctrine which they preach; but show by their lives, that they believe what they say, and feel the power of the truths which they preach.

And your familiar companions have great advantage to help or hinder your salvation, as well as your teachers. [71] The matter is not so great, whom you meet by the way, or travel with, or trade and buy and sell with, as whom you make your intimate or familiar friends. For such have both the advantage of their interest in your affections, and also the advantage of their nearness and familiarity; and, if they have but also the advantage of higher abilities than you, they may be powerful instruments of your good or hurt. If you have a familiar friend, that will defend you from error, and help you against temptations, and lovingly reprove your sin, and feelingly speak of God, and the life to come, composing his talk from the inward power of faith, and love, and holy experience; the benefit of such a friend may be more to you, than of the most learned or greatest in the world. How sweetly will their speeches taste of the Spirit, from which they come! How deeply may they pierce a careless heart! How powerfully may they kindle in you a love and zeal for God and his commandments! How seasonably may they discover a temptation, prevent your fall, reprove an error, and restore your souls! How faithfully will they watch over you! How profitably will they stir you up, and spur you on; and pray with you fervently when you are cold; and remind you of the truth, and duty, and mercy, which you forget! It is a very great mercy to have a judicious, solid, faithful companion in the way to heaven.

But if your ears are daily filled with froth and folly, with coarse jokes or idle stories, with oaths and curses, with furious words or scorns and jeers against the godly, or with the sophistry of deceivers, is it likely this should leave a pleasant or wholesome taste on your minds? Is it likely that the effect should not be seen, in your lean or leprous hearts and lives, as well as the effects of an infected or unwholesome air or diet will be seen upon your diseased bodies? He is ungodly, that likes such company best: and he is proud and presumptuous, that will unnecessarily throw himself into it, in confidence that he will receive no hurt: and he is careless of himself, that will not cautiously avoid it: and few that long associate with such, come off without some notable loss; except when we live with such, as Lot did in Sodom, grieving for their sin and misery, or as Christ associated with publicans and sinners, with a holy zeal and diligence to convert and save them, or as those that have not liberty, who bear that which they have not power to avoid.

Among the rest, your danger is not least from them that are eager to win you over to some party or unsound opinion: that they think they are in the right, and that they do it in love, and that they think it necessary to your salvation, and that truth or godliness are the things which they profess, all this makes the danger much the greater to you, if it is not truth and godliness indeed, which they propose and plead for. And none are in more danger than the ungrounded and unexperienced, that yet are so wise in their own esteem, as to be confident that they know truth from error when they hear it, and are not afraid of any deceit, nor much suspicious of their own understandings. But of this earlier.

The like danger there is of the familiar company of lukewarm ones, or the profane. [72] At first you may be troubled at their sinful or unsavory talk, and make some resistance against the infection; but before you are aware, it may so cool and damp your graces, as will make your decay discernible to others. First, you will hear them with less offense; and then you will grow indifferent what company you are in; and then you will laugh at their sin and folly; and then you will begin to speak as they do; and then you will grow cold and more seldom in prayer and other holy duties; and if God does not stop it, at last your judgments will grow blind, and you will think all this allowable.

But of all bad company, the nearest is the worst. If you choose such into your families, or into your nearest marriage relationships, you cast water upon the fire; you imprison yourselves in such fetters as will gall and grieve you, if they do not stop you; you choose a life of constant, close, and great temptations: whereas, your grace, and comfort, and salvation, might be much promoted by the society of such as are wise and gracious, and suitable to your state. To have a constant companion to open your heart to, and join with in prayer, and edifying conversation, and faithfully help you against your sins, and yet to be patient with you in your frailties, is a mercy which worldlings neither deserve nor value.

[69] Namsi falsi et solo nomine tumidi, non modo non consulendi, sed vitandi sunt, quibus nihil est importunius, nihil insulsius, &c. Petrarch. Dial. 117. lib. 2. (English: For if [they are] false men, puffed up in name alone, they are not only not to be consulted but are to be avoided; than whom nothing is more troublesome, nothing more insipid, etc. Rendered loosely, since the sentence is cut off. Petrarch, Dialogue 117, book 2. The source prints "Namsi" for "Nam si".)

[70] Scientis est posse docere. Proverb. Sub indocto tamen doctus evadere potes, afflatu aliquo divino, ut Cicero loquitur. Augustinus de seipso testatur (cui non omnia credere nefas est) quod et Aristotelicas Categorias, quæ inter difficillima numerantur, et artes liberales, quas singulas à præceptoribus didicisse magnum dicitur) nullo tradente, omnes intellexit. Bernardus item, vir doctrina et sanctitate clarissimus, omnes suas literas (quarum inter cunctos sui temporis abundantissimus fuit) in silvis et in agris didicit, non hominum magisterio, sed meditando et orando, nec ullos unquam alios præceptores habuit, quam quercus et fagos. Petrarch. lib. 2. Dialog. 40. (English: It belongs to one who knows to be able to teach. Proverb. Yet under an unlearned man you can come out learned, by some divine inspiration, as Cicero puts it. Augustine testifies of himself (whom it is wrong not to believe in everything) that he understood, with no one teaching him, both the Aristotelian Categories, which are counted among the most difficult, and the liberal arts, each one of which it is said to be a great thing to have learned from teachers) all of them. Bernard likewise, a man most renowned for learning and holiness, learned all his letters (in which he was the most abundant of all in his time) in the woods and the fields, not by the teaching of men, but by meditating and praying, and never had any other teachers than oaks and beeches. Petrarch, book 2, Dialogue 40. Rendered loosely in places. The source's parentheses are unbalanced: a ")" after "didicisse magnum dicitur" has no matching opening bracket.)

[71] Imperat (Rex) ut nostræ religionis illorum mensa nullum communem haberent, neque cum Catholicis omnino vescerentur. Quæ res non ipsis aliquod præstitit beneficium, sed nobis maximum contulit lucrum: nam sisermo eorum sicut cancer consuevit serpere, quanto magis communis mensa ciborum potuit inquinare, cum dicat Apostolus, cum nefariis nec cibum habere communem. Victor. Utic. p. 418. Magnum virtutis præsidium societas bonorum, socius exemplo excitat, sermone recreat, consilio instruit, orationibus adjuvat, autoritate continet, quæ omnia solitudini desunt. Jos. Acosta, 1. 4. c. 13. Dicunt Stoici amicitiam solos inter bonos, quos sibi innicem studiorum similitudo conciliet, posse consistere. Porro amicitiam ipsam societatem quandam esse dicunt omnium quæ sunt ad vitam necessaria, cum amicis ut nobismet ipsis utamur: atque ob id amicum eligendum, amicorumque multitudinem inter expetenda ponunt: inter malos non posse constare amicitiam. Laert. in Zenone. (English: He [the king] commands that those of our religion should have no table in common with them [the grammar of this clause is garbled in the source], and that they should not eat with Catholics at all. This did not bring them any benefit, but gave us very great gain; for if their talk is accustomed to creep like a cancer, how much more could a common table of food defile, since the Apostle says not to have even food in common with the wicked. Victor of Utica [as the source abbreviates "Victor. Utic."], p. 418. Rendered loosely, since the first passage is garbled; the source prints "sisermo" for "si sermo". The society of the good is a great defense of virtue: a companion rouses by example, refreshes by conversation, instructs by counsel, helps by prayers, restrains by authority, all of which are lacking in solitude. Joseph Acosta, book 4, chapter 13. The Stoics say that friendship can exist only among the good, whom likeness of pursuits joins to one another. Further, they say friendship itself is a kind of partnership in all things necessary for life, in which we use our friends as ourselves; and on that account they hold that a friend is to be chosen, and put a multitude of friends among the things to be sought; and that friendship cannot exist among the wicked. Laertius, in the life of Zeno. The source prints "innicem" for "invicem".)

[72] Non tamen ut corporum, sic animorum morbi, transeunt ad nolentes: Imo vero nobilis animus, vitiorum odio, ad amorem virtutis accenditur. Petrarch. Dialog. de alior. morib. (English: Yet the diseases of souls do not, as those of bodies do, pass to the unwilling; indeed a noble mind is kindled to the love of virtue by hatred of vices. Petrarch, Dialogue on the morals of others.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). All NET wording was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **James 1:14–15 (NET flag):** Baxter's King James text reads "drawn away of his own lust, and enticed ... when lust hath conceived ... when it is finished." The NET has "lured and enticed by his own desires ... when desire conceives ... full grown." Baxter's warning is about lust in particular (the fire-and-gunpowder image); the NET's more general "desires" is less pointed, though the context he builds around it supplies the sense. The NET's "full grown" for "finished" slightly changes the picture (sin matures, rather than being completed). Baxter's reference was given before the quotation; we place it after, per our practice.
- **2 Timothy 2:22 (NET flag):** Baxter has "Flee youthful lusts." The NET has "keep away from youthful passions." The NET loses the verb "flee" (running away), but "keep away" fits Baxter's next sentence ("Keep at a full distance"). "Passions" is a broader word than "lusts"; Baxter reads the phrase as chiefly about lust.
- **Acts 20:30:** Baxter's "Of your ownselves shall men arise, speaking perverse things, to draw away disciples after them" is the King James; the NET has "Even from among your own group men will arise, teaching perversions of the truth to draw the disciples away after them." The sense he needs (error rising from within) is kept. The NET's "teaching perversions of the truth" is a little more explicit than the King James "speaking perverse things"; Baxter's use of the verse (a warning against teachers eager to win followers) is not changed by it.
- **Unmarked quotations at the end of Direction 14:** "Mortify the flesh" and "always watch, and avoid temptations" are in Baxter's quotation marks but carry no reference. They read as allusions rather than exact quotations. We keep Baxter's wording. Possible sources, not stated by Baxter and not confirmed as his intent: Romans 8:13 (NET: "by the Spirit you put to death the deeds of the body, you will live"); Matthew 26:41 (NET: "Stay awake and pray that you will not fall into temptation"); Luke 21:36 (NET: "stay alert at all times, praying"). We have not put any reference in the text.
- **Other unmarked allusions (kept in Baxter's wording):** Lot in Sodom grieving over its sin (probably 2 Peter 2:7–8, and Genesis 19; the NET of 2 Peter 2:7–8 has Lot "in anguish over the debauched lifestyle of lawless men" and "tormented in his righteous soul"); Christ with publicans and sinners (the Gospels, for example Matthew 9:10–13); "the truth which is according to godliness" (probably Titus 1:1; the NET there reads "the truth that is in keeping with godliness"); "lukewarmness" (compare Revelation 3:16, which we have not fetched). These are possibilities, not Baxter's own references.
- **Word swaps (Baxter's word, then ours):** lieth, to depends; cast not yourselves upon, to do not throw yourselves into; sports, to amusements; temperature and complexion, to bodily temperament and constitution (his words refer to the mix of bodily "humors" thought to govern a person's inclinations); extirpate, to root out; fantasy, to imagination; get the head, to get its head (a horse that takes the bit); bred up, to brought up; still (always), to always; suffer, to allow; presently, to at once; smart, to pain; case, to state; secret duty, to private duty (mainly prayer and devotion done alone); gripes of conscience, to pangs of conscience; wilful, to willful; covetous, to greedy; infidels, to unbelievers; countenanced, to supported; in credit, to in good standing; major vote, to majority vote; vitiated, to corrupted; sensual (of the ignorant), to pleasure-loving; sensually (of ministers' lives), to in physical indulgence and wickedness; cruellest, to cruelest; take heed, to beware; pretendeth, to claims; proselyting, to winning you over to; singular opinion, to peculiar opinion; bear reference to, to defer to; tender of, to careful of; unblamable, to blameless; inditing his discourse, to composing his talk; relish of, to taste of; provoke, to stir you up; put you on, to spur you on; mind you of, to remind you of; recover, to restore; ribaldry, to coarse jokes; relish (a taste on your minds), to taste; conjugal relations, to marriage relationships; edifying conference, to edifying conversation; ordinary (companions), to regular; familiars, to close friends; converse (with), to associate (with). In the Direction 15 heading and the later "converse" sentences, Baxter's "converse" means to keep company, not to talk.
- **Kept as Baxter's terms:** mortify/mortifying; concupiscence (the inclination to desire, especially forbidden desire); lust; carnal; malignant (spiteful, hostile to godliness); licentious; graceless; graces (the Spirit's gifts in the soul); sophistry; novices; publicans; papists (his word for Roman Catholics), and the whole sentence about them is his polemic, kept as he wrote it; worldlings; plays (the theater of his day); "leprous" and "lean" (his image of a spiritually diseased heart).
- **"the sword of the magistrate"; "get the sword to favor them":** Baxter's picture of civil power backing a religion; kept.
- **"prevent your fall"** (companions): kept; here "prevent" is close to the modern sense, though Baxter's older sense is "go before" (forestall).
- **"in express contradiction to sense and reason":** "sense" here is common sense; kept as written.
- **Numbered list (1 to 10):** these run inside one paragraph in the source, and we keep them inline in Baxter's order and punctuation.
- **"But of this before"** (changed to "But of this earlier"): Baxter's cross-reference to something he said earlier, probably in the discussion of sects and unsound opinions. We have not checked where.
- **Baxter's heading sentence for Direction 15** is long and grammatically loose ("especially for your near relations"); we keep it as written.
- **Footnote placement:** in the source marker [69] stands inside the heading of Direction 15; we keep it there. Our practice puts a space before each marker.
- **Footnote 70:** the Augustine passage is Baxter's source's wording as printed (its parenthesis is unbalanced). Our reading of "cui non omnia credere nefas est" is loose. "Bernardus" is probably Bernard of Clairvaux, but the source does not say, and we have not confirmed it.
- **Footnote 71:** "Victor. Utic." is printed as an abbreviation; we have not confirmed which work or author is meant beyond the abbreviation (probably Victor of Utica, as we render it). Acosta is the same author Baxter cites elsewhere on the native peoples of the Americas; here Acosta, book 4, chapter 13, speaks of the value of good company. "Laert. in Zenone" is presumably Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers*, on Zeno; we have not verified the passage. The Apostle's saying about not eating with the wicked is an unmarked allusion, probably to 1 Corinthians 5:11 (NET: "not to associate with anyone who calls himself a Christian who is sexually immoral, or greedy ... Do not even eat with such a person"); that is our guess, not Baxter's reference.
- **Footnotes 69, 70, 72:** the author is given only as "Petrarch" with the dialogue number; we have not identified the work.
