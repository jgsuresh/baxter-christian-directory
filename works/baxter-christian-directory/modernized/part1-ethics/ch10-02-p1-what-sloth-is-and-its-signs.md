# Chapter 10: Title 2, what sloth and idleness are, and their signs

**Title 2. Directions against idleness and sloth.**

*In the margin: What sloth and idleness is.*

Here I must show you what idleness and sloth is, and what are the signs of it; and then give you directions how to conquer it. Sloth signifies chiefly the indisposition of the mind and body; and idleness signifies the actual neglect or omission of our duties. Sloth is an aversion to labor, through a carnal love of ease, or indulgence of the flesh. This aversion to labor is sinful when it is a voluntary reluctance toward that labor which is our duty. Sloth shows itself, 1. In keeping us from our duty, and causing us to delay it, or omit it: and, 2. In making us do it slowly and by halves: and both these effects are called idleness, which is the omission or negligent performance of our duties through a flesh-pleasing reluctance toward labor.

*In the margin: What it is not.*

By this you may see, 1. That it is not sloth or sinful idleness to omit a labor which we are unable to perform: as for the sick, and aged, and weak to be averse to labor through the power of an irresistible disease or weakness; or when nature is already wearied by as much labor as it can bear. 2. Or when reason allows and requires us to refrain from our usual labor for our health, or for some other sufficient cause. 3. Or when we are unwillingly restrained and hindered by others; as by imprisonment, or denial of opportunity: as if the magistrate forcibly hinders a preacher, or physician, or lawyer from that which otherwise he should do. 4. Or if a mistake or sinful error only keeps a man from his labor, it is a sin, but not this sin of sloth; so also if any physical vice or pleasure besides this love of ease takes him off. 5. If it is a reluctance only toward such labor as is no duty to us, it is but a natural and not a sinful sloth. But involuntary aversion to the labor of our duty through indulgence of fleshly ease is the sinful sloth or laziness which we speak of.

*In the margin: The aggravations of it.*

Sloth and idleness thus described is a sin in all; but a far greater sin in some than in others. [572] And you may thus know what sloth it is that is the most sinful. 1. The more sloth is seated in the mind itself, and the less it is seated in the body, the greater is the sin. For the mind is the nobler part, and immediate seat of sin. 2. The smaller the bodily disorders or temptations are which seduce the mind, the greater is the sin; for it shows the mind to be the more corrupted and tainted with the disease of sloth. He that is under an irresistible indisposition of body sins not at all (except insofar as he voluntarily contracted that disease). But if the body's indisposition to labor is great, but yet not irresistible, it is a sin to yield to it; but so much the smaller sin, *cæteris paribus*, as the bodily disease is greater. He that has some scorbutic lassitude, or phlegmatic heaviness and dullness, sins if he does not strive against it as much as he can, and as in reason he should: it is not every bodily indisposition that will excuse a man from all labor, as long as he is able to labor notwithstanding that disease; but if the disease is great, so that he resists his lassitude with a great deal of labor, the sin is the less: but he that has a body sound and able, that has no disease to indispose him, sins most of all if he is slothful, as showing the most corrupted mind. 3. He is most sinfully slothful who is most voluntarily slothful. As he that makes the least effort against it, and he that most loves it, and would not leave it; and he that is least troubled at it, and least repents and laments it, and contrives to accommodate his sloth. 4. The sloth is, *cæteris paribus*, the worst which most leads to the omission or negligent performance of our duty; but that sloth which does but indispose us, but is so far conquered by our resistance as not to keep us from our duty, or not much and often, is the smaller sin. 5. That is the most sinful sloth, *cæteris paribus*, which is against the greatest duties: to be reluctant toward the most holy duties (as praying, and hearing or reading the word of God, etc.) or toward duties of public consequence, is a greater sin than to be lazily reluctant toward a common, toilsome work. 6. That is the most sinful sloth and idleness which is committed against the greatest motives to labor and diligence: therefore, in that respect, a poor man's sloth is more sinful than a rich man's, because he is under the pressure of necessity; and in another respect the rich man's sloth is worst, because he buries the greatest talents, and is idle when he has the greatest wages. A man that has many children sins more than another by his idleness, because he wrongs them all whom he must provide for. A magistrate or pastor of the church sins incomparably more than common people, if they are slothful; because they betray the souls of men, or sin against the good of many. As it is a greater sin to be lazy in quenching a fire in the city than in a common, needless business; so it is a greater sin to be slothful in the working out of our salvation, and making our calling and election sure, when God, and Christ, and heaven, and hell are the motives to rouse us up to duty, and when the time is so short in which all our work for eternity must be done, I say, it is a far greater sin than to be slothful when only bodily needs or benefits are the motives which we resist. Yet indeed the will of God is resisted in all, who forbids us to be "slothful in business" (Romans 12:11).

*In the margin: The signs of sloth.*

Sloth is a thing that is easily discerned: the signs of it are, 1. When the very thought of labor is troublesome and unpleasing, and ease seems sweet. 2. When duty is omitted hereby and left undone. 3. When the easy part of duty is picked out, and the harder part is cast aside. 4. When the judgment will not believe that laborious duty is a duty at all. 5. When that which you do is done with reluctance, and with a constant weariness of mind, and there is no alacrity or pleasure in your work. 6. When you do no more in much time than you might do in less, if you had a willing, ready mind. 7. When the reluctant mind is putting it off with excuses, or finding something else to do, or at least delaying it. 8. When you choose a condition of greater ease and smaller labor before a laborious condition of life which in other respects is better for you. As when a servant would rather live in an ungodly family where there is more ease (and plenty) to be had, than in a place of greatest advantage for the soul, where there is more labor (and need). 9. When little impediments discourage or stop you. "The sluggard has said, 'There is a lion in the road!'" (Proverbs 26:13; 22:13). "The way of the sluggard is like a hedge of thorns" (Proverbs 15:19). "He will not plow because of the cold" (Proverbs 20:4). 10. When you make a great matter of a little business. It cannot be done but with such preparation, and so much fuss, that shows a slothful mind in the doer. Even the "putting his hand to his mouth," and "pulling it out of his bosom," is a business with the sluggard; that is, he makes a great matter of a little one (Proverbs 26:15; 19:24). 11. Lastly, the fruits of slothfulness usually expose it, in soul, and body, and estate; for it corrupts, impoverishes, and ruins all. The weeds of his field or garden, the vices of his soul, the sins of his life, the duties omitted, or sleepily performed, the disorders of his family or charge, and usually, or often, his poverty, expose him (Proverbs 24:30; 12:24, 27). [573]

By this much it is easy to discern the impudent folly of the Quakers and some ignorant rustics, that rail against magistrates and ministers for living idly, because they do not plow or thresh, or use some manual trade or labor; as if the labor of their highest calling were no labor, but mere idleness. Thus proud men speak evil of that which they do not understand! Had they tried it, they would have found that the work of a faithful minister is further distant from idleness than a thresher is. Do not Christ and the Holy Spirit often call them "laborers, fellow laborers with Christ, and workmen, and their work a labor"? (Luke 10:27; 1 Corinthians 3:9; 1 Timothy 5:17–18; 2 Timothy 2:15; Matthew 10:10; 1 Corinthians 3:13–15; 9:1; Ephesians 4:12; Philippians 2:30).

Hence also you may see, 1. That though all that can must labor, yet there is great diversity of labors; and all men are not to do the same work. Magistrates, and pastors, and lawyers, and physicians must labor diligently; but they are not all bound to plow, and thresh, and use the more servile labors of their inferiors. 2. That every man must labor in the works of his own calling, and "attend to [his] own business" (1 Thessalonians 4:11; 2 Thessalonians 3:11); and take that for the best employment for him which God calls him to, and not presume to step out of his place, and take the work of other men's callings out of their hands. 3. That a man that is paid for his labor by another (as lawyers, physicians, schoolmasters, servants) rob them by their idleness when they withhold from them any part of that which they are paid for.

[572] It was one of Solon's laws: Is qui sectatur otium, omnibus accusare volentibus obnoxius esto. (Let him who pursues idleness be liable to anyone who wishes to accuse him.) Ut Laert. in Sol. (As Laertius [reports] in [his life of] Solon.) Num solum aquas haurio, inquit Cleanthes? nonne et fodio et rigo et omnia facio philosophisæ causa? ("Do I only draw water?" said Cleanthes. "Do I not also dig and water and do everything for the sake of philosophy?") when they asked him why he would draw water.

[573] How little have some men (yes, ministers themselves) to show of all the good they might have done through all their lives! The work they have done calls them idle.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **Title:** "Tit." in the source is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," meaning a heading or division. Part 1 of Chapter 10 is divided into Titles; this file begins Title 2.
- **calling:** Baxter's word for one's vocation or occupation, the work God calls a person to. Kept throughout.
- **Romans 12:11:** Baxter quotes the King James "not slothful in business." The NET reads "Do not lag in zeal, be enthusiastic in spirit, serve the Lord," which has no word for "business." Since his point rests on the King James wording (that God forbids sloth in our work), we keep his words and flag the difference.
- **Proverbs 26:13; 22:13:** NET wording of 26:13 (the first line only). 22:13 in the NET reads "There is a lion outside!" Baxter's "slothful" is the NET's "sluggard."
- **Proverbs 15:19:** NET wording. The King James has "The way of the slothful man is as an hedge of thorns"; Baxter quotes it as "His way is an hedge of thorns." We give the NET phrase, which names the sluggard where Baxter has "His."
- **Proverbs 20:4:** Baxter's "He will not plough by reason of cold" follows the King James. The NET reads "The sluggard will not plow during the planting season," with no mention of cold. Baxter uses the verse as an example of a little impediment (cold weather) stopping the sluggard, so the NET changes the point he leans on; we keep his wording, modernized, and flag it.
- **Proverbs 26:15; 19:24:** Baxter's "putting his hand to his mouth" and "pulling it out of his bosom" is his own paraphrase of the King James ("hideth his hand in his bosom ... bring it again to his mouth"). The NET reads "plunged his hand in the dish; he is too lazy to bring it back to his mouth" (26:15) and "plunged his hand into the dish, and he will not even bring it back to his mouth!" (19:24). The NET's "dish" replaces the King James "bosom"; Baxter's point (the sluggard makes a labor of the smallest motion) stands either way. We keep his paraphrase.
- **"laborers, fellow laborers with Christ, and workmen, and their work a labor"** (Quaker paragraph): a blend of King James phrases from the verses he lists, not a single quotation; kept in his wording, modernized. The NET uses "workers" (Luke 10:2, 7; Matthew 10:10; 1 Timothy 5:18), "coworkers belonging to God" (1 Corinthians 3:9), "a proven worker" (2 Timothy 2:15), and "work hard in speaking and teaching" (1 Timothy 5:17). Note that 1 Corinthians 3:9 in the NET does not say "with Christ" (Baxter's own reading of "labourers together with God").
- **Luke 10:27** (Baxter's reference): this verse is the command to love God and neighbor, and has nothing about laborers. It is probably a misprint for Luke 10:2 or 10:7, which speak of "workers," but we are not sure which, so we keep Baxter's reference.
- **1 Thessalonians 4:11:** NET wording ("to attend to your own business"), with "[his]" in place of "your" to fit Baxter's sentence about "every man." 2 Thessalonians 3:11 in the NET speaks of those "not doing their own work but meddling in the work of others."
- **Proverbs 24:30; 12:24, 27** (signs of sloth, no. 11): cited by Baxter, not quoted.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "buries the greatest talents" (the parable of the talents, Matthew 25:14–30); "working out of our salvation" (Philippians 2:12); "making our calling and election sure" (2 Peter 1:10); "speak evil of that which they do not understand" (Jude 10; 2 Peter 2:12).
- **involuntary** (end of "What it is not"): the source prints "But involuntary averseness to the labour of our duty ... is the sinful sloth." This contradicts Baxter's definition just above (sinful sloth is a "voluntary backwardness"), so it is probably a misprint for "voluntary." We keep the source's word.
- **cæteris paribus:** Latin, "other things being equal." Kept in italics.
- **scorbutic lassitude:** weariness from scurvy (Baxter's "scorbutical"). **phlegmatic heaviness:** sluggishness attributed to an excess of phlegm, one of the four humors in the medicine of Baxter's day; a dated medical idea.
- **carnal:** unspiritual; ruled by the flesh. Kept. **magistrate:** a civil ruler or official. Kept.
- **Quakers:** Baxter's polemic against them is kept as he wrote it. **rustics:** country people.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): averseness, aversion; backwardness (and "backward"), reluctance (and "reluctant"); forbear, refrain from; unresistible, irresistible; subjected (in the mind / body), seated; distempers, disorders; vicious (sloth), sinful (meaning "of vice," not "cruel"); sensual (vice), physical; endeavoureth least, makes the least effort; prevaileth to, leads to; corporal wants, bodily needs; culled out, picked out; with an ill will, with reluctance; shifting it off, putting it off; fulness, plenty; want, need; ado, fuss; use to detect, usually expose; detect, expose; thrash, thresh; mechanic trade, manual trade; Holy Ghost, Holy Spirit; yea, yes (note 573).
- **estate:** one's property and worldly condition. **charge:** those under one's care. Both kept.
- **"a man that is paid ... rob them"** (last paragraph): Baxter's grammar shifts from "a man" to the plural "they"; we keep it as he wrote it.
- **Solon; Laertius; Cleanthes** (note 572): Solon was the Athenian lawgiver; "Laert." is Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of the Philosophers*; Cleanthes was a Stoic philosopher, remembered (as Baxter's anecdote implies) for doing manual labor such as drawing water. We have not checked the exact passages in Diogenes Laertius. "philosophisæ" is printed as in the source and is probably a misprint for "philosophiæ" (of philosophy).
