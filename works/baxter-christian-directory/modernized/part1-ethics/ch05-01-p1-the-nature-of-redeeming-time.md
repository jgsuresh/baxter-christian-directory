# Chapter 5: The nature of redeeming time (what time is, its seasons, and the price of redeeming it)

**Chapter 5. Further subordinate directions for the next great duties of religion; necessary to the right performance of the former.** [280]

**Directions for redeeming or making good use of time.**

Time being man's opportunity for all those works for which he lives, and which his Creator expects from him, and on which his endless life depends, the redeeming or making good use of it must necessarily be of the highest importance to him; and therefore it is well made by holy Paul the great mark to distinguish the wise from fools: "Therefore consider carefully how you live—not as unwise but as wise, taking advantage of every opportunity" (Ephesians 5:15–16); so Colossians 4:5. I shall therefore give you special directions for it, when I have first explained the nature of the duty to you, and told you what is meant by time, and what by redeeming it.

*In the margin: What is meant by time.*

Time, in its most common sense, is taken generally for all that space of this present life which is our opportunity for all the works of life, and the measure of them. Time is often taken more strictly, for some special opportunity which is fitted to a special work, which we call the season or the fittest time: in both these senses time must be redeemed.

*In the margin: What are the special seasons of duty.*

As every work has its season which must be taken (Ecclesiastes 3:1); so have the greatest works assigned us for God and our souls some special seasons besides our common time. 1. Some times God has fitted by nature for his service. So the time of youth, and health, and strength is especially fitted for holy works. 2. Some time is made especially fit by God's institution; as the Lord's day above all other days. 3. Some time is made fit by governors' appointment: as the hour of public meeting for God's worship; and lecture days; and the hour for family worship, which every master of a family may appoint to his own household. 4. Some time is made fit by the constitution of men's bodies: the morning hours are the best for most, and for some rather the evening; and for all, the time when the body is freest from pain and disabling weaknesses. 5. Some time is made fit by the course of our necessary, natural, or civil business; as the day is fitter than the sleeping time of the night, and as that hour is the fittest in which our other employments will least disturb us. 6. Some time is made fit by a special shower of mercy, public or private; as when we dwell in godly families, among the most exemplary, helpful company, under the most lively, excellent means, the most faithful pastors, the most profitable teachers, the best masters or parents, and with faithful friends. 7. Some time is made fit by particular acts of Providence: as a funeral sermon at the death of anyone near us; as the presence of some able minister or private Christian, whose company we cannot ordinarily have; or a special leisure, as the eunuch had to read the Scripture in his chariot (Acts 8). 8. And some time is made especially fit by the special workings of God's Spirit upon the heart; when he more than ordinarily enlightens, teaches, quickens, softens, humbles, comforts, stirs up, or confirms. As time in general, so especially these seasons, must be particularly used well for their respective works: we must take the wind and tide while we may have it, and be sure to strike while the iron is hot. 9. And some time is made fit by others' necessities, and the call of God: as it is the time to relieve the poor when they ask, or when they are most in want; or to help our neighbor, when it will do him most good; to visit the sick, the imprisoned, and afflicted, in the needful season (Matthew 25). Thus are the godly like trees planted by the riverside, which bring forth fruit in their season (Psalm 1:3). So to speak in season to the ignorant and ungodly for their conversion, or to the sorrowful for their consolation (Isaiah 50:4). 10. Our own necessity also makes our seasons: so the time of age and sickness is made by necessity the season of our special repentance and preparation for death and judgment. 11. The present time is commonly made our season, through the uncertainty of a fitter, or of any more. "Do not withhold good from those who need it when you have the ability to help. Do not say to your neighbor, 'Go! Return tomorrow and I will give it,' when you have it with you at the time" (Proverbs 3:27–28). "Give a portion to seven, and also to eight; for you do not know what evil shall be upon the earth" (Ecclesiastes 11:2). "Do not boast about tomorrow; for you do not know what a day may bring forth" (Proverbs 27:1). "So then, whenever we have an opportunity, let us do good to all people, and especially to those who belong to the family of faith" (Galatians 6:10). These are our special seasons.

*In the margin: What redeeming time presupposes.*

To redeem time presupposes, 1. That we know what we have to do with time, and on what we ought to lay it out; and of how great worth the things are for which we must redeem it. 2. That we highly value time with a view to this necessary work. 3. That we are conscious of the greatness of our sin and loss, in our negligent or willful losing of so much as we have done already. 4. That we know the particular season of each duty. 5. And that we value less all that which we must part with in our redeeming time than we do time itself, and its due ends: or else we will not make the bargain.

*In the margin: What it contains.*

And as these five things are presupposed, so these following are contained in our redeeming time. 1. To redeem time is to see that we cast none of it away in vain; but use every minute of it as a most precious thing, and spend it wholly in the way of duty. 2. That we are not only doing good, but doing the best and greatest good which we are able and have a call to do. 3. That we do not only the best things, but do them in the best manner and in the greatest measure, and do as much good as we possibly can. 4. That we watch for special opportunities. 5. That we take them at once when they come, and make good use of them when we take them. 6. That we part with all that is to be parted with, to save our time. 7. And that we plan ahead for the preventing of impediments, and the removal of our clogs, and the obtaining of all the helps to speed and success in duty. This is the true redeeming of our time.

*In the margin: To what uses time must be redeemed.*

The ends and uses which time must be redeemed for are these: 1. In general, and ultimately, it must be all for God. Though not all employed directly upon God, in meditating on him, or praying to him; yet all must be laid out for him, directly or indirectly: that is, either in serving him, or in preparing for his service; in mowing, or in sharpening; in traveling, or in stopping for refreshment to fit us for travel. And so our time of sleep, and eating, and needful recreation is laid out for God. 2. Time must be redeemed, especially for works of public benefit; for the church and state; for the souls of many; especially by magistrates and ministers, who have special charge and opportunity; who "must spend and be spent" for the people's sake, though rewarded with ingratitude and contempt (2 Corinthians 12:14–15). 3. For your own souls, and your everlasting life: for speedy conversion without delay, if you are yet unconverted; for the killing of every soul-endangering sin without delay; for the exercise and increase of young and unconfirmed grace, and the growth of knowledge; for the making sure of our calling and election; and for the storing up of provisions of faith, and hope, and love, and comfort against the hour of suffering and of death.

4. We must redeem time for the souls of every particular person that we have opportunity to do good to; especially for children, and servants, and others whom God has committed to our trust. 5. For the welfare of our own bodies, that they may be serviceable to our souls. 6. And lastly, for the bodily welfare of others. And this is the order in which those works lie, for which and in which our time must be redeemed.

*In the margin: From what and at what price it must be redeemed.*

The price that time must be redeemed with is, 1. Above all, by our utmost diligence: that we be always doing, and put forth all our strength, and run as for our lives; and whatever our hand shall find to do, that we do it with our might, remembering that there is no work, nor planning, nor knowledge, nor wisdom in the grave where we are going (Ecclesiastes 9:10). Our sluggish ease is an easy price to be parted with for precious time. To redeem it is not to call back time past; nor to stop time in its hasty passage; nor to obtain a long life on earth: but to save it, as it passes, from being devoured and lost by sluggishness and sin. 2. Time must be redeemed from the hands, and by the loss, of sinful pleasures, amusements, and revelings, and all that is of itself or by accident unlawful; from wantonness, and licentiousness, and empty pursuits. Both these are set together: "And do this because we know the time, that it is already the hour for us to awake from sleep, for our salvation is now nearer than when we became believers. The night has advanced toward dawn; the day is near. So then we must lay aside the works of darkness, and put on the weapons of light. Let us live decently as in the daytime, not in carousing and drunkenness, not in sexual immorality and sensuality, not in discord and jealousy. Instead, put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh to arouse its desires" (Romans 13:11–14). 3. Time must be redeemed from things indifferent and lawful at another time, when things necessary do require it. He who ought to save men's lives, or quench a fire in his house, or provide for his family, or do his master's work, will not be excused if he neglects it, by saying that he was busy with an indifferent or a lawful business. Natural rest and sleep must be parted with for time, when necessary things require it. Paul preached till midnight, being about to leave the next day (Acts 20:7). The lamenting church calling out for prayer says, "Get up! Cry out in the night when the night watches start! Pour out your heart like water before the face of the Lord!" (Lamentations 2:19). Cleanthes' lamp must be used by those whose sunlight must be otherwise employed. 4. Time must be redeemed from worldly business and advantage, when matters of greater weight and advantage do require it. Trades, and plow, and profit must stand aside when God calls us (by necessity or otherwise) to greater things. Martha should not so much as trouble herself in providing food for Christ and his followers to eat, when Christ is offering her food for her soul, and she should with Mary have been listening at his feet (Luke 10:42). Worldly people are thus called by him: "Hey, all who are thirsty, come to the water! ... Why pay money for something that will not nourish you? Why spend your hard-earned money on something that will not satisfy? Listen carefully to me and eat what is nourishing! Enjoy fine food" (Isaiah 55:1–3). 5. Time must be redeemed from smaller duties, which in their season must be done, as being no duties when they hinder greater duty which should then take precedence. It is a duty in its time and place to show respect to neighbors and superiors, and to those around us, and to look after our family affairs; but not when we should be at prayer, to God, or when a minister should be preaching, or at his necessary studies. Private prayer and meditation, and visiting the sick, are duties; but not when we should be at church, or engaged in any greater duty which they hinder.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). Every verse in quotation marks as NET was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Title:** in the divisions that follow this opening, Baxter heads each one "*Tit.* 1." through "*Tit.* 5." "Tit." is his abbreviation for "Title," meaning a heading or main division of the chapter. We keep the word *Title* in those headings.
- **Chapter heading:** "the former" means the duties treated in the earlier chapters. The bold sub-heading is Baxter's "Directions for redeeming or well improving Time"; "improve" in his day meant to make good use of, turn to profit (not to make better), and we render it "making good use of" here and "use well" / "make good use of" in the body.
- **Ephesians 5:15–16 (important):** The whole chapter is built on the King James phrase "redeeming the time," and Baxter's later sections explain what "redeeming" presupposes, contains, and costs ("the price that time must be redeemed with"). The NET reads "taking advantage of every opportunity," which keeps the sense of seizing time but drops the image of buying it back at a price. The King James also had "walk circumspectly, not as fools"; the NET has "consider carefully how you live—not as unwise." Baxter's "the great mark to distinguish the wise from fools" still fits. Flagged. Colossians 4:5 (cited only) is likewise "making the most of the opportunities" in the NET, where the King James had "redeeming the time."
- **Ecclesiastes 11:2 (important):** Baxter quotes the King James, "Give a portion to seven, and also to eight," as a call to give to others now while one can. The NET reads "Divide your merchandise among seven or even eight investments, for you do not know what calamity may happen on earth," which takes the verse as advice to spread one's business risk, not to give alms. Because the NET makes a different point from the one Baxter uses, we kept his (King James) wording, modernized, in his quotation marks, and give the NET here. Flagged.
- **Proverbs 3:27–28:** NET wording. The King James "from them to whom it is due" is "from those who need it" in the NET, and "when it is in the power of thine hand to do it" is "when you have the ability to help." Baxter's point (do good now, not later) is unchanged.
- **Proverbs 27:1; Galatians 6:10; Lamentations 2:19:** NET wording. In Galatians the King James "As we have therefore opportunity" is "whenever we have an opportunity." In Lamentations the King James "Arise" and "in the beginning of the watches" are "Get up!" and "when the night watches start!"; Baxter quotes only the first part of the verse.
- **2 Corinthians 12:14–15:** Baxter's quotation marks enclose "must spend and be spent"; the words "spend and be spent" are the NET wording too ("I will most gladly spend and be spent for your lives," 12:15), but "must" is Baxter's own adaptation (neither the King James nor the NET has it), so the quoted phrase is his paraphrase and is kept in his wording. He applies Paul's words to magistrates and ministers.
- **Romans 13:11–14:** NET wording. Several King James words that Baxter's surrounding sentence leans on change: "chambering and wantonness" is "sexual immorality and sensuality," "rioting" is "carousing," "strife and envying" is "discord and jealousy," "walk honestly" is "live decently," "the armour of light" is "the weapons of light," and "The night is far spent" is "The night has advanced toward dawn." Baxter's "Both these are set together" probably refers to his points 1 and 2 (diligence against sluggishness, and the giving up of sinful pleasures and wantonness); the word "wantonness" that links his sentence to the verse is gone from the NET. Flagged.
- **Isaiah 55:1–3:** Baxter cites verses 1–3 but quotes from verse 1 and verse 2 only, skipping the middle of verse 1 (marked "..."). We keep his reference, 55:1–3; the quoted words are all from verses 1–2. The NET differs from the King James in ways that touch his use: "your labour for that which satisfieth not" is "your hard-earned money on something that will not satisfy," so the word "labor" (which fits his point about worldly business) is lost; and "let your soul delight itself in fatness" is "Enjoy fine food." The King James "Ho" is "Hey." Flagged.
- **Ecclesiastes 9:10:** not in quotation marks in Baxter; it is his own sentence built on the verse, kept in his wording, modernized ("device" to "planning," "whither we go" to "where we are going"). The NET reads: "Whatever you find to do with your hands, do it with all your might, because there is neither work nor planning nor knowledge nor wisdom in the grave, the place where you will eventually go."
- **Psalm 1:3; Isaiah 50:4; Ecclesiastes 3:1:** Baxter's wording is his own allusion, kept. The NET has "a tree planted by flowing streams; it yields its fruit at the proper time" (Psalm 1:3), and for Isaiah 50:4, "The Sovereign Lord has given me the capacity to be his spokesman, so that I know how to help the weary." The King James of Isaiah 50:4, "that I should know how to speak a word in season to him that is weary," is what Baxter's "to speak in season ... to the sorrowful" draws on; the NET loses "in season." Flagged.
- **Acts 20:7:** the NET has Paul "extended his message until midnight" because "he intended to leave the next day." **Luke 10:42:** the NET reads "one thing is needed. Mary has chosen the best part." **Acts 8** (the Ethiopian eunuch, Acts 8:26–39) and **Matthew 25** (cited only).
- **Source oddity, "Acts viii. 8.":** the source prints "his chariot, Acts viii. 8. And some time is made specially fit...". We read "Acts viii." as the reference and "8." as the number of Baxter's eighth point (the list runs 7, 8, 9). The eunuch's reading is in Acts 8:28, not 8:8.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "making sure of our calling and election" (2 Peter 1:10). We added "of" ("making sure of," "storing up of") for modern grammar.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): must needs be, must necessarily be; of most high importance, of the highest importance; opened, explained; acceptation, sense; specially, especially; temper (of bodies), constitution; illuminateth, enlightens; exciteth, stirs up; improved, used well; several (works), respective; supposeth, presupposes; in order to, with a view to; sensible of, conscious of; set less by, value less; presently, at once; fall (of opportunities), come; forecast, plan ahead for; expedition, speed; immediately or mediately, directly or indirectly; whetting, sharpening; baiting (stopping at an inn on a journey to rest and feed), stopping for refreshment; feeding, eating; still doing, always doing; procure, obtain; sports, amusements; vanity, empty pursuits; he that should, he who ought to; was about, was busy with; being to depart on the morrow, being about to leave the next day; commodity (both times), advantage; stand by, stand aside; meat, food; hearing at his feet, listening at his feet; worldlings, worldly people; take place, take precedence; about us, around us; look to, look after; about (any greater duty), engaged in.
- **quickens:** kept; it means "makes alive, enlivens," as in earlier sections.
- **clogs:** kept. A clog was a weight fastened to the leg of a person or animal to hinder movement; Baxter means hindrances.
- **wantonness, licentiousness:** kept. "Wantonness" in Baxter's day meant lewdness or unrestrained self-indulgence.
- **indifferent:** kept, in the sense of morally neutral (neither commanded nor forbidden).
- **lecture days:** kept. Probably weekday sermons or "lectures" given in addition to Sunday worship; we have not checked Baxter's own usage elsewhere.
- **governors:** kept; those in authority, civil or church.
- **"at prayer, to God,":** the comma is Baxter's and is kept.
- **Cleanthes' lamp:** probably the Stoic philosopher Cleanthes, whom ancient anecdote remembers for laboring by night and studying by lamplight; we have not confirmed which story Baxter had in mind. His point is that those whose daylight hours are taken up elsewhere must use the night.
- **"wind and tide," "strike while the iron is hot":** proverbs, kept.
- **Note 280:** "Part 2" is Baxter's "part ii." of the *Christian Directory*. We have not checked the chapter he points to.
