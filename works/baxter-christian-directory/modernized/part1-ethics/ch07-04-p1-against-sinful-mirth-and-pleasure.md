# Chapter 7: Title 4, against sinful mirth and pleasure (Directions 1-12)

**Title 4. Directions against sinful mirth and pleasure.**

Mirth is sinful, 1. When men rejoice in that which is evil; as in the hurt of others, or in men's sin, or in the sufferings of God's servants, or the afflictions of the church, or the success or prosperity of the enemies of Christ, or of any evil cause: this is one of the greatest sins in the world, and one of the greatest signs of wickedness, when it is wickedness that they rejoice in. [331] 2. When it is unseasonable, or in an unfit subject: as to be merry in the time and place of mourning; to feast when we should fast; or for an unsanctified, miserable soul to be taken up with mirth, that is in the power of sin and Satan and near to hell. 3. Mirth is sinful when it tends to the committing of sin, or is managed by sin: as to make merry with lies and fables, and tempting, unnecessary, time-wasting dances, plays, or recreations; or with the slander or abuse of others; or with drunkenness, gluttony, or excess. 4. Mirth is sinful when it is a hindrance to our duty and unfits the soul for the exercise of that grace which is most suitable to its condition: as when it hinders a sinner's conviction and humiliation, and resists the Spirit of God, and shouts down the calls of grace, and the voice of conscience, that they cannot be heard: and when it banishes all sober consideration about the matters that we should most regard, and will not give men leave to think with fixedness and sobriety, upon God and upon themselves, their sin and danger, upon death and judgment and the life to come: when it makes the soul more unfit to take reproof, to profit by a sermon, to call upon God. This drunken mirth which shuts out reason, and silences conscience, and laughs at God, and jests at damnation, and does nothing but intoxicate the brain, and make men mad in the matters where they should most show their wisdom, I say, this mirth is the devil's sport, and the sinner's misery, and the wise man's pity: of which Solomon speaks, "I said of partying, 'It is folly,' and of self-indulgent pleasure, 'It accomplishes nothing!'" (Ecclesiastes 2:2). "Like a madman who shoots firebrands and deadly arrows, so is a person who has deceived his neighbor, and said, 'Was I not only joking?'" (Proverbs 26:18–19). "Carrying out a wicked scheme is enjoyable to a fool" (Proverbs 10:23). 5. But mirth is most horribly odious when it is blasphemous and profane: when incarnate devils make themselves merry with jesting and mocking at Scripture, or at the judgments of God, or the duties of religion; or in horrid oaths and cursed speeches against the servants of the Lord.

**Direction 1. First see that you are a person fit for mirth, [332] and that you are not a miserable slave of Satan, in an unregenerate, unholy, unjustified state!**

You would scarcely think the innocent games or amusements were becoming to a criminal that must die tomorrow. An unregenerate, unholy person is sure, whenever he dies such, to be damned; if he does not believe this, he must deny God or the gospel to be true. And he is not sure to live an hour. And he is sure that he shall die before long. And now, if you have not fooled away your reason, tell me whether your reason can justify the mirth of such a man? Do you ask, what harm is it to be merry? None at all for one that has cause to be merry, and rejoices in the Lord. But for a man to be merry in the way to hell, and that so near it; for a man to be merry before his soul is sanctified, and his sin is pardoned, or before he seeks it with all his heart, this is harm; if folly and unbelief, and contempt of God and his dreadful justice, are any harm. O listen to the calls of God; abhor your sins, and set your heart on heaven and holiness, and then God and conscience will allow you to be merry. Get a renewed heart and life, and get the pardon of your sins, and a title to heaven, and a readiness to die, and then there is reason and wisdom in your mirth. [333] Then your mirth will be honorable and justifiable; better than the lame man's that was healed (Acts 3:8), that went with Peter and John into the temple, "walking and leaping and praising God." But it is a most pitiful sight to see an ungodly, unregenerate sinner laugh, and sport, and play, and live merrily, as if he did not know what evil is near to him! It would draw tears from the eyes of a believer that knows him, and thinks where he is likely to dwell forever. I remember the credible narrative of one that lived not far from me, that in his profaneness used to wish that he might see the devil; who at last appeared to him in his terror; and sometimes he smiled on him; and the man used to say that he never seemed so ugly and terrible as when he smiled (and the man was frightened by it into a reformed life). So though a servant of the devil is never attractive, yet he never seems so ghastly as when he is most merry in his misery.

**Direction 2. Yet do not destroy nature by excessive heaviness, on the pretext that you have no right to be merry.**

For, 1. The very discovery of your misery puts you into the fairer hopes of mercy. 2. And many of God's children live long without assurance of their justification, and yet should not therefore cast away all joy. 3. And so much ease and quiet of mind must be kept up by the unsanctified themselves, as is necessary to preserve their natures, that they may have time continued, and may wait on God till they obtain his grace. Above all men, they have reason to value their lives, lest they die and are lost before they are recovered. And therefore, as they must not starve themselves by going without food or drink, so their sorrows must not be such as may destroy their bodies (of which more later).

*In the margin: The true method of rejoicing.*

**Direction 3. See that you first settle the peace of your souls upon solid grounds, and get such evidences of your special interest in Christ and heaven as will rationally warrant you to rejoice; and then make it the business of your lives to rejoice and delight yourselves in God, and take this as the principal part of grace and godliness, and not as a small or indifferent thing; and so let all lawful, natural mirth be taken in, as animated and sanctified by this holy delight and joy; and know that this natural, sanctified mirth is not only lawful, but a duty exceedingly fitting and becoming for a thankful believer in his way to everlasting joy.**

This is the true method of rejoicing. Though, as I said, so much quietness may be kept up by the unregenerate as is needful to keep up life and health, and the gospel where it comes is news of great joy to those that hear it; yet no man can live a truly comfortable, merry life but in this method; but all his mirth, besides that which either supports nature, or meets mercy in his returning to God, will be justly chargeable with madness; and makes him a more pitiful sight. [334]

The first thing therefore to be done is to lay the groundwork of true mirth. And this is done by sincere repenting, and turning to God by faith in Christ, and becoming new creatures, a sanctified, peculiar people, and being justified and adopted to be the children of God; and then by discerning (upon sober examination) the evidences and witness of all this in ourselves, that we may know that we have passed from death to life.

And though there are several degrees both of grace and of the discerning of it, some having but little holiness, and some but little discerning of it in themselves, yet the least may afford much comfort to the soul upon justifiable grounds, though not so much as the greater degrees of grace, and clearer discerning of it, may do.

The foundation being thus laid, it must be our next effort to build upon it a settled peace of conscience, and quietness of soul; for till we can attain to joy, it is a great mercy to have peace, and to be free from the accusations, fears, and griefs which belong to the unjustified; and peace must be the temper more ordinary than much joy, to be expected in this our frail condition.

Thirdly, Peace being thus settled, we must strive to rise up daily into joy, as our great duty and our great happiness on earth; it being frequently and earnestly commanded in the Scriptures that we "rejoice in the Lord always," and "shout for joy, all you who are morally upright" (Psalm 33:1; Philippians 3:1; 4:4; Deuteronomy 12:12, 18; 27:7). Thus he that "examine[s] his own work" may "take pride in himself" (Galatians 6:4), even in "the testimony of [his] conscience," of his own "pure motives and sincerity which are from God" (2 Corinthians 1:12). And this all believers should maintain and exercise in themselves.

Fourthly, With this rejoicing in God, our lawful, natural mirth must be taken in, as subordinate or sanctified; that is, we must further our holy joy by natural mirth and cheerfulness, and by the comforts of our bodies in God's lower mercies, promote the service and the comforts of our souls. And this is the right place for this mirth to come in, and this is the true method of rejoicing.

**Direction 4. Note well the usefulness and tendency of all your mirth: and if it is useful to fit you for your duty, and intended by you to that end (though you do not always observe that intention at the time), and if it tends to do you good, or help you to do good, without a greater hurt or danger, then cherish and promote it; but if it tends to carry you away from God to any creature, and to unfit your soul for the duties of your place, and to carry you into sin, then avoid it as your hurt: always remembering that the necessary support of nature must not be avoided by good or bad.**

A Christian that has any acquaintance with himself, and with the work of holy watchfulness, may discern what his mirth is by the tendency and effects, and know whether it does him good or harm.

**Direction 5. Be careful that the flesh does not defile your mirth, by dropping in any obscene or ribald talk, or by stirring up fleshly lust and sin.**

Which it will quickly do, if not well watched; and holy mirth and cheerfulness is very apt to degenerate suddenly into sinful mirth.

**Direction 6. Consider what your mirth is likely to prove to others as well as to yourselves.**

If it is likely to stir up sin in others, or to be offensive to them, you must the more avoid it in their presence, or manage it with the greater caution: if it is needful to cheer up the drooping minds of those you associate with, or to remove their prejudice against a holy life, you must the more make room for it: for it is good or bad as it tends to good or bad.

**Direction 7. Never leave out reason or godliness from any of your mirth.**

Abhor that mirth that makes a man a fool, or plays the fool: and beware of that ungodliness which makes a man merriest when he is furthest from God, like the horse or ox that leaps and plays for gladness when he is unyoked or loosed from his labor. Something of God and heaven should appear or be dropped into all our mirth, to sweeten and to sanctify it.

**Direction 8. Watch your tongues in all your mirth; for they are very apt to take liberty then to sin.**

Mirth is to the tongue as holidays and play days to idle schoolboys; who are glad of them as a time in which they think they have liberty to play games, and fight, and do wrong.

**Direction 9. If a word breaks forth from yourselves or companions to the wrong of others in your mirth, as of backbiting, evil-speaking, jeering, scorning, defaming (yes, though it is your enemy), rebuke it, and cast it out, as dirt or dung that falls into your dish or cup.**

**Direction 10. If profaneness intrudes, and any make merry with jesting at Scripture, religion, or the slanders or scorns of godly persons, with a tendency to make religion odious or contemptible; if they are such as you may speak to, reprove them with reverent seriousness to their terror: if they are not, then show your abhorrence of it by turning your backs and leaving the place and company of such devilish enemies of God.**

Do not be silent or seemingly-consenting witnesses of such odious mirth against your Maker.

**Direction 11. If the mirth of others in your company grows insipid, frothy, foolish, wanton, impious, or otherwise corrupt, drop in some holy salt to season it; and something that is serious and divine to awe it and repress it.**

As to remind them of God's presence, or to recite such a text as Ephesians 5:3–4, "But among you there must not be either sexual immorality, impurity of any kind, or greed, as these are not fitting for the saints. Neither should there be vulgar speech, foolish talk, or coarse jesting—all of which are out of character—but rather thanksgiving."

*In the margin: Considerations to repress excessive mirth.*

**Direction 12. If mirth grows immoderate and exceeds in measure, and carries you away from God and duty by the very carnal pleasure of it, always have at hand these following considerations to repress it.**

1. Remember that God is present; and levity is not becoming in his sight. 2. Remember that death and judgment are at hand, when all this levity will be turned into seriousness. 3. Remember that your souls are yet under a great deal of sin, and needs, and danger, and you have a great deal of serious work to do. 4. Look on Jesus Christ, and remember what an example he gave you upon earth; whether he laughed, and played, and jested, and taught you immoderate or carnal mirth; and whether you live like the disciples of a crucified Christ. 5. Think on the ordinary way to heaven, described in Scripture; which is through many tribulations, afflictions, fastings, temptations, humiliations, sufferings, and mortifications; and think whether a wanton, jesting, playful life is like this. 6. Think of the course of the ancient and excellent Christians, who went to heaven through labor, and watchings, and fasting, and poverty, and cruel persecutions, and not through carnal mirth and sport. 7. Think of the many calamitous objects of sorrow that are now abroad in the world! of the millions of heathens and Muslims, and other strangers or enemies to Christ! of the obstinate Jews; of the dark, corrupted, lamentable state of the Greek, Armenian, Ethiopian and Roman churches, where religion is so woefully obscured and dishonored by ignorance, error, superstition, and profaneness: of the papal tyranny and usurpation; and of the divided state of all the churches, and the profaneness, and persecution, and uncharitableness, and contentions, and mutual reproaches and revilings, which make havoc for the devil among the members of Christ.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[331] Stoici dicunt severos esse sapientes, quod neque ipsi loquantur ad voluptatem, neque ab aliis ad voluptatem dicta admittant. Esse autem et alios severos, qui ad rationem acris vini severi dicantur; quo ad medicamenta, potius quam ad propinationem, utuntur. Laert. in Zenone. (The Stoics say that the wise are severe, because they neither speak for pleasure themselves nor admit things said by others for pleasure. But that there are also others called severe in the way a harsh wine is called severe, which is used for medicines rather than for drinking. Laertius, in his life of Zeno. Rendered loosely in the second sentence.)

[332] Proverbs 19:10, "Luxury is not appropriate for a fool."

[333] Siquis est quem flentem mori deceat, riderededecuit viventem; cum instare, semperque supra verticem videret, unde mors flendum sciret. Risum illum haud dubie fletus hic non longo sejunctus spatio sequebatur. Petrarch. dial. 119. li. 2. (If there is anyone whom it befits to die weeping, it was unfitting for him to laugh while living, since he saw standing near, and always over his head, that from which he knew death was to be wept for. Without doubt this weeping followed that laughter after no long interval. Petrarch, dialogue 119, book 2. Rendered loosely; "riderededecuit" appears to be a misprint, probably for "ridere dedecuit," "it was unfitting to laugh," and the "cum ... sciret" clause is obscure as printed.)

[334] See my sermon at Paul's called "Right Rejoicing." And earlier, chapter 3, direction 13.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **Title:** "Tit." is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," that is, a heading or division. Chapter 7 ("Directions for the Government of the Passions") is divided into ten Titles; this is Title 4. "Passions" is Baxter's word for the emotions and affections.
- **Range:** this file covers the whole of Title 4, from its opening through Direction 12. Direction 12's list ends with item 7, and Title 4 ends there; Title 5 (against sinful hopes) begins on the next line of the source.
- **Ecclesiastes 2:2 (important):** Baxter quotes the King James, "I said of laughter, It is mad: and of mirth, What doth it?" His whole subject is mirth, and he brings in the verse as Solomon's verdict on "laughter" and "mirth." The NET reads "I said of partying, 'It is folly,' and of self-indulgent pleasure, 'It accomplishes nothing!'" This narrows the verse to partying and self-indulgence, which still fits his point about drunken mirth, but the verse no longer names laughter or mirth themselves. Flagged.
- **Proverbs 10:23 (important):** Baxter's King James, "It is as sport to a fool to do mischief," uses "sport" (fun, mirth), which is why he cites it here. The NET reads "Carrying out a wicked scheme is enjoyable to a fool"; the word "sport" is gone, though the sense (a fool enjoys doing wrong) remains. We quote only the first half of the NET verse, as Baxter quotes only the first half. Flagged.
- **Proverbs 26:18–19:** NET wording. Baxter's "Am not I in sport?" is "Was I not only joking?" The point about mirth survives.
- **Psalm 33:1 (wording from Psalm 32:11):** Baxter's quoted words "shout for joy, all that are upright in heart" are the King James of Psalm 32:11, the verse just before the one he cites; King James Psalm 33:1 reads "Rejoice in the Lord, O ye righteous." We kept Baxter's list of references and quoted the NET of Psalm 32:11, "Shout for joy, all you who are morally upright!" (exclamation mark dropped in his sentence). We are not sure whether Baxter meant to cite 32:11 or simply grouped the two adjacent verses. The NET of Psalm 33:1 is "You godly ones, shout for joy because of the Lord! It is appropriate for the morally upright to offer him praise."
- **"Rejoice in the Lord always":** NET of Philippians 4:4 (same words as the King James here). Philippians 3:1 in the NET is "rejoice in the Lord!" Deuteronomy 12:12, 18; 27:7 are cited only.
- **Galatians 6:4 (important):** Baxter's King James reads "let every man prove his own work, and then shall he have rejoicing in himself." He cites it because of the word "rejoicing," in a paragraph about the duty of joy. The NET reads "Let each one examine his own work. Then he can take pride in himself." "Take pride" changes the sense he leans on: he means a believer's rightful gladness in the evidence of his own sincerity, not pride. Flagged. The brackets in "examine[s]" mark the change from the NET's "examine" to fit Baxter's sentence.
- **2 Corinthians 1:12:** Baxter adapts Paul's first person to the third person ("his conscience," "his own"). The NET has "The testimony of our conscience, that with pure motives and sincerity which are from God." The bracketed "[his]" replaces the NET's "our." Baxter's King James "simplicity and godly sincerity" is in the NET "pure motives and sincerity which are from God."
- **Acts 3:8:** NET wording ("walking and leaping and praising God"); Baxter placed the reference before the quotation.
- **Ephesians 5:3–4:** NET wording. The King James "fornication," "uncleanness," "covetousness," "filthiness," "jesting; which are not convenient" are "sexual immorality," "impurity," "greed," "vulgar speech," "coarse jesting ... out of character." Baxter's point (jesting is out of place for saints) survives.
- **Proverbs 19:10** (note 332): Baxter's King James "Delight is not seemly for a fool" is the NET "Luxury is not appropriate for a fool." Baxter cites it for "delight" (joy, mirth) in the sense of his Direction 1; the NET's "luxury" turns it toward comfort and wealth. Flagged. We quote only the first half, as Baxter does.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "tidings of great joy," here "news of great joy" (Luke 2:10); "new creatures" (2 Corinthians 5:17); "peculiar people" (1 Peter 2:9; Titus 2:14, King James); "passed from death to life" (1 John 3:14; John 5:24); "holy salt to season it" (Colossians 4:6); "through many tribulations" (Acts 14:22).
- **Grammar:** "when wickedness is it that they rejoice in" becomes "when it is wickedness that they rejoice in"; "doth but intoxicate the brain, and make men mad" becomes "does nothing but intoxicate the brain, and make men mad."
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): unmeet, unfit; estate (of the soul), condition; bawleth down, shouts down; horridly, horribly; sports, amusements; malefactor, criminal; ere long, before long; hearken, listen; warrantable, justifiable; was wont, used; affrighted, frightened; comely (of the devil's servant), attractive; comely (of mirth and levity), becoming; overmuch, excessive; under pretence, on the pretext; famish themselves by forbearing meat, starve themselves by going without food; anon, later; exceeding congruous and comely, exceedingly fitting and becoming; tidings, news; unfeigned, sincere; trial, examination; endeavour (noun), effort; endeavour (verb), strive; felicity, happiness; actuate, exercise; mark well, note well; alway, always; still remembering, always remembering; take heed, be careful / beware; on a sudden, suddenly; converse with, associate with; give place to, make room for; scholars, schoolboys; game, play games (Baxter's "game" may mean gamble; we chose the broader sense); amiss, wrong; quitting, leaving; reverend, reverent (Baxter's spelling for the sense "reverent"); remember them of, remind them of; wants, needs; Mahometans, Muslims (Baxter's word for Muslims, now dated); labour, labor.
- **"will not give men leave to think":** kept; "leave" means permission.
- **conviction and humiliation** (item 4 of the opening): Baxter's terms for a sinner being convinced of sin and humbled by it, the first steps toward conversion. Kept.
- **nature** (Directions 2 and 4): bodily life and health, "the necessary support of nature." Kept.
- **special interest in Christ** (Direction 3): "interest" means a share or claim in Christ, not curiosity. Kept. **indifferent:** morally neutral. Kept.
- **peculiar people:** God's own special people (King James phrase), not "odd." Kept.
- **temper** ("peace must be the temper more ordinary"): settled state of mind. Kept.
- **creature** (Direction 4): any created thing, as opposed to God. Kept.
- **"in his terror"** (Direction 1): kept as printed; it most likely means the devil appeared to the man's terror, or in a terrifying form. The story is Baxter's own report, which we keep as his and do not vouch for.
- **wanton:** undisciplined, frivolous, unruly (not only lewd). Kept. **frothy:** trivial, empty. Kept. **levity:** lightness, frivolity. Kept.
- **"the Greek, Armenian, Ethiopian and Roman churches ... the papal tyranny and usurpation"** (Direction 12, item 7): Baxter's own judgment and polemic against the Roman Church and others, kept exactly as he wrote it. "Heathens," "Mahometans," and "the obstinate Jews" are likewise his words and views, rendered faithfully.
- **Note 331:** "Laert. in Zenone" is probably Diogenes Laertius's life of Zeno the Stoic in his *Lives of the Philosophers*; we have not confirmed the passage.
- **Note 333:** "Petrarch. dial. 119. li. 2" is probably a dialogue in book 2 of Petrarch's *De remediis utriusque fortunae* (*Remedies for Fortune Fair and Foul*); we have not confirmed it.
- **Note 334:** "my Sermon at Paul's called 'Right Rejoicing'": presumably a sermon Baxter preached at St Paul's in London and published under that title; we have not confirmed the details. The cross-reference "chap. iii. dir. xiii." (chapter 3, direction 13) has not been checked.
- **Footnote markers:** Baxter's markers [331]–[334] fall where shown; markers are written with a space before the bracket.
