# Chapter 4: Directions 15-17 against Pride (the body, the gifts, and the graces)

**Direction 15. Look well upon yourself, both body and soul, and think whether you are a person fit for pride.**

God has purposely clothed your immortal soul in the coarse attire of corruptible flesh, and placed it in so poor and ruinous a cottage, that it might be kept from pride: yes, he made this frail and corruptible body to be a constitutive part of our very person, that in knowing it, we may know ourselves. Some will have a dead man's skull stand by them, in their studies or chambers, as an antidote against pride. But God has fastened us yet closer to mortality: death dwells in our bowels. We are apt to marvel that so noble a soul should be lodged in so mean a body, made of the earth to which it must return! [236] A stone is durable and clean; but my flesh is corruptible, and must turn to loathsome filth and rottenness. A marble pillar will stand firm and beautiful from age to age, but I must perish and consume in darkness. The seats we sit upon, the pillars we lean to, the stones we tread upon, will be here, when we are turned to dust. The house that I build, may stand when I am rotten in the grave. A tree will live, when he that planted it is dead. Our bodies are of no better materials than the brutes; our substance is in a continual flux or waste, and loses something every day; and if it were not repaired and patched up by daily air and nourishment, it would soon be spent, and our oil consumed. If you were chained to a dead carcass, which you must still carry about with you, it were not a matter so fit to humble you, as to be united so nearly to so vile a body of your own. We carry a dunghill continually within us. Alas! how pitiful a creature is the greatest, the strongest, and the most attractive of you all! What is that flesh which you so much pamper, but a skin full of corruption? a bag of filth, of phlegm, or choler, or such like excrements? If the most fastidious ladies had but a sight of the phlegm in their heads and bowels, the choler about their liver and gall bladders, the worms or filth in other parts, they would come near to vomiting at such a sight: the swine or beast has as clean an inside. And what if this filth be covered with a whiter skin or clearer color than your neighbors have, is there any cause of pride in that? When sickness has altered and consumed you, then where is that which you call beauty? If but the leprosy or the smallpox deform it, or a fever, consumption, or dropsy waste it, or the stone, or gout, or any such torment seize upon you, you will feel or see that which may shame your pride. Should such a worm be proud, that cannot, though he be a Herod, keep the worms from eating him alive? that in a flux cannot retain his excrements? that cannot bear easily the aching of a tooth? If you are fit for pride, forbid diseases to touch your flesh, or stain your beauty; do not be sick, nor weak, nor pained; let not the worm and corruption be your guests. Or if you are so poor a thing as cannot hinder any of these, then know yourself, and be ashamed of pride.

And when you are in sickness, you will be burdensome to others. It is likely you must have their help, even to feed you, to dress you, to turn you, and keep you clean; and when all is done, you must die, and be laid in darkness in a grave! There must you lie rotting night and day, until your flesh is turned into earth. The grass does wither when it is cut down, but yet it is sweet; the tree that is cut down will rot in time, but not with such a loathsome stink as we. He that had seen what the late doleful wars often showed us, when the fields were strewn with the carcasses of men, and when they lay by heaps among the rubbish of the ditches of towns and castles that had been assaulted, would think such loathsome lumps of flesh should never have been proud. When once death has deprived your body of its soul, your best friends will quickly be weary of the remainder, and glad to rid you out of sight and smell. Go to the churchyard, and look on the dust and bones that are there cast up and scattered, and consider whether those that must come to this have reason to be proud? See whether there be any differing mark of honor upon the dust of the rich, or strong, or beautiful? and whether the bones there strive for principality and dominion? Therefore the desire of adorned monuments upon men's graves, is one of the most odious sorts of pride; when the neighborhood of rottenness and dust does shame it. As our serious poet Herbert says,

      When the hair grows sweet with pride and lust,
      The powder does forget the dust.

And though your soul is far nobler than your body, yet here how ignorant, and weak, and disordered is it! How full of false ideas are men's minds! How little know they of that which they might know, or are confident they do know! How dark are we about all the works of God, and about his word; much more about himself! The greatest doctors are strongly tempted to be skeptics; and the ignorant that this year are confident to a contempt and censoriousness of all that differ from them, perhaps the next year do change their judgments, and recant themselves.

And are our hearts and lives any happier than our understandings; while we are imprisoned in flesh, and its interest is ours, and its appetites and passions have so much advantage, to corrupt, seduce, or disturb the soul? Know yourself, and pride will die.

**Direction 16. If you have anything to be proud of, remember what it is, and that it is not your own, but given or lent you by that God who chiefly hates pride.**

1. Are you tempted to be proud of riches? Remember that they are in themselves but dross, which will leave you at the grave as poor as any. And as to their usefulness, they are but your Master's talents; and the more you have, the greater will be your account. And very few rich men escape the snare, and come to heaven. Your charge and danger therefore should rather humble you, and make you exceedingly to fear. Read James 5:1-4; and Luke 12:19-20.

2. Is it greatness, and dominion, or human applause, or honor, that you are proud of? Remember that this also is in itself a dream, that makes you really neither better nor safer than other men. You stand upon higher ground, where you have more than others of the storms and dangers, and shall be leveled with the lowest in your fall. And as to the use of your power and greatness, it is for God, and not yourself. And so great will be your reckoning, according to the trust reposed in you, as would frighten a considerate believer to foresee.

3. Is it youthful strength that you are proud of? How little can it do for you, of that which you most need! And how soon will it be turned to weakness! How many are cut off in youth, and their life is among the unclean, as Elihu speaks, Job 36:14. Their bones are full of the sins of their youth, which shall lie down with them in the dust, Job 20:11.

4. Is it beauty that you are proud of? I have told you what sickness and death will do to that before. When God rebukes man for sin, he makes his beauty to consume as a moth: surely every man is vanity, Psalm 39:11. Read Psalm 49:12-14. And if your beauty would continue, how little good will it do you! and who but fools do look at the skin of a rational creature, when they would discern their worth? A fool, and a slave of lust and Satan, may be beautiful. A sepulchre may be gilded that has rottenness within. Will you choose the finest purse, or the fullest? Who but a child or fool will value his book by the fineness of the cover, or gilding of the leaves, and not by the worth of the matter within? Absalom was beautiful, and what the better was he? 2 Samuel 14:25. Favor is deceitful, and beauty is vain; but a woman that fears the Lord, she shall be praised, Proverbs 31:30.

5. If it is fine clothes and gaudy ornaments that you are proud of, it is a sin so foolish, and worse than childish, that I shall give it no other refutation, than to tell you, that it contradicts itself, by making the person a scorn and laughing-stock to others, when their design was to be more admired; and that an ass or a post may have as fine and costly attire as you; and that shortly you shall change it for a winding-sheet. [237]

6. Is it your birth, and progenitors, and great friends that you are proud of? Personal merits are incomparably more excellent than this relation to the most meritorious parents; much more than a relation to their empty titles. Cain was the son of Adam the father of mankind, and Ham of Noah, and Esau of Isaac, and Absalom of David; when a godly son of a wicked father is more honorable than they. Your ancestors are but of the common stock of sinful Adam: and your great friends may possibly become your enemies: and it is little that the greatest of them can do for you, if God is not your friend.

7. Is it your learning, or wisdom, or ability for speech or action, that you are proud of? Remember that the devils, and many that are now in hell, have far exceeded you in these; and that the wiser you are indeed, the humbler you will be; and by pride you refute your ostentation of your wisdom. Ahithophel's wisdom, which does not save the owner from perdition, is little cause of glorying. Jeremiah 8:8-9, there were men that boasted of their wisdom, even in the law of God, who yet were ashamed and dismayed; for they rejected the word of the Lord: and then what wisdom could there be in them? Therefore, "The Lord says, Wise people should not boast that they are wise. Powerful people should not boast that they are powerful. Rich people should not boast that they are rich. If people want to boast, they should boast about this: They should boast that they understand and know me. They should boast that they know and understand that I, the Lord, act out of faithfulness, fairness, and justice in the earth and that I desire people to do these things," says the Lord (Jeremiah 9:23-24). Those were not unlearned, of whom Paul speaks; "Where is the wise man? Where is the expert in the Mosaic law? Where is the debater of this age? Has God not made the wisdom of the world foolish?" (1 Corinthians 1:20).

8. Is it success in wars, or great undertakings, that you are proud of? But by whose strength did you perform it? and how unhappy a success is that which hinders your success in the work of your salvation! and how many have been brought down again to shame, that have been lifted up in pride of their successes!

9. Is it the applause of men that proclaim your excellence that you are proud of? Alas, how poor a portion is the breath of man! and how changeable are your applauders! that perhaps the next day will turn their tunes, and as much reproach you. Will you be proud of praise, when it is the devil's whistle, purposely to entice you into this pernicious snare, that he may destroy you? It is a danger to be feared; for it destroys many: but not a benefit much to be rejoiced in, much less to be proud of; for few are the better for it. Titles and applause increase not real worth and virtue, but puff up many with a mortal tympany.

10. Is it your grace and goodness, or eminence in religion, that you are proud of? This is most absurd; when predominant pride is a certain sign that you have no saving grace at all, and so are proud of what you have not: and if you have it, so far as you are proud of it you abuse it, contradict it, and destroy it; for pride is to grace, what the plague or consumption is to health. It is novices that have least grace and knowledge, that are aptest to be puffed up with pride, and thereby to fall into the condemnation of the devil, 1 Timothy 3:6; that is, into the like punishment for the like sin. When the pot boils over, that which was in it is lost in the fire. Rise not too high in the esteem of your grace, lest you rise to the loss of it. "Do not be arrogant, but fear!" (Romans 11:20). When you "think you stand, take heed lest you fall," 1 Corinthians 10:12.

**Direction 17. Look to the nature and tendency of every grace and ordinance and duty, and use them diligently; for they all tend to the destruction of pride.**

Knowledge discerns the folly and pernicious tendency of pride, and abundant matter for humiliation. Faith is the casting off our pride, and going with empty, hungry souls to Christ for mercy and supply. It shows us the most powerful sight in the world for the humbling of a soul, even a crucified Christ, and a most holy God, and a glorified society of humble souls, and a dreadful judgment and damnation for the proud. I might show you the same of every grace and duty, but for being tedious.

## Baxter's notes

[236]

      Fama est fictilibus cœnasse Agathoclea regem,
        Atque abacum Samio sæpe onerasse luto.
      Fercula gemmatis cum poneret aurea vasis,
        Et misceret opes pauperiemque simul,

      Querenti causam respondit: Rex ego qui sum
        Sicaniæ, figulo sum genitore satus.
      Fortunam reverentur habe, quicunque repente
        Dives ab exili progredire loco.
                                 Auson. li. Epigram.

(English, draft: The story is that King Agathocles dined from earthenware, and often loaded his sideboard with Samian clay. When he set out golden dishes with jeweled vessels, and mixed wealth and poverty together, he answered one who asked the reason: "I who am king of Sicily am the son of a potter. Hold fortune in reverence, whoever you are who advance suddenly rich from a humble place." Ausonius, book of Epigrams.)

[237] Isaiah 3.

## Translator's notes

- **Latin (note 236):** the English is a draft prepared for this project and needs a Latinist's check. "Auson. li. Epigram." is Baxter's abbreviation, which we read as Ausonius, book of Epigrams; we have not confirmed the exact epigram number. The printed "reverentur" (line 7) looks like a misprint (perhaps for "reverenter," "reverently"); we kept it as printed and rendered the sense loosely. Baxter's note carries no comment of its own; why it is attached at "must return!" is our guess: a man of lowly birth reminded of his origin.
- **Isaiah 3 (note 237):** a bare reference in Baxter, kept as printed. We have not checked which verses he means (the chapter speaks of ornaments and finery, but that is our reading, not Baxter's statement).
- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible. Quotation marks are used only where Baxter used them. Where Baxter quotes without marks (Job, Psalms, Proverbs, 1 Timothy, Jeremiah 8), his King James-based wording is kept, modernized, and the NET is given below.
- **Jeremiah 9:23-24:** NET wording, with line breaks run together. The NET starts "The Lord says," where Baxter has "thus saith the Lord," and turns his third-person "the wise man ... the mighty man ... the rich man" into plural "wise people ... powerful people ... rich people." It also has "act out of faithfulness, fairness, and justice" where the King James has "loving-kindness, judgment, and righteousness," and adds "and that I desire people to do these things" for "for in these do I delight." Baxter's argument (do not glory in wisdom, might, or riches) is unchanged.
- **1 Corinthians 1:20:** NET wording. Baxter's "the scribe" is the NET's "the expert in the Mosaic law," and his "disputer of this world" is "the debater of this age." Baxter's point (learned men are not saved by their learning) survives.
- **Romans 11:20:** NET "Do not be arrogant, but fear!" for Baxter's "Be not highminded, but fear." Same sense.
- **1 Corinthians 10:12:** Baxter's "think you stand, take heed lest you fall" is an adaptation of the verse into the second person. The NET reads "So let the one who thinks he is standing be careful that he does not fall." We kept Baxter's wording in his quotation marks.
- **Job 36:14:** the NET reads "They die in their youth, and their life ends among the male cultic prostitutes." Baxter's "their life is among the unclean" follows the King James. Baxter's "as Elihu speaks" is correct for this part of Job (Elihu speaks in chapters 32-37). Kept as Baxter wrote it; the NET differs in sense.
- **Job 20:11:** the NET reads "His bones were full of his youthful vigor, but that vigor will lie down with him in the dust." Baxter's "full of the sins of their youth" follows an older rendering (probably the King James; we have not checked the exact wording). The NET has no "sins" here, so Baxter's moral point rests on the older rendering. Flagged.
- **Psalm 39:11:** the NET reads "You severely discipline people for their sins; like a moth you slowly devour their strength. Surely all people are a mere vapor." Baxter's "makes his beauty to consume as a moth" has the object "beauty"; the NET has "strength." Flagged. "Vanity" is kept in the quotation because it is the King James word.
- **Psalm 49:12-14:** cited only. The NET (verses 12-14) speaks of people who "do not last" and "will travel to Sheol like sheep"; we have not quoted it.
- **Proverbs 31:30:** the NET reads "Charm is deceitful and beauty is fleeting. A woman who fears the Lord-she makes herself praiseworthy." Baxter's "beauty is vain" and "she shall be praised" follow the King James. The NET's "makes herself praiseworthy" changes the sense Baxter leans on (she shall be praised by others). Flagged. "Vain" is kept here as part of the quotation.
- **1 Timothy 3:6:** the NET reads "He must not be a recent convert, or he may become arrogant and fall into the punishment that the devil will exact." Baxter's "condemnation of the devil" is the King James, and he explains it himself: "the like punishment for the like sin." The NET's reading is of the devil as the one who exacts punishment, which is not the sense Baxter gives. Flagged.
- **Jeremiah 8:8-9:** Baxter's description of men "that boasted of their wisdom, even in the law of God" is his paraphrase, not a quotation. The NET reads "How can you say, 'We are wise! We have the law of the Lord'? The truth is, those who teach it have used their writings to make it say what it does not really mean" and "Since they have rejected the Lord's message, what wisdom do they really have?" Baxter's "ashamed and dismayed" is in the NET as "put to shame ... dumbfounded."
- **James 5:1-4; Luke 12:19-20; 2 Samuel 14:25:** cited only.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "a skin full of corruption" and "worms" eating "a Herod" (compare Acts 12:23, an allusion we have not verified Baxter intends); "the grass does wither" (compare Isaiah 40:7-8 and similar passages); "a sepulchre may be gilded that has rottenness within" (compare Matthew 23:27); "the devil's whistle" is Baxter's own image; "your Master's talents" (Matthew 25:14-30).
- **Word swaps:** "silly piece" to "pitiful creature"; "comeliest" to "most attractive"; "curiousest dames" to "most fastidious ladies" (curious in the sense of particular or fussy); "galls" to "gall bladders"; "go near to vomit" to "come near to vomiting"; "bethink thee" to "consider"; "distempered" to "disordered"; "sceptics" to "skeptics"; "confute / confutation" to "refute / refutation"; "tympany" kept (a morbid swelling; Baxter's image of being puffed up); "winding-sheet" kept (burial shroud); "excellency" to "excellence"; "eminency" to "eminence"; "mutable" to "changeable"; "affright" to "frighten"; "reposed" kept.
- **Kept as Baxter wrote them:** "choler" (yellow bile, one of the four humors), "phlegm," "dropsy," "the stone" (kidney or bladder stone), "gout," "a flux" (a discharge, as in dysentery), "doctors" (learned teachers), "predominant," "ostentation," "novices," "progenitors," "doleful," "principality and dominion."
- **"the late doleful wars":** probably the English civil wars of the 1640s, but Baxter does not name them here; we have not confirmed this.
- **Herbert:** Baxter calls him "our serious poet Herbert." This is probably George Herbert; we have not confirmed the source of the lines. "Powder" means dust. We modernized "doth" to "does" in the verse; the rhyme and meter are otherwise as printed.
- **"Know yourself, and pride will die":** Baxter's modernized line keeps the plain command; no source for the saying is given in the text.
- **Direction 16:** Baxter's sub-points 1-10 are kept numbered as printed. The heading sentence of each Direction is set in bold as the other files do; the sentences that follow it in the same paragraph in the source (for instance Direction 16, point 1) are placed in the body.
- **Margin notes:** none in this range.
