# Chapter 4: Pride's hindrance of duty; Directions 4 to 6 for humility

*This part begins in the middle of Direction 3 (Baxter's signs or effects of pride), at his numbered point 4.*

4. Pride is a deep-rooted and a self-preserving sin; and therefore harder to be killed and rooted up than other sins. It hinders the discovery of itself. It drives away the light. It hates reproof. It will not give the sinner leave to see his pride when it is reproved; nor to confess it if he sees it; nor to be humbled for it if he does confess it; nor to loathe himself and forsake it, though conviction and terror seem to humble him. Even while he hears all the signs of pride, he will not see it in himself. When he feels his hatred of reproof, and knows that this is a sign of pride in others, yet he will not know it in himself. If you would go about to cure him of this or any other fault, you shall feel that you are handling a wasp or an adder; yet when he is spitting the venom of pride against the reprover, he does not perceive that he is proud; this venom is his nature, and therefore is not felt nor troublesome. If all the town or congregation should note him as notoriously proud, yet he himself, who should best know himself, will not observe it. It is a wonder to see how this sin keeps strength, in persons who have long taken pains for their souls, and seem to be in all other respects the most serious, mortified Christians! Yet, let them but be touched in their interest or reputation, or seem to be slighted, or see another preferred before them, while they are neglected, and they boil with envy, malice, or discontent, and show you that the heart of sin, even selfishness and pride, is still alive, unbroken, and too strong. Especially if they are not persons of a natural gentleness and mildness, but of a more passionate temper; then pride has more oil and fuel to kindle it into these discernible flames. He is a Christian indeed who has conquered pride.

5. Pride is the defense not only of itself, but of every other sin in the heart or life. For it hates reproof and keeps off the remedy; it hides, and minimizes, and excuses the sin, and thinks well of that which should be hated.

6. Pride hinders every means and duty from doing you good; and often corrupts them, and turns them into sin. Sometimes it keeps men from the duty, and sometimes it keeps them from the benefit of the duty. It makes men think that they are so whole and well as to have little need of all this medicine, yes, or of their daily necessary food. They think all this is more ado than is needed: what need of all this preaching, and praying, and reading, and holy conference, and meditation, and heavenly-mindedness? One is ashamed of it, and another does not need it, and another is above it, and they ask you, Where are we commanded to pray in our family, and to pray so often, and to hear so often, and read any book but the holy Scriptures, etc.; for they feel no obligation from general commands, (as to "constantly pray," and "always," and "not lose heart," nor be "weary in doing good," to "take advantage of every opportunity," and do all "for the strengthening of the church," and "be enthusiastic in spirit, serve the Lord," etc. 1 Thessalonians 5:17; Luke 18:1; Galatians 6:9; Ephesians 5:16; 1 Corinthians 14:26; Romans 12:11,) because they do not feel that need or sweetness which should help them to perceive that frequency is good or necessary for them. If the physician tells two men "eat often," and one of them has a strong appetite, and the other has none; he who is hungry will interpret the word "often" to signify three times a day, at least, and he who has no appetite will think that once a day is "often." Healthy men do not usually ask, How do you prove that I am bound to eat twice or three times a day? Feeling the need and benefit, they will be satisfied with an allowance without a command. They will rather ask, How do you prove that I may not do it? for they feel reason in themselves to move them to it, if God does not restrain them. So it is with a humble soul, about the means of his edification and salvation: it feels a need of preaching, and prayer, and holy spending of the Lord's day, and family duties, etc. Yes, it feels the need and benefit of frequency in duties, and is glad of leave to draw near to God, and feels the bond of love constrain. Whereas the proud are full and senseless, and could easily be content with little in religion, if the laws of God or man did not constrain them, and will do no more than they must. Yes, some of late have been advanced by pride above all ordinances, that is, above obedience to God, in the use of his appointed means, but not above the need of means, nor above the plagues prepared for the proud and disobedient. Humility secures men from many such pernicious opinions.

**Direction 4. To the conquering of pride, it is necessary that you perceive that indeed it is in yourselves, and is the radical sin, and the very poison of your hearts; and that you set yourselves watchfully to mark its motions; and make it a principal part of your religion and business of your lives to overcome it, and to walk in humility with God and man.**

For if you do not see that it is your sin, you will let it alone, and trouble yourselves little about it. Pride lives in men who seem religious, because they do not perceive it, or think they have but some small degree, which is not dangerous. And they do not see it in themselves, because they do not mark its operations and appearances: the life in the root must be perceived in the branches, in the leaves, and fruit. If you saw more evil in this, than in many more disgraceful sins, and set yourselves as heartily and diligently to conquer it, as you do to cast out the sins which would make you be judged by men to be utterly ungodly, no doubt the work would go on more happily, and you would see more excellent fruits of your labor, in the work of mortification, than most Christians see.

**Direction 5. Be much in humbling exercises; but so as to take heed of mistaking the nature of them, or running into extremes.**

I have told you the true nature of humility before. Abundance of Christians are tempted by Satan to think it consists, much more than it does, in passionate grief, and tears, and bodily exercises, of long and frequent fastings, and confessions, and penance, or such like: and thus Satan diverts them from true efforts for true humiliation, by keeping them employed all their days, in striving for tears, or in these external exercises! Whereas you should most strive for such a sight of your sinfulness and nothingness, as will teach you to esteem Christ highly, and to loathe yourselves, and take yourselves to be as vile and sinful as you are, and will make you humbly beg for mercy, and stoop to any means to obtain it; and will make you patient under the rebukes and chastisements of God, and under the contempts and injuries of men: this is the humility which you must labor for. But in order to this, external exercises of humiliation must be used: especially studying the holy law of God, and searching yourselves, and confession of sin, and moderate, seasonable fastings, and taming of the flesh. And indeed the exercises of humiliation most become those who are most prone to pride: and the doctrine of those men who cry down true humiliation, comes from pride, and is made to cherish pride in others. A humble soul cannot receive it; but is more prone here to run into excess.

**Direction 6. There is no more powerful means to take down pride, than to look seriously to God, and set yourselves before his eyes, and consider how he loves the humble, and abhors the proud.**

One sight of God by a living faith, would make you know with whom you have to do, and teach you to abhor yourselves as vile. A glowworm is not discerned in the sunshine, though it glitters in the dark. A glimpse of the majesty of God would make you, with Isaiah, cry out, "Woe to me! I am destroyed, for my lips are contaminated by sin," etc.; and, chapter 6:5, with the Israelites, desire that Moses, and not God, might speak to you, lest you die. Men are proud because they do not know God, and do not look to him, but to fellow-sinners, with whom they think they may be bold to compare themselves.

Remember also that God is as it were engaged against the proud, both in the holiness of his nature, and in honor; for a proud man sets up himself against him, and is such an idol as God will either take down by grace, or spurn into the fire of destruction. And if he does appear before God among others in days and external exercises of humiliation, you may judge how much an abhorred person will be accepted. It is not to all who are clothed in sackcloth, but to the humble soul that God has respect; even to the self-abhorring person, who judges himself unworthy to come among the people of God, or to be a doorkeeper in his house, or to eat of the crumbs of the children's bread; who subject themselves to one another, and think no office of love and service too low for them to perform to the least believer; who in charitable meekness instruct opposers, and bear contradiction and contempt from men; who patiently suffer the injuries of enemies and friends, and heartily forgive and love them; who bear the most sharp and plain reproofs with gentleness and thanks; who think the lowest place in men's esteem, affections, and respects, the fittest for them; who are much more concerned how they love others, than how others love them, and how they discharge their duties to others, than how others do what they ought for them; who will settle for smaller evidence to think well of the hearts or actions of others, than of their own; who reprove themselves more often and more sharply than other men reprove them, and are readier to censure themselves than others, or than most others are to censure them; who have a low esteem of their own understandings, and parts, and doings, and therefore are readier to learn than teach, and to hear than speak; who highly value every bit and drop of mercy, especially Christ, and grace, and glory. These are the humble whom God accepts, and this is the fast that he requires. These are they who pray effectively, and who must save the land. These only are aware what sin is; when others do not feel it, or are proud in the midst of their largest confessions and tears. These only from their hearts acknowledge their desert of God's severest judgments, and justify God when he afflicts them. Others rather marvel at the greatness and continuance of judgments, and argue with God as dealing hardly and unkindly with them, and tell him what a good people he afflicts. These only understand the sinfulness of their very humiliations and prayers, through the weakness of that good which should be in them, and the mixture of much evil; when the proud are marveling if God does not hear them at the first word. These only wait in patience for God's answer, and accept mercy in his time and measure; when the proud are short-winded, and if God does not come just when they expected, they do, with Saul, 1 Samuel 13:9-12, make haste, or murmur at his providence, and say it is in vain to serve the Lord, and begin to think of forsaking him, and taking some better way. These proud ones who have joined in outward humiliations, and have lifted up themselves in heart, while they cast down their bodies, are they who have turned the heart of God so much against us, to break us in pieces, because he has found among us so many of the proud whom he takes for his enemies. We have had those humbling themselves in our assemblies, who were wise in their own eyes, despising, and scorning, and reviling their teachers; such as undervalued and censured others, who were not for their opinions and interest; who over-loved the respect and honor that is from men, and could not endure to be looked down on or thought little of; who could not bear an injury or a foul word, but were prone to anger, if not revenge; who could not seek peace, nor stoop to others, nor bear plain dealing in reproof, nor forgive a wrong without much submission; who had high expectations from others, and loved those best who most esteemed them; who counted it baseness to stoop to the meanest places or services for others' good; yes, who quarreled with God, his word, and providences, and valued no other mercies but those that exalted themselves or pleased their flesh (which proved judgments). And yet while they thus by pride excommunicated themselves from the face of God, and made themselves abhorred by him, they separated from the holiest assemblies and servants of God in the land, as unworthy of communion with such as they, unless they would first become of their opinion and sect. We little consider how great a hand this pride has had in our desolations. God has been scattering the proud of all sorts in the imaginations of their own hearts, Luke 1:51.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®, fetched from labs.bible.org; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **Start of range:** the file opens at Baxter's numbered point 4 under Direction 3; points 1 to 3 are in the previous file. Directions 4, 5, and 6 are headed in bold using Baxter's first sentence; the rest of each Direction follows as ordinary text.
- **Point 6, the list of "general commands":** Baxter's quotations are loose King James fragments. We give NET wording for each, with the references in the order Baxter gives them.
  - 1 Thessalonians 5:17: NET "constantly pray" (KJV "pray without ceasing"; Baxter "pray continually").
  - Luke 18:1: NET "always pray and not lose heart"; Baxter's "not wax faint" is the KJV "not to faint." Same sense.
  - Galatians 6:9: NET "grow weary in doing good" (Baxter "weary of well doing," from the KJV).
  - Ephesians 5:16: Baxter's "redeem the time" (KJV) is NET "taking advantage of every opportunity." The NET drops the buying-back image ("redeem") that Baxter's phrase carries, though the sense of using time well is the same. We changed the form to "to take advantage of every opportunity" to fit his sentence.
  - 1 Corinthians 14:26: Baxter's "do all to edification" is his paraphrase of the verse's end; NET has "Let all these things be done for the strengthening of the church," which we give in his quotation marks with "do all" outside them.
  - Romans 12:11: Baxter's "fervent in spirit, serving the Lord" (KJV). NET reads "Do not lag in zeal, be enthusiastic in spirit, serve the Lord." We give "be enthusiastic in spirit, serve the Lord." The NET "enthusiastic" here is a plain modern word for what Baxter calls "fervent"; do not confuse it with Baxter's technical term "enthusiasts" (those who claimed direct inspiration), which does not occur in this range.
- **Isaiah 6:5 (Direction 6):** Baxter wrote "Woe is me, for I am undone, a man of unclean lips." NET reads "Woe to me! I am destroyed, for my lips are contaminated by sin, and I live among people whose lips are contaminated by sin." We quote through the first clause and keep his "etc." His "undone" (KJV) corresponds to the NET "destroyed." Baxter's reference is printed oddly: "chap. vi. 5" stands after the Isaiah quotation and before the Israelites clause, so it probably belongs to Isaiah; we keep it where he put it as "chapter 6:5." The Israelites' request that Moses speak and not God is an unmarked allusion; probably Exodus 20:19 (NET: "You speak to us and we will listen, but do not let God speak with us, lest we die"), also told in Deuteronomy 5. Not given by Baxter here.
- **1 Samuel 13:9-12:** a reference only (Saul offers the sacrifice in haste). Baxter's "make haste" is his summary, not a quotation; the NET has Saul say "I felt obligated to offer the burnt offering."
- **Luke 1:51:** reference only, with Baxter's wording ("scattering the proud ... in the imaginations of their own hearts," from the KJV). NET reads "he has scattered those whose pride wells up from the sheer arrogance of their hearts." The sense is the same; "imaginations" (the KJV) is a little different from the NET "arrogance."
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "the door-keepers in his house" (Psalm 84:10); "the crumbs of the children's bread" (Matthew 15:27; Mark 7:28); "this is the fast that he requires" (Isaiah 58:5-7); "it is in vain to serve the Lord" (Malachi 3:14); "wise in their own eyes" (Isaiah 5:21; Proverbs 3:7). These identifications are ours, not Baxter's.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): physic, medicine; extenuateth, minimizes; ofttimes, often; glister, glitter; sensible (of what sin is), aware; expostulate, argue; solicitous, concerned; disesteemed, looked down on; set by, thought of; ofter and sharplier, more often and more sharply; proner, more prone; use to ask, usually ask; wants it not, does not need it; effectually ("pray effectually"), effectively; lively (faith), living; door-keepers, a doorkeeper (Baxter's plural turned singular to match "to be"); "town" kept; "hath ... " verbs and thou-forms to modern forms throughout.
- **Kept as Baxter wrote:** "ado" (fuss); "short-winded" (quickly out of breath, here impatient); "interest" (one's self-interest or standing); "parts" (natural abilities); "affections" (feelings); "providences" (events seen as God's ordering); "means" (the ordinary ways God uses, such as preaching and prayer); "mortified" (those who have put sin to death); "mortification"; "conference" (spiritual conversation); "ordinances" (the church's appointed means, such as preaching, sacraments, prayer); "plain dealing"; "the radical sin" (the root sin); "excommunicated themselves" (here, cut themselves off).
- **"some of late ... above all ordinances":** Baxter's polemic against groups who claimed to be above the use of church ordinances; he does not name them here.
- **Source oddity:** the source prints two "[Sidenote]" lines together before "Remember also"; we give both as "In the margin" lines in that place, as printed.
- **"our desolations":** Baxter refers to the troubles of his own day; we have not tried to identify particular events.
- **"1 Cor. xiv. 26":** the reference does give the quoted idea about edification, in NET "strengthening." Left as Baxter has it.
