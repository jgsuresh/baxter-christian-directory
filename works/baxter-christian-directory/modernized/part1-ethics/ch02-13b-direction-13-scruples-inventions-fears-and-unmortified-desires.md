# Chapter 2: Direction 13 (part 2 of 2)

*In the margin: By scrupulosity.*

3. Another temptation to confuse you in your religion is by filling your heads with practical scrupulosity; so that you cannot go on for doubting every step whether you go right; and when you should cheerfully serve your Master, you will do nothing but disquiet your minds with scruples, whether this or that is right or wrong. Your remedy here is not by casting away all care of pleasing God, or fear of sinning, or by debauching conscience; but by a cheerful and quiet obedience to God, so far as you know his will, and an upright willingness and effort to understand it better; and a thankful receiving of the gospel pardon for your failings and weaknesses. Be faithful in your obedience; but live still upon Christ, and do not think of reaching any such obedience as will set you above the need of his merits, and a daily pardon of your sins. Do the best you can to know the will of God and do it: but when you know the essentials of religion, and obey sincerely, let no remaining lacks deprive you of the comfort of that so great a mercy, which proves your right to eternal life. In your seeking further for more knowledge and obedience, let your care be such as tends to your progress, and furthers you toward your end, and does not hinder your joy and thanks for what you have received: but whatever destroys your joy and thankfulness, and only perplexes you, and does not further you in your way, is merely harmful scrupulosity, and is to be laid aside. When you are right in the main, thank God for that, and be concerned further only so far as to help you on, but not to hinder you. If you send your servant on your errand, you would rather he went on his way as well as he can, than stand fretting over every step whether he should set the right or left foot forward; and whether he should step so far, or so far at a time, etc. Hindering scruples do not please God.

*In the margin: By setting you on overdoing by your own inventions.*

4. Another way to confuse you in your religion is by setting you on overdoing it with inventions of your own. When a poor soul is most desirous to please God, the devil will be religious, and set him on some such task of voluntary humility, or will-worship, as the apostle speaks of (Colossians 2:18, 20–23); or set him on some ensnaring, unnecessary vows or resolutions, or some papist works of fanciful supererogation, which is what Solomon calls being "excessively righteous" (Ecclesiastes 7:16). Thus many have made duties for themselves which God never made for them; and take as sin what God never forbade them. The papist religion is very largely made up of such commandments of their own, and traditions of men. As if Christ had not made us work enough, people are eager to make much more for themselves. And some who should teach them the laws of Christ think that their office is in vain unless they may also prescribe laws of their own to them, and give them new precepts of religion. Yes, some who are the bitterest enemies of the strict observance of the laws of God, as if it were a tedious, needless thing, must still insist on loading us with an abundance of unnecessary precepts of their own. And so religion is made both wearisome and uncertain, and a door is set open for people to enlarge it, and increase the burden at their pleasure. Indeed papistry is fitted to deceive and quiet sleepy consciences, and to torment with uncertainties the consciences that are awake.

And there is something in the corrupted nature of man that inclines him to some additions and voluntary service of his own inventions, as an offering most acceptable to God. Hence it is that many poor Christians rashly entangle their consciences with vows about circumstances and unnecessary things, such as to give so much, to observe such days or hours in fasting and prayer, not to do such or such a thing that is lawful in itself, with an abundance of such things, which perhaps some change of providence may make their duty to do afterward by circumstance, or may leave them unable to perform their vows; and then these snares are fetters on their perplexed consciences, perhaps as long as they live. Yes, some of the antinomians teach the people that things indifferent are the fittest matter of a vow; such as to live single, to possess nothing, to live in solitude, and the like: indeed all lawful things, when they are vowed, must be performed; but it is unfit to be vowed if it is not first profitable and best, for ourselves or others; and that which is best is not indifferent, since it is every man's duty to choose what is best. Vows are to bind us to the performance of what God had bound us to by his laws before; they are our expression of consent and resolution, by a self-obligation, to obey his will; and not to make new duties of religion for ourselves, which otherwise would never have been our duty.

To escape these snares, it is necessary that you be careful not to corrupt your religion by burdens and mixtures of your own devising. You are called to obey God's laws, and not to make laws for yourselves. You may be sure that his laws are just and good, but yours may be bad and foolish. When you obey him, you may expect your reward and encouragement from him: but when you choose to obey yourselves, you must reward yourselves. You may find it enough for you to keep his laws, without devising more work for yourselves; or inventing duties which he did not command, or sins which he did not forbid. Do not be rash in making vows; let them reach only to necessary duties; and let them have their due exceptions when they concern changeable things: or if you are entangled by them already, consult with the most judicious, able, impartial men, so that you may come clearly off without a wound. There is a great deal of judgment and sincerity necessary in your counselors, and a great deal of submission and self-denial in yourselves, to bring you safely out of such a snare. Avoid sin, whatever you do; for sinning is not the way to your deliverance. And for the time to come, be wiser, and lay no more snares for yourselves; and do not burden yourselves with your own inventions, but cheerfully obey what God commands you, who has wisdom and authority enough to make you perfect laws. Christ's yoke is "easy to bear," and his load "not hard to carry" (Matthew 11:30), and his commandments "do not weigh us down" (1 John 5:3). But if your mixtures and self-devised snares are burdensome to you, do not blame God, but yourselves who made them.

*In the margin: By overwhelming fears and sorrows.*

5. Another of Satan's ways to make religion burdensome and wearisome to you is by overwhelming you with fear and sorrow. Partly by persuading you that religion consists in excess of sorrow, and so causing you to spend your time striving to trouble and grieve yourselves unprofitably, as if it were the course most acceptable to God; and partly by taking advantage of a fearful, emotional nature; and so making every thought of God, or serious exercise of religion, a torment to you, by raising some overwhelming fears; for "fear has to do with punishment" (1 John 4:18). In some feminine, weak, and melancholy persons, this temptation has so much advantage in the body that the holiest soul can do but little in resisting it; so that though there is in such persons a sincere love for God, his ways and servants, yet fear so plays the tyrant in them that they perceive almost nothing else. And it is no wonder if religion is wearisome and unpleasant to such as these.

But, alas! it is you yourselves who are the causes of this, and bring the matter of your complaint with you. God has commanded you a sweeter work. It is a life of love, and joy, and cheerful progress to eternal joy that he requires of you; and no more fear or grief than is necessary to separate you from sin, and teach you to value and use the remedy. The gospel presents to you such abundant matter of joy and peace as would make these the very complexion and temperament of your souls, if you received them as they are presented. Religious fears, when they are excessive and hurtful, are sinful, and indeed against religion; and must be resisted like other hurtful passions. Become better acquainted with Christ and his promises, and you will find enough in him to calm the soul, and give you confidence and holy boldness in your access to God (Hebrews 4:16; Ephesians 3:12; Hebrews 10:19). The spirit which he gives is not the spirit of bondage, but the spirit of adoption, of love and confidence (Romans 8:15; Hebrews 2:15).

*In the margin: By unmortified lusts.*

6. Another thing that makes religion seem burdensome is retaining unmortified physical desires. If you keep up your lusts, they will strive against the gospel, and all the works of the Spirit which strive against them (Galatians 5:17). And every duty will be unpleasant to you in proportion as you are carnal, because it is against your carnal inclination and desire. Away, therefore, with your beloved sickness, and then both your food and your physician will be less burdensome to you. Mortify the flesh, and you will dislike the things of the Spirit less. For "the outlook of the flesh is hostile to God, for it does not submit to the law of God, nor is it able to do so" (Romans 8:7–8).

*In the margin: By actual sin.*

7. Another cause of confusing and wearying you is the mixture of your actual sins, dealing unfaithfully with God, and wounding your consciences by renewing guilt, especially of sins against knowledge and consideration. If you thus keep the bone out of joint, and the wound unhealed, it is no wonder if you are unwilling to work or travel. But it is your sin and folly that should be burdensome to you, and not that which is contrary to it, and would remove the cause of all your troubles. Resolutely forsake your willful sinning, and come home by "repentance toward God and faith in our Lord Jesus" (Acts 20:21), and then you will find that when the thorn is out, your pain will cease, and that the cause of your trouble was not in God or religion, but in your sin.

*In the margin: By ignorance of the tenor of the gospel.*

8. Lastly, to make religion unpleasant to you, the tempter would keep the substance of the gospel unknown or unnoticed by you: he would hide the wonderful love of God revealed in our Redeemer, and all the riches of saving grace, and the great deliverance and privileges of believers, and the certain hopes of eternal life: and the kingdom of God, which consists in righteousness, and peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit, will be represented to you as consisting in errors only, or in trifles; in shadows and shows, and bodily exercise which profits little (1 Timothy 4:8). If you ever would know the pleasures of faith and holiness, you must labor above all to know God as revealed in his infinite love in the Mediator, and read the gospel as God's amnesty, and the testament and covenant of Christ, in which he gives you eternal life: and in every duty draw near to God as a reconciled Father, the object of your everlasting love and joy. Know and use religion as it is, without mistaking or corrupting it, and it will not appear to you as a burdensome, tedious, or confusing thing.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). All quoted NET wording was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Where this begins:** the range starts in the middle of Direction 13, at item 3 of a list of ways the tempter makes religion burdensome (items 1 and 2 are in part 1). The numbers 3 to 8 are Baxter's.
- **Ecclesiastes 7:16:** Baxter's "righteous over-much" (King James) is NET "excessively righteous"; the sense he needs (going beyond what God requires) is the same. The NET adds "or excessively wise," which Baxter does not quote.
- **Matthew 11:30:** Baxter gives the words in the third person ("Christ's yoke is easy, and his burden light"). The NET has Jesus speaking in the first person ("my yoke is easy to bear, and my load is not hard to carry"). We use the NET's phrases inside Baxter's third-person sentence. **1 John 5:3:** the King James "not grievous" is NET "do not weigh us down"; the sense is close.
- **1 John 4:18 (flag):** Baxter leans on the King James "fear hath torment," which he uses to say that fear itself is painful. The NET has "fear has to do with punishment" (the context is fear of judgment, which perfect love drives out). The NET wording shifts the point from fear as pain to fear as dread of punishment; Baxter's argument about overwhelming fear still stands, but this verse supports it less directly.
- **Romans 8:7-8 (flag):** Baxter's "the carnal mind is enmity against God" is NET "the outlook of the flesh is hostile to God"; the NET reads "outlook" where the King James has "mind," and it has no word "carnal." Baxter's argument turns on "carnal" (the flesh ruling the person), which he keeps in his own prose. His quotation marks in the source also enclosed his own sentence "Mortify the flesh... Spirit"; we put the quotation marks only around the verse. His "For" is kept as the start of the next sentence; the NET verse itself begins "because."
- **Acts 20:21 (flag):** Baxter has "repentance towards God, and faith towards our Lord Jesus Christ." The NET reads "repentance toward God and faith in our Lord Jesus"; it lacks "Christ" and has "faith in" for "faith towards." The sense of the pairing (repentance then faith) is unchanged.
- **Colossians 2:18, 20-23 (flag):** Baxter cites these verses for "voluntary humility, or will-worship" without quoting them. The two King James phrases come from verses 18 and 23. The NET renders verse 18 as "false humility" and verse 23 as "self-imposed worship and humility achieved by an unsparing treatment of the body." The NET's "self-imposed" is close to Baxter's use ("inventions of your own"), but "voluntary" and "will-worship" are Baxter's (King James) words, kept here as he gave them.
- **1 Timothy 4:8:** Baxter's "bodily exercise which profiteth little" is his paraphrase of the King James; the NET reads "physical exercise has some value, but godliness is valuable in every way." Baxter's emphasis (that bodily exercise is nearly worthless beside the substance of the gospel) is stronger than the NET's "some value."
- **Citations only, not quoted:** Galatians 5:17; Hebrews 4:16, 10:19, 2:15; Ephesians 3:12; Romans 8:15. We converted these to modern form and did not check them against the NET. **"the spirit of bondage... the spirit of adoption"** is Baxter's paraphrase of Romans 8:15, kept in his words.
- **Unmarked allusions:** "the kingdom of God, which consists in righteousness, and peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit" (Romans 14:17); "Christ's yoke... load" (Matthew 11:30, cited); "traditions of men" (compare Mark 7:8, Colossians 2:8; not in Baxter's text as a reference).
- **scrupulosity; scruples:** kept. Baxter means an anxious, paralyzing doubt over whether each small act is right or wrong.
- **debauching conscience:** kept as Baxter wrote; he means corrupting the conscience, or dulling it.
- **supererogation:** kept; the Roman Catholic doctrine of works beyond what God requires. Baxter's "conceited" (self-important, fanciful) became "fanciful." **papist; popery** (his "popish," "popery"): kept as Baxter's polemic against Rome; "papistry" is our word for "popery."
- **antinomians:** kept. Baxter's term for those who hold that the moral law no longer binds believers; the teaching about vows is his report of their view. **things indifferent:** things neither commanded nor forbidden; a term of the day's debates.
- **endeavour** to **effort**; **tendeth to your profiting** to **tends to your progress**; **solicitous** to **concerned**; **confound** to **confuse**; **counsellors** to **counselors**; **propounded** to **presented**; **inordinate** to **excessive**; **passionate nature** to **emotional nature**; **timorous** to **fearful**; **disrelish** to **dislike**; **loth** to **unwilling**; **travail** to **travel** (Baxter's "work or travail" pairs labor with a journey); **wilful** to **willful**; **grievous** to **burdensome** or **wearisome** by context; **accidentally** to **by circumstance**; **feigning** to **inventing**; **act of oblivion** to **amnesty**; **sensual desires** to **physical desires**; **Holy Ghost** to **Holy Spirit**; **labour** to **labor**.
- **Item 3:** "scrupling every step" is rendered "fretting over every step." "On your message" is rendered "on your errand."
- **"feminine, weak, and melancholy persons":** Baxter's own wording and view that such persons are more exposed to this temptation through the body. **melancholy** is kept (see GLOSSARY.md). A dated generalization, kept as written.
- **"very complexion and temperament of your souls":** Baxter has "complexion and temperature"; "temperature" became "temperament." Both words use the language of the four bodily humors for one's settled inward disposition.
- **"beloved sickness":** a figure of a pet sin kept like a patient clings to a sickness; kept as written.
- **"the tenor of the gospel"** (margin note to item 8): the gospel's content or drift. Kept as Baxter wrote.
- **carnal; mortify; unmortified; lusts:** kept (see GLOSSARY.md and STYLE_GUIDE.md).
- **Mid-item "etc.":** Baxter's "&c." becomes "etc."
- **Papistry polemic:** the claims in item 4 about Roman religion and about preachers who "load" people with unnecessary rules are Baxter's own, not confirmed by us.
- **Source oddity:** in item 5 Baxter writes "Partly by persuading that religion consisteth..." with no object; we added "you" to complete the grammar.
