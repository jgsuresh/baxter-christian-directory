# Chapter 4: Directions against hypocrisy, Directions 7-17

**Direction 7. Do not place more in the externals, or modes, or circumstances, or ceremonies of worship, than is due; and do not lay out more zeal for indifferent or little things than comes to their share; but let the great substantials of religion have the precedence, and be far preferred before them. [150]**

Let the love of God and man be the sum of your obedience; and be sure you learn well what that means, "I want mercy and not sacrifice." And remember that the great thing which God requires of you is "to carry out justice, to love faithfulness, and to live obediently before your God"—"Do not destroy by your food someone for whom Christ died." Do not call for fire from heaven upon dissenters; and do not think every man intolerable in the church that is not in every little matter of your mind. Remember that the hypocrisy of the Pharisees is described by Christ as consisting in a zeal for their own traditions, and the inventions of men, and the smallest matters of the ceremonial law, with a neglect of the greatest moral duties, and a furious cruelty against the spiritual worshippers of God. Matthew 15:2, "Why do your disciples disobey the tradition of the elders? For they don't wash their hands when they eat." Verses 7-10, "Hypocrites! Isaiah prophesied correctly about you when he said, 'This people honors me with their lips, but their hearts are far from me, and they worship me in vain, teaching as doctrines the commandments of men.'" (Matthew 15:7-9). Matthew 23:4-6, 13, 14, etc. "They tie up heavy loads ... but they themselves are not willing even to lift a finger to move them. They do all their deeds to be seen by people, for they make their phylacteries wide and their tassels long. They love the place of honor at banquets and the best seats in the synagogues, and elaborate greetings in the marketplaces and to have people call them 'Rabbi.'—... You keep locking people out of the kingdom of heaven!" and were the greatest enemies of the reception of the gospel by the people. They "give a tenth of mint, dill, and cumin," and "neglect what is more important in the law—justice, mercy, and faithfulness." They "strain out a gnat yet swallow a camel." They had a great veneration for the "dead prophets and saints," and yet were persecutors and murderers of their "successors" that were "living," verse 23, etc. By this description you may see which way hypocrisy most ordinarily works: even to a blind and bloody zeal for opinions, and traditions, and ceremonies, and other little things, to the treading down of the interest of Christ and his gospel, and a neglect of the life and power of godliness, and a cruel persecuting of those servants of Christ whom they are bound to love above their ceremonies. I marvel that many papists do not tremble when they read the character of the Pharisees! But that hypocrisy is a hidden sin, and is an enemy to the light which would discover it.

**Direction 8. Be conscientious about the duties of obedience to superiors, and of justice and mercy toward men, as well as of acts of piety to God.**

Do not say a long mass in order to devour a widow's house, or a Christian's life or reputation. Be equally exact in justice and mercy as you are in prayers; and labor as much to exceed common men in the one as in the other. Set yourselves to do all the good you can to all, and do hurt to none; and do to all men as you would have them do to you.

**Direction 9. Be much more busy about yourselves than about others; and more censorious of yourselves than of other men; and more strict in the reforming of yourselves than of any others.**

For this is the character of the sincere: when the hypocrite is little at home and much abroad; and is a sharp reprehender of others, and perniciously tender and indulgent to himself. Mark his talk in all companies, and you shall hear how liberal he is in his censures and bitter reproach of others: how such men, and such men (that differ from him, or have opposed him, or that he hates) are thus and thus faulty, and bad, and hateful. Indeed, he is as great an accuser of his adversaries for hypocrisy as if he were not a hypocrite himself; because he can accuse them of a heart sin without any visible control. If he calls them drunkards, or swearers, or persecutors, or oppressors, all that know them could know that he belies them; but when he speaks about matters in the dark, he thinks the reputation of his lies have more advantage. Many a word you may hear from him, how bad his adversaries are; but if such hypocritical talk did not tell you, he would not tell you how bad he is himself. [151]

**Direction 10. Be impartial, and set yourselves before your consciences in the case of others.**

Think to yourselves, How should I judge of this, in such and such a man, that I usually blame? What should I say of him, if my adversary did as I do? And is it not as bad in me as in him? Is not the sin most dangerous to me that is nearest me? And should I be more vigilant over any man's faults than my own? My damnation will not be caused by his sin; but by my own it may. Instead of seeing the gnat in his eye, I have more cause to cast out a gnat from my own than a camel from his.

**Direction 11. Strive first to be whatever (judiciously) you desire to seem.**

Desire a thousand times more to be godly than to seem so; and to be generous than to be thought so; and to be blameless from every secret or presumptuous sin than to be esteemed such. [152] And when you feel a desire to be considered good, let it make you think how much more necessary and desirable it is to be good indeed. To be godly is to be an heir of heaven: your salvation follows it. But to be esteemed godly is of little profit to you.

**Direction 12. Do not overvalue man, and do not set more by the approval or applause of his thoughts or speeches of you than they are worth.**

Hypocrisy much consists in overvaluing man, and making too great a matter of his thoughts and words. The hypocrite's religion is divine in name, but human in deed: it is man that he serves and observes most; and the shame of the world is the evil which he most studiously avoids; and the high esteem and commendation of the world is his reward. O think, what a pitiful worm is man! And of how little importance are his thoughts or speeches of you, in comparison of the love of God! His thoughts of you make you not the better or the worse; and if they either lift you up or trouble you, it is your proud and foolish fantasy that does it, when you might choose. If you have not lost the key and government of your hearts, shut the door, and keep all that out, and let men's reproaches go no further than your ears; and then how much worse will you be for all the lies and slanders of the world? And besides the pleasing of an effeminate mind, how much better are you for their applause? [153]

**Direction 13. Look upon all men that you associate with, as ready to die and turn to dust, and passing into that world where you will be little concerned in their censure or esteem of you.**

If you do anything before an infant, you little care for his presence or observation of you: much less if it be before the dead. If you knew that a man were to die tomorrow, though he were a prince, you would not be much solicitous to avoid his censure or procure his applause; because his thoughts all perish with him; and it is a small matter what he thinks of you for a day. Since therefore all men are hastening to their dust, and you are certain that all that applaud or censure you will be quickly gone, how little should you regard their judgment! Look that man in the face whose applause you desire, or whose censure you fear, and remember that he is a breathing clod of clay; and how many such are now in the grave, whose thoughts you once as much esteemed! and this will make you more indifferent in the case.

**Direction 14. At least remember that you are passing out of the world yourselves, and look out every moment for when you are called away, and certainly know that you will be here but a little while.**

And is it any great matter what strangers think of you as you are passing by? You can be contented that your name, and worth, and virtues be concealed in your inn, where you stay but a night, and that they be unknown to travelers that meet you on the road. The foolish expectation of more time on earth than God has given you warrant to expect is the cause that we overvalue the judgment of man, as well as other earthly things, and is a great maintainer of every vice of physical indulgence.

**Direction 15. Set yourselves to the mortifying of self-love and pride:**

for hypocrisy is but the exercise of these. Hypocrisy is dead so far as pride is dead; and so far as self-denial and humility prevail. Hypocrisy is a proud desire to appear better than you are. Be thoroughly humbled and vile in your own eyes, and hypocrisy is done.

**Direction 16. Be most suspicious of your hearts in cases where self-interest or passions are engaged;**

for they will easily deal deceitfully and cheat yourselves, in the smoke and dust of such distempers. Interest and passion so blind the mind that you may truly think you are defending the truth, and serving God in sincerity and zeal, when all the while you are but defending some error of your own, and serving yourselves, and fighting against God. The Pharisees thought they took part with God's law and truth against Christ. The pope, and his cardinals and prelates, think (as in charity I must think) that it is for Christ, and unity, and truth, that they try to subject the world to their own power. And what is it but interest that blinds them into such hypocrisy? So, emotional debaters ordinarily deceive themselves, and think truly that they are zealous for the faith, when they are but contending for their honor or notions. Passion hides much deceit from the emotional.

**Direction 17. Suspect yourselves most among the great, the wise, the learned, and the godly, or any whose favor, opinion, or applause you most esteem.**

It is easy for an outright hypocrite to despise the favor or opinion of the common people, of the ignorant, of the profane, or any whose judgment he holds in contempt. It is no great honor or dishonor to be praised or dispraised by a child, or a fool, or a person that for his ignorance or profaneness has become contemptible. But hypocrisy and pride work most to procure the esteem of those whose judgment or talents you most admire. One most admires worldly greatness; and such a one will play the hypocrite most, to flatter or please the great ones he admires. Another that is wiser more admires the judgment of the wise and learned; and he will play the hypocrite to procure the good esteem of such, though he can slight a thousand of the ignorant; and his pride itself will make him slight them. Another that is yet wiser is convinced of the excellency of godly men, above all the great and learned of the world: and this man is more in danger of pride and hypocrisy in seeking the good opinion of the godly; and therefore can despise the greatest multitude of the ignorant and profane. Indeed, pride itself will make him take it as an addition to his glory to be vilified and opposed by such miscreants as these.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[150] It is a pitiful cure of the Indians' idolatry, which the honest Jesuit Acosta (as the rest) prescribes, book 5, chapter 11, page 483. "But you must especially take care that saving rites be introduced instead of hurtful ones, and ceremonies be obliterated by ceremonies. Let the priests persuade the novices that holy water, images, rosaries, grains, and torches, and the rest which the church allows and uses, are very fit for them; and let them extol them with many praises in their popular sermons, that instead of the old superstition they may be used to new and religious signs." This is to quench the fire with oil.

[151] It is one of Thales' sayings, in Laert. Q. Quomodo optime ac justissime vivemus? Resp. Si quæ in aliis reprehendimus ipsi non faciamus. (English: Question: How shall we live best and most justly? Answer: If we ourselves do not do the things we blame in others.) To judge of ourselves as we judge of others is the way of the sincere.

[152] Cato, homo virtuti simillimus qui nunquam recte fecit, ut facere videretur, sed quia aliter facere non poterat; cuique id solum visum est rationem habere, quod haberet justitiam. Velleius Patercul. 1. 2. (English: Cato, a man most like virtue itself, who never did right in order to seem to do right, but because he could not do otherwise; and to whom that alone seemed to have reason which had justice. Velleius Paterculus, book 2.)

[153] Jam in ecclesiis ista quæruntur, et omissa Apostolicorum simplicitate et puritate verborum, quasi ad Athenæum et ad auditoria convenitur ut plausus circumstantium suscitentur, ut oratio rhetoricæ artis fucata mendacio quasi quædam meretricula procedat in publicum, non tam eruditura populos, quam favorem populi quæsitura. Hieron. in Præf. 1. 3. in Galat. (English: Now these things are sought in the churches; and, the simplicity and purity of the apostles' words having been set aside, people gather as if at a lecture hall or auditorium so that the applause of the bystanders may be stirred up, so that a speech painted over with the falsehood of rhetorical art may go out into public like some little harlot, not so much to teach the people as to seek the people's favor. Jerome, in the Preface to book 3 on Galatians. Rendered somewhat loosely.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). Every verse in quotation marks was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Range begins at the start of Direction 7.** It ends at the end of Direction 17; the next file continues the section.
- **"I want mercy and not sacrifice":** Baxter gives no reference. It is Hosea 6:6, quoted by Jesus in Matthew 9:13 and 12:7; we quote the NET wording of Matthew 9:13. (The NET of Hosea 6:6 itself reads "I delight in faithfulness, not simply in sacrifice," so it differs; Baxter's wording follows the form in Matthew.) Reference added here only; not in Baxter's text.
- **Micah 6:8 (flagged):** Baxter's quotation has no reference. It is Micah 6:8, which he gives as "to do justice, and love mercy, and walk humbly with your God." The NET has "to carry out justice, to love faithfulness, and to live obediently before your God." Baxter's "mercy" (which echoes the "mercy" he has just quoted) becomes "faithfulness," and "walk humbly" becomes "live obediently." His "love of God and man" and "mercy" emphasis is therefore less visible in the NET wording. Reference added here only.
- **Romans 14:15:** Baxter's "Destroy not him with thy meat for whom Christ died" has no reference in his text; it is Romans 14:15. NET: "Do not destroy by your food someone for whom Christ died." In his source it is run into the Micah quotation with "--"; we show the join with a dash outside the quotation marks. "Meat" meant food in general.
- **"Call not for fire from heaven":** an unmarked allusion, probably to Luke 9:54.
- **Matthew 15:2:** the King James, which Baxter quotes, has "eat bread"; the NET has "eat." Baxter's "Ver. 7-10" is kept as printed; his quotation covers only verses 7-9 (verse 10 begins a new thought in the NET), so we cite Matthew 15:7-9 in parentheses. The NET's "Hypocrites!" follows Baxter's "Ye hypocrites."
- **Matthew 23:4-7, 13, 14 (flagged):** Baxter's quotation blends and abbreviates these verses. NET differences: "bind heavy burdens, which they touch not themselves" becomes "tie up heavy loads ... but they themselves are not willing even to lift a finger to move them" (we elide the NET's "hard to carry, and put them on men's shoulders," which Baxter does not quote); "enlarge the borders of their garments" becomes "make their ... tassels long"; "greetings in public" becomes "elaborate greetings in the marketplaces." Verse 7 (greetings, Rabbi) is joined by Baxter to verse 6 as one sentence; we kept the sequence. The "..." and dash mark his "--" break. Baxter's last quotation, "shut up the kingdom of heaven against men," is verse 13, where the NET has "You keep locking people out of the kingdom of heaven!" We quote the NET after the ellipsis. Verse 14 does not appear in the NET text (probably because it is a textual variant; we have not confirmed), so there is no NET verse 14 to quote; Baxter's reference is kept as printed.
- **Matthew 23:23-24:** Baxter's "tithed mint, and anise, and cummin, and omitted the great matters of the law, judgment, and mercy, and faith" and "strained at a gnat, and swallowed a camel" are in the past tense. We quote the NET (present tense: "give a tenth of mint, dill, and cumin"; "neglect what is more important in the law—justice, mercy, and faithfulness"; "strain out a gnat yet swallow a camel"), so the tenses differ. "Anise" is the King James word; the NET has "dill." "Judgment" becomes "justice" and "faith" becomes "faithfulness." "Strained at a gnat" in the King James is commonly read as "strain out"; the NET makes that clear.
- **"dead prophets and saints," "successors," "living":** not a verbatim quotation of any verse; Baxter's summary of Matthew 23:29-31 and following, kept in his quotation marks. His "ver. 23, &c." is kept as printed; the matter he describes lies in verses 29-36 (and verse 23 is the tithing verse).
- **"entertainment of the gospel":** Baxter's "entertainment" meant reception or welcome; we wrote "reception."
- **"But that hypocrisy is a hidden sin":** kept as Baxter wrote it; the sense is "except that hypocrisy is a hidden sin."
- **Word swaps:** Baxter's "Place not" and similar commands became "Do not place," etc.; "make conscience of" became "be conscientious about"; "belieth" became "belies" (tells lies about); "Think with yourselves" became "Think to yourselves"; "as you would they should do to you" (Direction 8) became "as you would have them do to you"; "study" (strive) became "strive"; "liberal" in Direction 11 (generous) became "generous" (in Direction 9, "liberal in his censures," meaning lavish, is kept); "approbation" became "approval"; "silly" (pitiful, weak) became "pitiful"; "moment" (importance) became "importance"; "converse with" became "associate with"; "look every moment when" became "look out every moment for when"; "sensual vice" became "vice of physical indulgence"; "arrant" became "outright"; "the vulgar" became "the common people"; "contemneth" became "holds in contempt"; "parts" (abilities) became "talents"; "passionate" became "emotional" (as in the glossary); "disputers" became "debaters"; "conceits" became "notions"; "endeavour" became "try"; "verily" became "truly"; "honour" and similar spellings Americanized.
- **Kept words:** "mortifying" (putting to death), "distempers" (disorders of mind or body), "effeminate" (soft, self-indulgent; not a comment on women), "papists" (Baxter's word for Roman Catholics), "reprehender" (one who blames), "solicitous" (anxious), "miscreants" (here, base or wretched people). His remarks about the pope, cardinals, prelates, and papists are his polemic against Rome and are kept as written; he adds "as in charity I must think."
- **Grammar kept:** "the reputation of his lies have more advantage" (Direction 9) is Baxter's grammar, left as is.
- **Footnote 150:** the quotation from Acosta is given in English in Baxter's text (apparently his own rendering of Acosta's Latin, though he does not say so); we only modernized it. "The Indians" is Baxter's (and Acosta's) word for the native peoples of the Americas. Page and chapter numbers are Baxter's. We have not checked them.
- **Footnote 151:** "Laert." probably abbreviates Diogenes Laertius (we have not confirmed this). The Thales question and answer is Latin; the draft English is given above.
- **Footnote 152:** "Patercul. 1. 2." is kept as printed; the "1." probably abbreviates "liber" (book); we render "book 2." Not confirmed.
- **Footnote 153:** "Præf. 1. 3." read as "Preface to book 3." Jerome's commentary on Galatians; not confirmed. "Athenæum" (the Roman lecture hall) is kept in the Latin and rendered "lecture hall." "Meretricula" ("little harlot") is a blunt word and is rendered as such.
