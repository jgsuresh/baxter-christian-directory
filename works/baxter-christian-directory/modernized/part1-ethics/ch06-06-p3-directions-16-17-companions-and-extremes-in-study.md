# Chapter 6: Title 6, Directions 16-17 on companions and on extremes in study

*This file continues Title 6 (directions for young students) from the previous file; it begins at a new Direction and ends partway through Direction 17, after Instance 7.*

**Direction 16. Be sure you make choice of fit teachers and companions for your studies and your lives; that they are such as will assist you in the holy practice of what you know, as well as in your knowledge: and shun as a plague the familiarity, 1. Of pleasure-loving, idle, brutish persons. 2. And of carnal, ambitious ones, who know no higher end than promotion and applause. 3. And of proud, heretical, contentious wits, whose wisdom and religion are nothing but censuring, reproaching, and vilifying those that are wiser and better than themselves.**

Bad company is the common ruin of both: their own love of pleasure is easily stirred up by the temptations of the pleasure-loving; and their consciences overborne by the examples of other men's self-indulgent lives. It emboldens them to sin, to see others sin before them; as cowards themselves are drawn on in an army to run upon the face of death, by seeing others do it, and to avoid the reproach of cowardice; and the noise of mirth and ranting language are the drums and trumpets of the devils, by which their ears are kept from hearing the cries of wounded, dying men, the lamentations of those that have found the error of that way. And there is in corrupted nature so strong an inclination to the prosperity and vainglory of the world, that makes them quickly take the bait, especially when the devil does offer it to them by a fit instrument, which will not deter them, as it would do if he had offered it to them himself. It is a pleasant thing to flesh and blood to be rich and great, and generally applauded; and a grievous thing to be poor, and despised, and afflicted. [321] The rawness also and unsettledness of youth, who lack well-furnished understandings and experience, is a great advantage to heretics and deceivers, who continually sweep many such away, wherever they come and have but opportunity. Children are "easily tossed up and down, and carried to and fro with every wind of doctrine, by the cunning trickery and subtlety of those that lie in wait to deceive" (Ephesians 4:14). Deceivers have their methods; and methods are the common instruments of deceit, which are not easily detected by the inexperienced. On the contrary, the benefit of wise, and staid, and sober, and peaceable, meek, humble, holy, heavenly companions is exceedingly great, especially to youth! Such will lead them in safe paths, and be continually preserving them, and promoting the most necessary parts of knowledge, and quickening them to holy practice, which is the end of all.

**Direction 17. In all your studies be wary of both extremes; and distinctly discern which are the extremes, that you do not run into one while you avoid the other. And be especially careful that you do not imagine co-ordinates or subordinates to be opposites; and do not throw away every truth which you cannot at once place rightly in the frame, and see it fall in agreeably with the rest; for a further insight into true method (attained only by very few) may reconcile you to that which now offends you. What God has joined together, be sure that you never separate; though as yet you cannot find their proper places. [322]**

There is scarcely any error more common among students than supposing those truths to be inconsistent which indeed have a necessary dependence on each other; and casting truth away as error, because they cannot reconcile it to some other truth. And there is nothing that so much causes this as lack of a true method. But one that has no significant method, or after much careful labor has fallen upon a false method, or a method that in any one significant point is out of joint, will deal this way with many certain truths: as an ignorant person that is to set all the scattered parts of a clock or watch together, if he misplaces one, will be unable rightly to place all the rest; and then, when he finds that they do not fit the place which he thinks they must be in, he casts them away, and thinks they are not the right ones, and is searching for or makes something else to fit that place. False method rejects many a truth.

And, unless it is in loving God, or other acts of the superior faculties, about their ultimate end and highest object, there is scarcely anything in this mortal life but has its extremes. And where they are not discerned, they are seldom well avoided. And usually narrow-sighted persons are fearful only of one extreme, and see no danger but on one side; and therefore are easily carried, by avoiding that, into the contrary.

I think it not unprofitable to give instances in several particular cautions, that you do not imitate those that separate what God has conjoined, and do not cast away truth as often as you are puzzled in the right placing or methodizing of it.

*Instance 1.* The first and second causes are conjoined in their operations, and therefore must not be separated. If the way of influx, concourse, or co-operation is dark and unsearchable to you, do not deny that it is, because you do not see how it is. The honor of the first and second cause also are conjoined, according to their several interests in the effects: do not therefore imagine that all the honor ascribed to the second cause is denied or taken away from the first; for then you do not understand their order: otherwise you would see that as the second causes in dependence on the first, and in subordination to it, and has no power but what is imparted by it, so it has no honor but what is received from it; and that it is no less honor for the first cause to operate mediately by the second than immediately by itself: and that there is no less of the power, wisdom, or goodness of God in an effect produced by means and second causes than in that which he produces of himself only, without them: and that it is his goodness to impart a power of good to his creatures, and the honor of working and causing under him: but he never loses anything by imparting, nor has the less himself by giving to his creatures: for if all that honor that is given to the creature were taken wrongfully from God, then God would never have made the world, nor made a saint; and then the worst creatures would least dishonor God: then he would not shine by the sun, but by himself immediately: and then he would never glorify either saint or angel. But on the contrary, it is God's honor to work by adapted means; and all their honor is truly his; as all the commendation of a clock or watch is given to the workman. And though God does not do all so immediately as to use no means or second causes; yet he is none the further from the effect, but, *immediatione virtutis et suppositi*, is himself as near as if he used none.

*Instance 2.* The special providence of God, and his being the first universal cause, are conjoined with the culpability of sinners; and no man must separate these. Those that cannot see just how they are conjoined may be sure that they are conjoined. It is no dishonor to an engineer that he can make a watch which will go longer than he is moving it with his finger. Nor is it a dishonor to our Creator that he can make a creature which can morally determine itself to an action as commanded or forbidden, without the predetermination of its Maker, though not without his universal concourse necessary to action as action. If Adam could not do this through the natural impossibility of it, then the law was that he should die the death if he did not overcome God, or do that which was naturally impossible; and this was the nature of his sin. Few dare say that God cannot make a free, self-determining agent; and if he can, we shall easily prove that he has; and the force of their opposition then has vanished.

*Instance 3.* The omniscience of God, and his dominion, government, and decrees, are conjoined with the liberty and sin of man: yet these by many are separated: as if God must either be ignorant or be the author of sin! As if he made one poor by decreeing to make another rich! As if he cannot be a perfect governor unless he brings all his subjects perfectly to keep his laws! As if all the fault of those that break the law were to be laid upon the maker of the law! As if all God's will *de debito* were not effective of its proper work, unless man fulfills it in the event! And as if it were possible for any creature to comprehend the way of the Creator's knowledge.

*Instance 4.* Many would separate nature and grace, which God the author of both conjoins. When grace presupposes nature, and in her garden sows all her seed, and excites and rectifies all her powers; yet these men talk as if nature had been annihilated, or grace came to annihilate it, and not to cure it. As if the leprosy and disease of nature were nature itself! And as if natural good had been lost as much as moral good! As if man were not man till grace made him a man!

*Instance 5.* Many separate the natural power of a sinner from his moral impotency, and his natural freedom of will from his moral servitude, as if they were inconsistent, when they are conjoined. As if the natural faculty might not coexist with an evil disposition; or a natural power with a habitual unwillingness to exercise it aright. And as if a sinner were not still a man.

*Instance 6.* Many separate general and special grace and redemption, as inconsistent, when they are conjoined; when the general is the proper way and means of accomplishing the ends of the special grace, and is always presupposed. As if God could not give more to some, if he gives anything to all. Or as if he gave nothing to all, if he gives more to any. As if he could not deal equally and without difference with all as a legislator, and righteously with all as a judge, unless he deals equally and without difference with all as a benefactor, in the free distribution of his gifts. As if he were obliged to make every worm and beast a man, and every man a king, and every king an angel, and every clod a star, and every star a sun!

*Instance 7.* Many separate the glory of God and man's salvation, God and man, in assigning the ultimate end of man! As if a moral intention might not take in both! As if it were not *finis amantis*; and the end of a lover were not union in mutual love! As if love to God may not be forever the final act, and God himself the final object: and as if, in this magnetic closure, though both may be called the end, yet there might not in the closing parties be an infinite disproportion, and only one be *finis ultimate ultimus*.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[321] Sana consultatio est ex eruditia; multarumque rerum peritia et experientia. Plato in Laert. (Sound counsel comes from learning, and from skill and experience in many things. Plato, in Laertius.)

[322] Cum opiniones tam variæ sint et inter se dissidentes, alterum fieri potest, ut earum nulla, alterum certe non potest, ut plus una vera fit. Cicero de Nat. Deor. page 5. (Since opinions are so various and disagree among themselves, the one thing can happen, that none of them is true; the other certainly cannot, that more than one is true. Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods*, page 5.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **Title:** Baxter's "_Tit._" is his abbreviation for "Title," that is, a heading or division of the chapter. This file is in Title 6, his directions for young students.
- **Ephesians 4:14 (paraphrase):** Baxter's "easily tossed up and down, and carried to and fro with every wind of doctrine, by the cunning sleight and subtlety of them that lie in wait to deceive" is a loose rendering of the King James ("tossed to and fro, and carried about with every wind of doctrine, by the sleight of men, and cunning craftiness, whereby they lie in wait to deceive"); "easily" and "up and down" are his own. We kept his wording, modernized ("sleight" to "trickery"), in his quotation marks. The NET reads: "So we are no longer to be children, tossed back and forth by waves and carried about by every wind of teaching by the trickery of people who craftily carry out their deceitful schemes." The NET drops the image of deceivers who "lie in wait," which Baxter's point about deceivers with their "methods" leans on. Flagged.
- **"What God has joined together ... never separate"** (Direction 17): an unmarked allusion to Matthew 19:6 (Mark 10:9), where it is said of marriage; Baxter applies it to truths that belong together. The King James has "put asunder"; we use "separate" here and throughout (Baxter's "put asunder" in the Direction and in Instances 1-3), which also matches the NET's "let no one separate." The allusion is kept in Baxter's wording, not quoted from the NET.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): meet, fit; sensual (persons), pleasure-loving; sensuality, love of pleasure; the sensual, the pleasure-loving; voluptuous, self-indulgent; preferment, promotion; imboldeneth, emboldens; want, lack; still (sweep away; be preserving; is supposed), continually / always; exceeding, exceedingly; jealous of, wary of; presently, at once; scarce, scarcely; considerable (method, point), significant; curious (labor), careful; in mortality, in this mortal life; instance in, give instances in; conjunct, conjoined; put asunder, separate; communicate / communicating (a power of good), impart / imparting; injuriously, wrongfully; procure (his subjects to keep), brings; supposeth, presupposes; consist with, coexist with; "never the further," none the further.
- **wits** (Direction 16): clever people, especially the sharp and argumentative. Kept. **staid:** settled, sober. Kept. **quickening:** stirring to life. Kept.
- **carnal:** unspiritual; ruled by the flesh (see GLOSSARY.md).
- **"the common ruin of both"** (first sentence of Direction 16): left as written. "Both" seems to refer back to "your studies and your lives" in the Direction; we have not changed it.
- **co-ordinates or subordinates** (Direction 17): truths that stand side by side, or one under another, as opposed to truths that contradict each other. Kept. **methodizing:** putting into a method, ordering. Kept.
- **"But that hath no method considerable"** (Direction 17): the source reads "But that hath"; the sense requires "he that hath." We render "But one that has" and note the probable misprint.
- **"independence on the first, and insubordination to it"** (Instance 1): the source prints these as single words, which reverses the sense. Baxter's argument (the second cause "hath no power but what is communicated by it") requires "in dependence on the first, and in subordination to it." We render it that way and note the probable printing error.
- **first and second causes; influx; concourse; mediately / immediately** (Instances 1-2): technical terms of the school theology Baxter uses. God is the first cause; creatures acting under him are second causes. "Concourse" is God's concurrence with the action of creatures; "influx" is the influence of a cause on its effect. Kept.
- ***immediatione virtutis et suppositi*** (Instance 1): Latin school term, roughly "by immediacy of power and of the agent [*suppositum*, the acting subject] himself": that is, God is present to the effect both by his power and in his own being, even when he works through means. Draft rendering; needs a Latinist's check.
- **"die the death"** (Instance 2): left as written; an emphatic phrase meaning "surely die," which probably echoes the King James wording of the threat to Adam (compare Genesis 2:17). We have not confirmed the exact echo.
- **predetermination; universal concourse** (Instance 2): Baxter is arguing against the view that God physically predetermines every act of the will, while keeping God's general concurrence with all action. Left as written.
- ***de debito*** (Instance 3): Latin, "concerning what is due," that is, God's will as lawgiver about what ought to be done, as distinct from his will about what will happen. Draft rendering.
- **general and special grace and redemption** (Instance 6): Baxter's own view, that a general grace and redemption is given to all and a special grace to the elect, and that the two do not contradict each other. Left as written; it is his position in a controversy of his day.
- ***finis amantis*** (Instance 7): Latin, "the end of a lover"; Baxter explains it himself in the next clause. ***finis ultimate ultimus***: probably a misprint for *finis ultimatè ultimus*, "the ultimately last end." Kept as printed.
- **magnetic closure** (Instance 7): Baxter's image of the union of God and the soul in love, drawing together like a magnet and iron. Kept.
- **Note 321:** "eruditia" is probably a misprint for *eruditione* ("learning"); we translate on that reading. Baxter attributes the saying to Plato as reported in Diogenes Laertius; we have not checked the passage.
- **Note 322:** "fit" at the end is probably a misprint for *sit* ("is"); we translate on that reading. The page reference is Baxter's; we have not checked it.
