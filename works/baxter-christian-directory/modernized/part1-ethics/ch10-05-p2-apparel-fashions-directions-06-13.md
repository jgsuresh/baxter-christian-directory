# Chapter 10: Part 3 (apparel), Question 4 and Directions 6–13

*This file begins partway through Direction 5 of Part 3 (on apparel), at the fourth of the questions that follow that Direction.*

*Question 4.* Is it not sometimes lawful to follow the fashions?

*Answer.* It is always lawful to follow the sober fashions of sober people; but it is not lawful to follow the empty, immodest, ill-signifying fashions of the riotous, proud, and wanton sort: unless it is in such cases of necessity as David was in, when he behaved himself like a madman, or as Paul when he told them that he was a Pharisee (Acts 23:6), to escape in a persecution, or from thieves or enemies. 2. Or unless for a time it proves as conducive to the good of others, as Paul's circumcising Timothy was, or his becoming all things to all men, that he might win some. [611] But to follow ill-signifying fashions, unnecessarily, or for carnal ends, to avoid the low esteem or evil speeches of carnal persons, or to seem to be as fine as they, this is undoubtedly a sin.

**Direction 6. Be sure to avoid excess of costliness in your apparel.**

Remember that you must answer for all your estates. And one day it will prove more comforting to find on your accounts, So much a year laid out in clothing the naked, than, So much a year in finery or elaborateness for yourselves or your children. Costly apparel devours that which would go far in supplying the necessities of the poor.

**Direction 7. Be sure you do not waste your precious time in needless elaborateness of dressing.**

I cannot easily tell you how great a sin, and horrible sign of folly and misery, it is in those gallants that spend whole hours, indeed, most of the morning, in dressing and prettifying themselves, before they appear to the sight of others; so that some of them can scarcely do anything else before dinner time, but dress themselves. The morning hours that are fittest for prayer, and reading the word of God, are thus consumed. They do not spend a quarter so much time in the serious searching and adorning of their souls, nor in any holy service of God; but God, and family, and soul, and all is thus neglected.

**Direction 8. Next to the usefulness of your apparel for your bodies and labors, let your rule be to imitate the common sort of the grave and sober persons of your own rank.**

Not here and there one that in other things are sober, who themselves follow the fashions of the proud and empty; but the ordinary fashion of grave and sober persons. For thus you will avoid both the frivolity of the proud, and the needless singularity of others.

**Direction 9. Regard more the hurt that your fashion may do, than the offense or reproach of anyone.**

For proud persons to say you are shabby, or not fine enough, and talk of your coarse attire, is no great disgrace to you, nor any great hurt; but it is a greater disgrace to be considered proud. It signifies an empty, childish mind, to be desirous to be thought fine: it is not only pride, but the pride of a fool, distinct from the pride of those that have but manly wit. And you ought not thus to disgrace yourselves, as to wear the badge of pride and folly, any more than an honest woman should wear the badge and attire of a whore. Moreover, humble apparel is no great temptation to yourselves or others to any sin; but proud and elaborate apparel does signify and stir up a lustful or proud disposition in yourselves; and it tempts those of the same sex to envy and to imitate you, and those of the other sex to lust or wantonness. You spread the devil's nets (even in the churches, and open streets, and meetings) to catch deluded, simple souls. You should rather serve Christ with your apparel, by expressing humility, self-denial, chastity, and sobriety, to draw others to imitate you in good, than to serve the devil, and pride, and lust by it, by drawing men to imitate you in evil.

**Direction 10. Remember what a body it is that you so carefully and elaborately adorn: well is it called by the apostle a "vile body" (Philippians 3:21).**

What a feeble, loathsome lump of dirt it is! What a thing would the pox, or leprosy, or almost any sickness make it appear to be! What loathsome excrements within are covered by all that finery without! Think what it is made of, and what is within it, and what it will turn to! How long it must lie rotting in a dark grave, more loathsome than the common dirt; and then must turn to common earth. And is purple and silk (Luke 16:19), and an elaborate dress, fitting for that body that must shortly have but a winding-sheet, and must lie thus in the grave, and it is to be feared the soul for this pride lie in hell (Luke 16:23, 25)? Is all this cost and elaborateness becoming for one that knows that he is returning to the dust?

**Direction 11. Remember that you have sinful souls that have continual cause of humiliation, and that have need of more care and adorning than your bodies.**

And therefore your apparel should express your humiliation; and show that you take more care for the soul. How vile should that sinner be in his own eyes, who knows what he has done against God! what mercy he has sinned against! what a God he has offended! what a Savior he has slighted! what a Spirit of grace he has resisted! and what a glory he has undervalued and neglected! He that knows what he is, and what he has done, and what he has deserved, and in what a dangerous case his soul yet stands, must necessarily have his soul habituated to a humble frame. Every penitent soul is vile in its own eyes, and does loathe itself for its inward corruptions and actual sins; and he that loathes himself as vile will not be very desirous to have his sinful, corruptible body seem fine, nor by elaborate ornaments to attract the eyes of empty spectators. How often have I seen proud, empty gallants suddenly cast off their finery and gaudy, gay attire, and clothe themselves in plainness and sobriety, as soon as God has but opened their eyes, and humbled their souls for sin, and made them better know themselves, and brought them home by true repentance! so that the next week they have not seemed the same persons: and this was done by mere humiliation without any arguments against their fashions or proud attire. [612] As old Mr. Dod said, when one desired him to preach against long hair: "Preach them once to Christ and true repentance, and they will cut their hair without our preaching against it." As pride would be seen in proud apparel; so humility will appear in a dress like itself, though it does not desire to be seen. Mark 1 Peter 3:3–5, "Let your beauty not be external—the braiding of hair and wearing of gold jewelry or fine clothes (that is, elaborate dressing or adorning the body beyond plain simplicity of attire)—but the inner person of the heart, the lasting beauty of a gentle and tranquil spirit, which is precious in God's sight. For in the same way (that is, with inward holiness and outward plainness) the holy women who hoped in God long ago adorned themselves by being subject to their husbands." Oh that God would print those words upon your hearts! 1 Peter 5:5, "And all of you, clothe yourselves with humility toward one another because God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble." Plainness among Christians is a greater honor than fine clothing (James 2:2–5). 1 Timothy 2:9, 10, "Likewise the women are to dress in suitable apparel, with modesty and self-control. Their adornment must not be with braided hair and gold or pearls or expensive clothing, but with good deeds, as is proper for women who profess reverence for God." I entreat those that are addicted to finery or elaborateness to read Isaiah 3 from verse 16 to the end.

**Direction 12. Do not make too great a matter of your clothing, but use it with such indifference as a thing so indifferent should be used.**

Do not set your hearts upon it. For that is a worse sign than the excess in itself. "Take no thought with what you shall be clothed: but remember how God clothes the lilies of the field" (Matthew 6:28). If you have "food and clothing, be content with that," though it is ever so plain (1 Timothy 6:8).

**Direction 13. Do not be too censorious of others for different fashions of apparel.**

Be as plain and modest yourselves as you can; but lay no greater stress on the fashions of others than there is cause. If they are grossly shameless, disown such fashions and seek to reform them: but to carp at everyone that goes finer than yourselves, or to censure them as proud, because their fashions are not like yours, may be of worse signification than the fashions you find fault with. I have often observed more pride in such censures than I could observe in the fashions which they censured. When you have your eye upon every fashion that is not according to your upbringing, or the custom of your rank or place, and are at once branding such as proud or empty, it shows an arrogant mind, that steps up in the judgment seat, and sentences those that you have nothing to do with, before they are heard, or you know their reasons. Perhaps their fashion was as common among the modest sort where they have lived, as your fashion is among those that you have associated with. Custom and common opinion do put much of the signification upon fashions of apparel.

\* \* \*

I should next have given you special directions about the using of your estates; [613] about your dwellings; about your food and drink; and about your honor or good name. But being reluctant that the book should prove too tedious, I shall refer you to what is said before, against greed, pride, and gluttony, etc.; and what is said before and after, of works of charity and family government.

As to sacred habits, and the different garbs, laws, orders of life, diet, etc. of those called religious orders among the papists, regular and secular, whether and how far such are lawful or sinful, they are handled so fully in the controversies of Protestants and papists, that I shall pass them by. Only remembering the words of the clergy of Ravenna to Carolus Junior, king of France, inter Epist. Hincmari Rhemensis, *Discernendi a plebe vel cæteris sumus, doctrina non veste, conversatione non habitu, mentis puritate non cultu. Docendi enim potius sunt populi, quam ludendi, nec imponendum est eorum oculis, sed mentibus præcepta sunt infundenda.* (English: We are to be distinguished from the common people and the rest by doctrine, not by clothing; by conduct, not by dress; by purity of mind, not by outward attire. For the people are to be taught rather than played with, and nothing is to be imposed on their eyes, but precepts are to be poured into their minds.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **Where this file starts:** the range opens with the fourth question attached to Direction 5 of Part 3 (on apparel); Questions 1–3 and the Direction itself are in the previous file. The "2." in the Answer is Baxter's numbering of a second exception (his first, about necessity, is not numbered); kept as printed.
- **Question; Answer:** Baxter's "_Quest._" and "_Answ._"
- **empty:** Baxter's "vain" throughout (see GLOSSARY.md), including "the proud and vain," "proud or vain," "vain spectators," and "vain gallants." In this context about dress the modern sense of "vain" (conceited) is close to his point, but his word means empty or worthless.
- **riotous; wanton:** kept. "Riotous" means dissolute, given to loose and extravagant living, not rioting in the streets; "wanton" means unrestrained, lewd.
- **ill-signifying:** kept: fashions that signal something bad (pride, lewdness) about the wearer. **signification:** kept (what a fashion signifies).
- **David ... like a madman:** an unmarked allusion to 1 Samuel 21:13, where David feigned madness before Achish of Gath. **Paul's circumcising Timothy** (Acts 16:3) and **"all things to all men, that he might win some"** (compare 1 Corinthians 9:22): unmarked allusions, kept in Baxter's wording.
- **Acts 23:6 (cited only):** Paul's "I am a Pharisee, a son of Pharisees," said before the council.
- **conducive:** Baxter's "conducible." **low esteem:** his "disesteem." **evil speeches:** kept (abusive talk). **carnal:** kept, meaning unspiritual, ruled by the flesh (see GLOSSARY.md).
- **estates:** kept, meaning one's property and means, not only landed estates. **comforting:** Baxter's "comfortable," in the older sense of giving comfort. "So much a year laid out in clothing the naked" and "So much a year in ..." are imagined entries in one's accounts, kept with Baxter's capital S.
- **finery:** Baxter's "bravery" throughout (fine, showy dress, not courage). **elaborateness; elaborate; elaborately:** Baxter's "curiosity," "curious," "curiously," meaning excessive care and nicety in dress, not inquisitiveness. In the parenthesis inside the 1 Peter 3 quotation, "elaborate dressing" is his "curious dressing."
- **gallants:** kept: fashionable, showy men of the town. **prettifying:** Baxter's "neatifying" (making neat and fine). **indeed:** his "yea." **most of the morning:** his "most part of the morning." **dinner time:** in Baxter's day dinner was the main meal around midday.
- **frivolity:** Baxter's "levity" (lightness of behavior, not humor). **singularity:** kept, meaning conspicuous oddness, standing out from others (the fault he treated in Direction 3).
- **reproach:** Baxter's "obloquy." **shabby:** his "sordid," which meant mean or squalid in dress, not morally foul. **considered:** his "esteemed." **manly wit:** kept; "manly" means grown-up, mature (as opposed to childish), and "wit" means understanding.
- **humble apparel:** Baxter's "mean apparel" (cheap, low-quality, not unkind). **simple souls:** his "silly souls," where "silly" meant simple, naive, easily deceived. **feeble:** his "silly" in "a silly, loathsome lump of dirt," where it meant weak, insignificant.
- **whore:** Baxter's blunt word, kept.
- **pox:** kept: in Baxter's day either smallpox or syphilis ("the great pox"); he does not say which. **dark:** his "darksome." **fitting:** his "beseeming." **becoming:** his "comely." **winding-sheet:** kept: the sheet in which a corpse was wrapped for burial.
- **Philippians 3:21 (flagged, Baxter's wording kept):** Baxter quotes the King James "vile body," and his whole argument here rests on the body's vileness ("a feeble, loathsome lump of dirt"). The NET reads "these humble bodies of ours" ("who will transform these humble bodies of ours into the likeness of his glorious body"). "Humble" speaks of lowliness rather than loathsomeness, so the NET wording would weaken the point Baxter draws from the verse; we kept his two words in quotation marks and flag it here.
- **Luke 16:19 (corrected reference):** the source prints "Luke xix. 19," which has nothing about dress; the rich man "dressed in purple and fine linen" is Luke 16:19, the same parable Baxter cites next (Luke 16:23, 25), so we corrected it. Baxter's "purple and silk" is his own phrase, not a quotation. **Luke 16:23, 25 (cited only):** the rich man in torment in Hades.
- **"returning to the dust":** an unmarked allusion to Genesis 3:19.
- **humiliation:** kept in Baxter's sense of being humbled (especially in repentance), not public embarrassment. **must necessarily:** his "must needs." **frame:** kept, meaning disposition or settled state of mind. **actual sins:** a technical term: sins committed in act, as distinct from inward corruption or original sin. **mere humiliation:** "mere" in its older sense of sheer, nothing but.
- **Savior:** Baxter's "Saviour."
- **old Mr. Dod:** probably John Dod, the Puritan minister of the early seventeenth century, known for his short sayings; we have not traced this saying. "Preach them once to Christ" is kept as printed.
- **Mark:** kept in its older sense of "take note of."
- **1 Peter 3:3–5 (NET wording, with Baxter's two parentheses kept; flagged):** Baxter quotes the King James at length and inserts two glosses of his own in parentheses. We have given the NET wording and kept his glosses where they fall. The King James forbids "putting on of apparel," which taken literally would forbid all clothing, and Baxter's first parenthesis explains that it means "curious dressing" beyond plain attire. The NET already reads "fine clothes," so it builds in the sense he argues for; his gloss now reads as a further explanation rather than a needed correction. Other changes: King James "meek and quiet spirit ... of great price" is NET "gentle and tranquil spirit, which is precious in God's sight"; "who trusted in God" is "who hoped in God."
- **1 Peter 5:5 (NET wording; flagged):** Baxter's quotation begins "Yea, all of you be subject one to another, and be clothed with humility." The NET has no "be subject one to another" here: it reads "And all of you, clothe yourselves with humility toward one another." (The King James follows Greek manuscripts that include "be subject"; the NET follows those that do not.) The point Baxter uses, being clothed with humility, is unaffected. The King James "resisteth" is NET "opposes."
- **1 Timothy 2:9–10 (NET wording):** King James "shamefacedness and sobriety" is NET "modesty and self-control"; "broidered hair" is "braided hair"; "costly array" is "expensive clothing"; Baxter's parenthesis "(which becometh women professing godliness)" is part of the King James text, which the NET renders "as is proper for women who profess reverence for God." The sense he leans on (modest rather than costly dress) is unchanged.
- **James 2:2–5; Isaiah 3:16 to the end (cited only):** the rich man in fine clothes favored in the assembly over the poor man; and the judgment on the proud "daughters of Zion" with their finery.
- **indifference; indifferent:** Baxter's "indifferency" and "indifferent." The first means unconcern; the second is the technical sense, a thing neither commanded nor forbidden in itself (*adiaphora*). He plays on the two senses.
- **Matthew 6:28 (Baxter's paraphrase kept):** "Take no thought wherewith ye shall be clothed: but remember how God clothes the lilies of the field" blends the King James "take no thought ... wherewithal shall we be clothed" (Matthew 6:31; compare 6:25) with the lilies of verse 28, and is not an exact quotation, so we kept his wording, modernized ("Take no thought" means "do not be anxious"). The NET has "Why do you worry about clothing? Think about how the flowers of the field grow" (6:28) and "do not worry about ... what you will wear" (6:25).
- **1 Timothy 6:8 (Baxter's wording kept; flagged):** Baxter quotes the King James "having food and raiment let us be therewith content," adapted to second person. The NET reads "But if we have food and shelter, we will be satisfied with that." Since Baxter's point is about clothing, the NET's "shelter" would remove the very thing he cites the verse for; the Greek word means a covering and can be taken of either clothing or shelter. We kept his wording ("food and clothing") and flag the difference here. **ever so plain:** his "never so plain," the older idiom with the same meaning.
- **shameless:** Baxter's "impudent." **carp at:** kept (find petty fault with). **often:** his "oft." **upbringing:** his "breeding." **at once:** his "presently." **associated with:** his "conversed with," meaning lived among, not only talked with.
- **The closing paragraphs:** after Direction 13 the source has a row of asterisks and then two paragraphs closing Part 3 and, as far as we can tell from this range, the chapter's specific directions. We render the asterisks as a centered break.
- **food:** Baxter's "meat," meaning food in general. **reluctant:** his "loth." **greed:** his "covetousness" (see GLOSSARY.md). **etc.:** his "&c." **handled so fully:** his "handled so largely," meaning at length.
- **sacred habits:** distinctive clerical and monastic dress. **religious orders ... regular and secular:** "regular" clergy live under a rule (monks, friars); "secular" clergy (parish priests) do not. **papists:** Baxter's word for Roman Catholics; his polemic, kept as he wrote it.
- **Latin quotation:** kept exactly as printed, with a draft English translation in parentheses (prepared for this project; please have a Latinist check it). "Ludendi" (to be played with, fooled) is rendered loosely. "inter Epist. Hincmari Rhemensis" means "among the letters of Hincmar of Reims"; kept as printed. "Carolus Junior" is Latin for Charles the Younger; we have not identified which Frankish king Baxter means. The words "discernendi a plebe ... doctrina non veste, conversatione non habitu, mentis puritate non cultu" are, as far as we know, also found in a fifth-century papal letter (commonly attributed to Pope Celestine I) on clerical dress; we have not checked Baxter's source or that attribution.
- **Note 611:** the list of Scripture references is converted to modern form and kept as printed; we have not checked how each verse bears on the point.
- **Note 612:** "Zenocrates" is kept as printed; probably Xenocrates, the head of Plato's Academy. **brought:** Baxter's "reduced," meaning brought down to. **light of nature:** reason and conscience without revelation. The Stoics, Cynics, Academics (Platonists), and Pythagoreans were ancient schools of philosophy.
- **Note 613:** "my Letter to Mr. Gouge" is kept as Baxter gives it. Mr. Gouge is probably Thomas Gouge, the nonconformist minister known for his charity; we have not identified the letter.
