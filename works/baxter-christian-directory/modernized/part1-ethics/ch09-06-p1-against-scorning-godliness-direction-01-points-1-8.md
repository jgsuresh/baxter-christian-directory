# Chapter 9: Title 6, against scorning godliness: the explanation, and Direction 1, points 1-8

**Title 6. Directions against profane deriding, scorning, or opposing godliness.**

*In the margin: The explanation.*

To forestall the replies or excuses of the scorner, I must here tell you, 1. That by godliness I mean nothing but an entire devotedness to God and living to him: the doctrine and practice which are agreeable to the holy Scripture. I mean no fancies of mistaken men, nor the private opinions of any sect; but the practice of Christianity itself.

2. And yet I must tell you that it is the common practice of these scorners to fasten more upon the concrete than the abstract, the person than the bare doctrine, and to oppose godly persons as such, when yet they say that they do not oppose godliness. The reasons for this are these: (1.) Because they dare to be bolder with the person than with the rule and doctrine of God himself. If they scorn at the Bible, or at godliness directly, as such, they would so openly scorn at God himself that the world would cry shame on them, and conscience would worry them: but as godliness is in such a neighbor, or such a preacher, or such a man, so they think they may reverence it less, and that what they do is against the person and not the thing.

(2.) In men they have something else to allege to be the matter of their scorn. Godliness in men is latent, invisible, unprovable as to the sincerity of it, and obscure as to the exercise. If he that scorns a godly man says, He is not godly, but a hypocrite; in this world there is no perfect vindication to be had against such a slander; but the probable evidence of profession and a godly life is all that can be brought. But godliness, as it is in the Scripture, lies open to the view of all, and cannot be denied there, but by denying the Scriptures themselves.

(3.) Godliness as in the rule of holy Scripture is perfect, without any blemish that may give a scorner a pretext; but godliness in men is very imperfect, and mixed with sins, with faults which the world may often discern, and the godly themselves are the most ready to confess; and therefore in them a scorner may find some plausible pretext. And when he derides these professors of godliness as being all hypocrites, he will not give instances from their virtues, but from their faults; as in Noah's drunkenness, and Lot's incest, and David's adultery and murder, and Peter's denying Christ; yet so as the dart shall be cast at piety itself; and the conclusion shall not be to drive men from drunkenness, adultery, or any sin, but from serious godliness itself.

(4.) Godliness as in the rule is to them a more unobserved, dormant thing, and does not so much annoy them; for they can shut their Bibles, or make nothing of it, but as a few good words; but godliness in the godly, existing in their teachers and neighbors, is more discernible to them, and more active, and more troublesome to them, and so more hated by them. In a dead letter, or dead saint, that does not trouble them, they can commend it; but in the living they are bothered by it; and the nearer it is to them, the more they are exasperated against it. The word is the seed of godliness; which least offends them, till it springs up and brings forth the fruit which condemns their wicked lives.

3. And as opposers and scorners usually strike at godliness through the person and his faults, so they are accustomed to strike at the particular parts of God's worship through some modes or circumstances, or imperfections of men in the performance. It is not preaching or praying that they scorn, if you believe them, but this or that manner or imperfection in preaching and praying. But the drift of all is not to help any man to do it better, but to make them odious that are most serious in doing it at all, and thereby to persuade men that it is a needless thing. [549]

4. Note also that it is not the image or dead part of religion that these men are most offended at and oppose; but it is the life, and zeal, and diligence of the godly. So that if they do not differ from themselves in profession about any doctrine or ceremony, yet they hate and scorn them for doing seriously the same which they themselves hypocritically profess.

5. Lastly, note also that this is not a difference of one sect, or party, or church against another, upon differing opinions; but it is that which is among all parties within themselves, when there is anything of serious religion to be found. Even among the papists there are some spiritual, serious, holy persons, who are derided and opposed by the profane that are of their own church. Yes, among the heathens, Seneca and others tell us that strictness in moral virtue was made the scorn of the rude and pleasure-loving sort of men. But though the quarrel is only that which was taken up from the beginning between the woman's and the serpent's seed, yet in all countries where church differences cause contention, this serpentine enmity with serpentine subtlety creeps in and takes advantage of them, and takes up the nicknames, or sharper weapons, which differing Christians form against each other, to strike at the heart of Christianity itself. [550]

**Direction 1. For the cure of those that are already infected with so heinous a sin, the chief direction is to understand the greatness of it, and the miserable consequences: as follows.**

1. Consider what it is that you deride. Do you know against what you open your mouth? 1. You deride or oppose men for loving God with all their heart, and soul, and might: and do you not confess that this is the duty of all men living? and that he is not worthy to be called a Christian that does not love God above all? You cannot deny this. And yet will you oppose it? Do not deny it; for this is the very thing that you oppose; either men's loving God, or showing their love to him. If you only loved him as much as they, you would seek and serve him as diligently as they. Do you not know this yourself, that if you loved him with all your heart, and soul, and strength, you would seek, and serve, and obey him with all your heart, and soul, and strength? If the godly do more than this, deride them and do not spare them. If they love God, and serve him with more than all the heart, and soul, and might, then call them righteous overmuch. If you know anyone that loves God or serves him more than he deserves, blame and oppose that man and do not spare him. You know that what you love most, you are diligent yourself in seeking and remembering. You labor for money because you love it: and they labor in seeking and serving God because they love him: and is it a work for any but a devil, to oppose or scorn men for; for loving or showing their love to God?

2. You deride men for delighting in that which is most delightful: for delighting in high and heavenly knowledge, and in a holy state of soul and life; and for delighting in the law of God, and meditating in it day and night (Psalm 1:2); and for delighting in holy prayer, and the praises of their Maker; and for delighting in the forethoughts and mention of eternal joys, and making their calling and election sure. What is it but the exercise of these holy desires and delights which you deride? And would you not be as serious in religion and holiness as they, if you had as much of these delights as they? Can you sit at your pots, or follow your game or amusements, or talk of empty things many hours together, because you delight in them? and yet do you deride those that pray or hear God's word opened to them many hours, because it is their delight? O poor souls! how quickly and how terribly will God acquaint you whether their delights or yours were the more rational and just! and whether their work or yours was fitter to be derided!

3. You scorn men for paying only what they owe to the God that created and redeemed them. Are they not his own? and did he not give them all their parts and powers? and are not all their abilities and possessions his? What do they have which they did not receive from him? And is this your justice and honesty, to deride men for offering to pay their debts, and to give God his own? If you know anyone that gives him more than he owes him, deride that superstitious, over-righteous man, and do not spare him. But if men should not be derided for paying their debts to you, do not deride men for paying their debt to God, and giving him that which is his own. As we must give to Caesar that which is Caesar's, so we must give that to God also which is God's.

4. You deride servants for diligently obeying their highest Master; and for diligently doing the greatest, best, and most needful work in all the world. And is this a good example for your own servants? Surely if a man should be mocked for serving God, he should be mocked more for serving such a one as you. Do you know where we may find a better master, whom we may serve with better encouragement than God? He has made us his stewards, and trusted us with his goods, and do you scorn us for being faithful in our stewardship? You deride his subjects for obeying the King of all the world; and is this a good example to the king's subjects? should it be a matter of scorn to obey the king? or do you think that God's authority is less? or obedience to him less commendable?

5. No, you deride men for doing only some part of their duty, and discharging only a little of their debt. For the holiest man whom you deride for doing too much does less than what he ought to do. You know that the best of men love God and serve him less than he deserves; and that the most careful come short of the perfect keeping of his laws; and yet will you scorn men for doing so much, when they know, and you confess, that they do too little? Could they do all, they would do only their duty (Luke 17:10).

6. You scorn men because they will not set up themselves, their own wit and will, against their Maker. God has commanded them to "make every effort to be sure of their calling and election" (2 Peter 1:10); and to "strive to enter in at the narrow gate" (Matthew 7:13); and "day and night to meditate on his law" (Psalm 1:2); and to love him with all their heart and might; and to "constantly pray" (1 Thessalonians 5:17). And you deride men for obeying these commands! Why, what would you have us do, man? should we tell God that we are wiser than he? and that he shall not have his will, but we will have our own? and that we know a better way than he has appointed us? and that he is mistaken, and would deceive us by his laws? Would you have men thus be voluntarily mad, and profess themselves open rebels against God?

7. You scorn men because they trust him that is truth and goodness itself. We cannot imagine that he can deceive us by his word, or that he makes any law for us that is not good, or requires any duty of us that shall be to our hurt, or that we shall be losers by. [551] And therefore we resolve to obey him as carefully as we can, because we are confident that goodness itself will not abuse us, and truth itself will not deceive us: and is this a matter to be scorned for? should not children trust their father?

8. You deride men for not sinning against their certain knowledge and experience. They know that a holy life is best, though you do not; they know the reasonableness of it; they know the sweetness of it; they know the necessity of it. [552] And must they renounce their own understandings? must they be ignorant because you are ignorant? and put out their eyes because you are blind? Is it a crime for men to be wiser than you? and that in the matters of God and their salvation? They have tried what a holy life is, and so have not you. They have tried what a life of faith and obedience is: and must they renounce their own experience? Must they that have tasted it say honey is bitter, because you that never tasted it say so? Alas, what unreasonable men we have to deal with!

Laert. in Socrat. (Socrates, while speaking, often used to toss his fingers about, the vehemence of his speech driving him to it, so that he was laughed at by most people and held in contempt: all of which, however, he bore with an even mind. Laertius, in his *Socrates*. "Toss his fingers about" is rendered loosely.)

[550] Si quis vero eorum mitior, et veritati aliquatenus propior, videretur, in hunc quasi Britanniæ subversorem omnia odia telaque sine respectu contorquebantur, et omnia quæ displicuerint, Deoque placuerint, æquali saltem lance pendebantur, si non gratiora fuissent displicentia. Gildas. (But if any of them seemed milder, and somewhat nearer to the truth, all hatreds and darts were hurled at him without regard, as if he were the overthrower of Britain; and all things that displeased [men] and pleased God were weighed at least in an equal balance, if the things that displeased [God] were not the more welcome. Gildas.) Quod autem quædam de illo inhonesta et maligna jactantur, nolo mireris: cum scias hoc esse opus semper diaboli, ut servos Dei mendacio laceret, et opinionibus falsis gloriosum nomen infamet; ut qui conscientiæ suæ luce clarescunt, alienis rumoribus sordidentur. Cyprian de Cornel. Epist. ad Antonian. (But I would not have you wonder that certain dishonorable and malicious things are bandied about concerning him: since you know that this is always the work of the devil, to tear the servants of God with lies, and to defame a glorious name with false opinions; so that those who shine bright in the light of their own conscience are soiled by the rumors of others. Cyprian, on Cornelius, *Letter to Antonianus*.) Hæc et nos risimus aliquando. Tertul. (We too once laughed at these things. Tertullian.)

[551] Malignity so blinds the understanding that it makes men ascribe all the evil that befalls them to that which is the only way to happiness: every bad success that the heathen Romans had, they imputed to the Christians: says Paul. Diaconus, book 3, when Radagusus the Goth invaded the Romans: Pavor infinitus Romam invadit; declamatur a cunctis, se hæc ideo perpeti, quod neglecta fuerunt magnorum sacra Deorum: magnis querelis ubique agere: et continuo de repetendis sacris celebrandisque tractatur: fere in tota urbe blasphemiæ ad nomen Christi, tanquam lues aliqua probris ingravantur, conducuntur a Romanis adversus Radagusum duo Pagani duces, &c. (Boundless dread invades Rome; it is loudly declared by all that they suffer these things because the rites of the great gods had been neglected: with great complaints everywhere they press it: and at once there is discussion of restoring and celebrating the rites: throughout almost the whole city blasphemies against the name of Christ, like some plague, are made heavier with reproaches; two pagan generals are hired by the Romans against Radagusus, etc. The clause "magnis querelis ubique agere" is rendered loosely.)

[552] Says Chrysostom, As those that run or act in public games, besides the prize which they hope for, much increase their strength and health by preparing their bodies for it: so besides the hopes of heaven, it is no small comfort and advantage here on the way which Christians get by their holy lives.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **Title:** "Tit." in the source is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," his word for each of the six headed divisions of Chapter 9 ("Directions for the Government of the Tongue"). This file opens Title 6.
- **The Title 6 heading** stands at source lines 45720-45721, the start of this file's range.
- **2 Peter 1:10:** NET wording ("make every effort to be sure of your calling and election"), with "your" changed to "their" to fit Baxter's indirect command. The King James "give diligence to make your calling and election sure" is closer to Baxter's "give all diligence ... make ... sure." In point 2, "making their calling and election sure" is Baxter's unmarked echo of the same verse, kept in his wording.
- **"strive to enter in at the narrow gate" (Matthew 7:13):** Baxter's quotation blends two verses: "strive to enter in at the strait gate" is the King James of Luke 13:24 (which has "strait gate" there), while Matthew 7:13 reads "Enter ye in at the strait gate." We keep his blended wording, modernized ("strait" to "narrow"), with his reference. The NET has "Enter through the narrow gate" at Matthew 7:13 and "Exert every effort to enter through the narrow door" at Luke 13:24. Baxter leans on "strive," which only the Luke verse has.
- **Psalm 1:2:** both uses are Baxter's adaptations into his own sentences, so his wording is kept. The NET reads "Instead he finds pleasure in obeying the Lord's commands; he meditates on his commands day and night." Where the King James and Baxter speak of delighting in and meditating on "the law," the NET has "obeying the Lord's commands" and "his commands"; Baxter's "delighting in the law of God" (point 2) is the King James sense, and the NET's "finds pleasure in obeying" shifts the delight toward obedience.
- **1 Thessalonians 5:17:** NET "constantly pray" for Baxter's King James "pray continually" (KJV "Pray without ceasing"). Same sense.
- **Luke 17:10** (point 5): reference only; the NET reads "we have only done what was our duty."
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): loving God "with all their heart, and soul, and might" (Deuteronomy 6:5; Mark 12:30); "righteous overmuch" (Ecclesiastes 7:16, King James "Be not righteous over much"; NET "do not be excessively righteous"); "What do they have which they did not receive from him?" (1 Corinthians 4:7; NET "What do you have that you did not receive?"); giving to Caesar what is Caesar's (Matthew 22:21; NET "give to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's"); "the woman's and the serpent's seed" (Genesis 3:15; NET "your offspring and her offspring," where "seed" is the King James word); faithful stewards (1 Corinthians 4:2; Luke 12:42); the cases of Noah, Lot, David, and Peter (Genesis 9:21; 19:33-36; 2 Samuel 11; Matthew 26:69-75).
- **Word swaps:** forestall (Baxter's "prevent," in the sense of "go before"); allege (his "pretend"); vindication (his "justification," in the sense of clearing one's name); slander (his "calumny"); pretext (his "pretence"); most ready (his "forwardest"); give instances from (his "instance in"); bothered (his "molested"); existing (his "existent"); are accustomed to (his "use to"); pleasure-loving (his "sensual"); consequences (his "consequents"); delightful (his "delectable"); empty things (his "vanity"); amusements (his "sports"); most needful (his "needfullest"); most careful (his "carefullest"); No (his "Nay," point 5); Yes (his "Yea," point 5 of the explanation).
- **"if they do not differ from themselves in profession"** (point 4 of the explanation): kept as Baxter wrote it; "themselves" seems to mean the scorners, so the sense is probably "if the godly do not differ from the scorners in profession."
- **professors of godliness:** those who profess godliness, not teachers.
- **sit at your pots** (point 2): drinking pots; Baxter means sitting at drink in the alehouse.
- **abuse us** (point 7): in the older sense of ill-using or deceiving.
- **papists:** Baxter's word for Roman Catholics. Here he grants that there are serious, holy persons among them who are mocked by the profane of their own church.
- **Seneca and others** (point 5 of the explanation): Baxter's claim; we have not traced the passage he has in mind.
- **"to oppose or scorn men for; for loving"** (Direction 1, point 1): the doubled "for; for" is in the source; kept.
- **"Could they do all, they would do only their duty"** (point 5): Baxter's "they did but their duty," a conditional in older English.
- **"If you only loved him"** (point 1): Baxter's "If thou didst but love him."
- **The two numberings:** Baxter numbers the explanation 1-5 with sub-reasons (1.)-(4.), then numbers the considerations under Direction 1 (point 1 contains a further "1." that has no "2."). All are kept as printed. This file ends after point 8; point 9 begins the next file.
- **Note 549:** "Laert." is probably Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of the Philosophers*, the life of Socrates; we have not checked the passage.
- **Note 550:** Gildas, the British writer on the ruin of Britain; Cyprian's letter to Antonianus defending Cornelius, bishop of Rome; and a short sentence from Tertullian. These identifications are probable; the passages have not been checked.
- **Note 551:** Baxter cites "Paul. Diaconus, lib. 3" (Paul the Deacon). "Radagusus" is probably Radagaisus, the Gothic leader who invaded Italy in the early fifth century. The Latin closely resembles Orosius's account of the same invasion, which Paul the Deacon may be drawing on; we have not confirmed this.
- **Note 552:** Chrysostom is quoted in English only, without a reference; we have not traced the passage.
