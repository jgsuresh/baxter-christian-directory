# Chapter 8: Part 4, Title 2, questions to the drunkard (16–20), "willing but not able" answered (questions 1–12), and Direction 1 against tippling

*This file begins partway through Baxter's series of questions put to the drunkard (Part 4, Title 2), at question 16; the earlier questions are in the previous file.*

*Question 16.* How does it come to pass that your very pride does not cure your drunkenness? [447] Pride is so natural and deep-rooted a sin, that I dare say you have not overcome it, if you have not overcome your indulgence in physical pleasure. And is your credit worth no more to you? will you for a cup of drink be made the talk of the country, the scorn of the town, the sport and laughingstock of boys, and the pity of sober persons? If you are a great man among them, and they dare not say it to your face, and you do not hear what they say of you, yet in private they make bold with your name, to talk of you as of a filthy beast. Can you think that sober men do honor you? What honor may incidentally be due to you from your place is another matter; but you take a course to keep them from honoring you for your worth, and do your worst to bring your rank and place into contempt. It is said that in Spain a drunkard is not allowed as a witness against any man: and surely he is not a credible person. Regard your reputation if you do not care for your soul.

*Question 17.* Do you not love the flesh itself which you so much pamper? If you do, why will you drown it, and choke it up with phlegm and filth? Ask physicians whether drunkenness is wholesome. Mark how many drunkards live to be old: *Ennius podagricus* is a proverb. The sickness is longer than the sweetness of your cup. If you do not fear hell, fear consumption, gout, or dropsy.

*Question 18.* Why should you not take more pleasure in the company of your family, and in the company of people fearing God, who worship him in truth of heart, and will do their best to help to save you? Can you give any reason for it, why such company should not be more pleasant to you than your drinking companions? and why it should not be pleasanter to talk of the way to heaven, and the pardon of sin, and the love of Christ, and of eternal happiness, than to chatter a lot of idle nonsense in an alehouse? There is no reason for it but your filthy mind, that is suited to emptiness and sin, and unsuited to all that is wise and holy.

*Question 19.* What if you should die in a drunken fit? Would you not yourself take your case to be desperate or dangerous? Why, it may be so for all you know; it has been the case of many a one. But if it is not so, yet to die a drunkard is as certain damnation as to die in drunkenness. If the guilt of the sin is on you, it is all one when it was committed, whether lately or long ago; for unpardoned sin is most sure damnation; and it is certainly unpardoned till it is truly repented of; and it is not repented of if it is not forsaken: and then consider how you will review these days, and what thoughts you will then have of your cups and company!

*Question 20.* Are you willing to part with your sin, or are you not? Speak, man; are you willing? If you are not willing, bear witness against yourself that you do not repent of it, and that you are not forgiven it; and therefore that you are at present a slave of the devil, and if you die so, as sure to be damned as you are alive. Bear witness that you were not kept from grace, and consequently from heaven, against your will, but by your willful refusal of it; and that it was not because you could not be saved that you go to hell, but because you would not. Surely even now you cannot have the face to deny any of this, if you confess that you are not willing to amend. Have your will in sin, if God's will must be violated, which offered you mercy, and commanded you to accept it; but be sure that God will have his will in punishing you.

But I suppose you will say that you are willing to amend and leave your sin, but you cannot do it because flesh is frail, and company is tempting, and God does not give you grace; willing you are, but yet unable. But stay a little! God will not let you carry it off so, and smooth over your wickedness with a lie. Your meaning, if you speak out, is not that you are willing, at once and heartily willing, to forsake your sin, but only that you would be willing, if the drink and the devil did not tempt you. And so you will be willing to love God and be saved, when nothing shall tempt you to the contrary! And would you thank your wife for such a willingness to forsake adultery, when nobody will tempt her to it? or your servant to do your work, when he has nothing to tempt him to idleness or neglect? Judge by this what thanks you deserve from God for such a willingness. But do not trifle with God, and do not mock your conscience, but speak to the question, Are you willing to give up your company and tippling, from this day forward, or are you not? Take heed what you say. If you say, No, God may say, No, to all your cries for mercy in the day of your misery and distress; but if you still say that you are willing, but not able, I will convict you of your falsehood.

*Question 1.* Tell me then, what force is used to make you sin against your will? Were you carried to the alehouse, or did you go yourself? Were you gagged and dosed? Was it poured down your throat by violence; or did you take the cup and pour it down yourself? Who was the man that held open your mouth and poured it in? Indeed, if it had been so, it would not have been your sin; for no will, no sin. Or did they set a sword or pistol to your breast, and so force you to it? If they had, that would not have proved you unwilling, but only that they forced you to be willing; and their force is no excuse: for God threatened hell, and you should have feared that most.

*Question 2.* Did you love the drink, or loathe it when you were drinking it? Did you love it against your will, when love and willingness are all one?

*Question 3.* Will you refrain the next time until you are carried to it, and until it is forcibly poured down with a horn? If not, confess it is your will.

*Question 4.* Could you not refrain if the judge or the king stood by? And can you not refrain when God stands by? If you will, you can.

*Question 5.* Could you not refrain if you were sure to be put to death for it? if the law hanged all drunkards, and the hangman were at your back? Surely you could. And can you not then refrain if you will, when God has made it worse than hanging, and when death is coming to fetch you to execution?

*Question 6.* Could you not refrain from it in sickness, if your physician required it, and told you, if you drink, it will be your death? I do not doubt that you could: if not, you are very unworthy to live, that cannot deny yourself a cup of drink for the saving of your life. And you are as unworthy to be saved, if you will not do that to save your soul which you would do to save your present life.

*Question 7.* Indeed, could you not refrain if it were to save the life of your wife, or child, or friend, or neighbor? If you knew that refraining from your forbidden cup would save the life of any one of them, could you not, indeed, would you not do it? If not, you tell the world what a husband, what a father, what a friend, and what a neighbor you are, that would not refrain from a cup of drink to save a friend or neighbor's life. I should think you an unworthy friend if you would not do that much at your friend's request, though there were no such necessity laid upon it. If this is so, I will never take a drunkard for my friend; for he would not refrain from a cup of drink for my sake, no, not if it were to save my life. If you say, God forbid, I would do more than that, why then did you say, You cannot refrain? Mark how your tongue reproves your falsehood. And can you not do that for your own soul which you could do for the life, or at the request, of a friend or neighbor?

*Question 8.* Could you not refrain if it were to get a lordship or a kingdom? indeed, to save your own estate, if it were all in danger, and this would save it? I do not doubt that you could. Why then do you say you cannot do it?

*Question 9.* If you were certain that you were to die tomorrow, would you be drunk tonight? Or if you were sure to die within this week or month, would you be drunk before then? I do not believe you would: fear would so long shut your mouth. You see then that you can refrain if you were only willing, and were only awakened out of your stupidity and folly.

*Question 10.* What if you were sure that there were an ounce of arsenic or other such poison in the cup? could you not then refrain from it? Yes, no doubt of it: it is plain therefore that you speak falsely when you say that you cannot. And is not God's wrath and curse in your cup much worse than poison?

*Question 11.* What if you saw the devil standing by you and offering you the cup, and persuading you to drink it, could you not then refrain? Yes, no doubt of it: and is he not as certainly there tempting you as if you saw him? Well, the matter is proved against you to your own conscience, that if you will refrain, you can.

*Question 12.* But yet if you cannot, consider whether you can better bear the pains of hell? For God is not in jest with you in his threatenings. If your thirst is harder to bear than hell, then choose that which is easiest to you: but remember hereafter that you had your choice.

Yet, are you willing to let go your sin? (for I am sure you are able so far as you are willing). I will take your case to be as it is; that is, that you have some half, ineffectual willingness, or lazy wish which will not conquer a temptation; and that you are sometimes in a little better mood than at other times, and that you love your sin, and therefore would not leave it if you could choose, but you do not love hell, and therefore have some thoughts of parting with your cups against your will, for fear of punishment. These wishes and purposes will never save you: it must be a renewed nature, loving God, and hating the sin, that must make you capable of salvation. But yet in the meantime it is necessary that you refrain from your sin, though it is only through fear; for you cannot expect otherwise that the Holy Spirit should renew your nature. Therefore I will give you directions how to refrain from your sin most surely and easily, if you are only willing, and also to promote your willingness itself with the performance.

**Practical Directions against Tippling and Drunkenness.**

**Direction 1. Write over your bed and your bedroom door, where you may read it every morning before you go out, some text of holy Scripture that is fit to be your reminder: as 1 Corinthians 6:10, "... drunkards ... will not inherit the kingdom of God"; and Romans 8:13, "if you live according to the flesh, you will die ... but if by the Spirit you put to death the deeds of the body, you will live": and read it before you go out of your doors.**

## Baxter's notes

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). All NET wording was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Labels:** this file sits in Part 4 of Chapter 8, Title 2 (against drunkenness). "Tit." in the source is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," a heading or division within a Part. Baxter's questions run to 20 here; after "willing, but not able" he starts a new series of questions at 1 (rendered *Question 1.* to *Question 12.*), which answers that excuse. The numbering is his.
- **Direction 1:** the whole Direction is a single sentence in the source, so all of it is set in bold. The Practical Directions continue in the next file with Direction 2.
- **1 Corinthians 6:10:** the NET verse is a list ("thieves, the greedy, drunkards, the verbally abusive, and swindlers will not inherit the kingdom of God"); Baxter quotes only the part about drunkards, so the omissions are marked "...". The sense he uses is unchanged.
- **Romans 8:13 (flag):** Baxter's King James has "mortify the deeds of the body"; the NET has "put to death." The sense is the same (mortify means put to death), but "mortify" is Baxter's own recurring term elsewhere in the *Directory*, and the link to it is lost in the NET wording. The NET puts the first clause in parentheses, "(for if you live according to the flesh, you will die)"; we dropped the parentheses and "for" and marked the gap with "...". Baxter's King James mixes "ye" and "you"; both become "you."
- **1 Thessalonians 5:7** (note 447): NET wording. The NET verse begins "For those who sleep, sleep at night, and"; Baxter quotes only the second half. The King James "They that are drunken, are drunken in the night" becomes "those who get drunk are drunk at night." No change to the sense he uses.
- ***Ennius podagricus*** (question 17): Latin, "Ennius the gouty," kept as printed. Ennius was an early Roman poet; he probably suffered from gout by ancient report (we have not confirmed this), and the tag apparently uses him as the type of a drinker paying for it in his body. We have not traced the source of the proverb.
- **Spain and drunken witnesses** (question 16): Baxter's report ("it is said"); we have not checked it.
- **Unmarked allusions:** "flesh is frail" (compare Matthew 26:41, "the flesh is weak"); "a slave of the devil" (compare John 8:34; 2 Timothy 2:26). Kept in Baxter's wording.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): sensuality → indulgence in physical pleasure; laughing-game → laughingstock; accidentally → incidentally (in his sense, "by circumstance of your place, not for your worth"); pot companions → drinking companions; prate a deal of → chatter a lot of (as in the earlier files of this Title); vanity → emptiness; suitable / unsuitable → suited / unsuited; for aught thou knowest → for all you know; bethink thee → consider; tendered → offered; presently → at once; dally → trifle; give over → give up; convince → convict (prove guilty; GLOSSARY.md has convince = prove wrong); forbear / forbearing → refrain / refraining (with "from" where an object follows); drenched → dosed ("drench" then meant to force a dose of medicine down an animal's throat, as the next sentence shows, not to soak); Yea → Indeed; ere → before; uneffectual → ineffectual; withal → also; Holy Ghost → Holy Spirit; chamber door → bedroom door; memorandum → reminder; Take thy will → Have your will; "God will not so let thee carry it" → "God will not let you carry it off so" ("carry it" meaning win the point or get away with it).
- **Words kept:** credit (reputation, standing); sport (object of fun); make bold with (take liberties with); wholesome; tippling (habitual drinking short of drunkenness; see the earlier files of this Title); estate (property); lordship (a lord's domain or title); "a horn" (question 3: a drenching horn, a horn used to pour liquid medicine down an animal's throat); consumption (a wasting disease, usually tuberculosis) and dropsy (swelling from fluid, now called edema) — Baxter's medical claims are of his time.
- **"No, God may say, No"** (end of question 20): the source has "If thou say, No, God may say, Nay"; both are rendered "No."
- **Question 1:** "Indeed, if it had been so" renders Baxter's "Nay, if it had been thus," where "Nay" means "indeed, rather" (not a denial).
- **Question 7:** "could you not, indeed, would you not" renders "couldst thou not, nay, wouldst thou not," where "nay" means "or rather."
