# Chapter 3: Grand Direction X (part 2 of 6)

*This section begins partway through Grand Direction 10, at Direction 4 (the Grand Direction's heading and Directions 1-3 are in the previous file), and ends after Direction 15; the next file continues the list.*

**Direction 4. Do not set duties of piety, justice, or charity against each other, as if they had an enmity to each other; but take them as inseparable, as God has made them.**

Do not think to offer God a sacrifice of injury, bribery, fraud, oppression, or any uncharitable work. And do not claim the benefit of men, or the safety of societies or kingdoms, for impiety against the Lord. [111]

**Direction 5. Acquaint yourselves with all the talents which you receive from God, and what is the use to which they should be put.**

Keep thus a just account of your receivings, and what goods of your Master's are put into your hands. And make it a principal part of your study to know what everything in your hand is good for to your Master's use; and how it is that he would have you use it.

**Direction 6. Keep an account of your expenses; at least, of all your most considerable talents; and bring yourselves daily or frequently to a reckoning, what good you have done, or made an effort to do.**

Every day is given you for some good work. Keep therefore accounts of every day (I mean, in your conscience, not in papers). Every mercy must be used to some good: call yourselves therefore to account for every mercy, what you have done with it for your Master's use. And do not think hours and minutes, and little mercies, may be passed without coming into the account. The servant who thinks he may do what he likes with shillings and pence, and that he is only to lay out greater sums for his master's use, and lesser for his own, will prove unfaithful, and come short in his accounts. Smaller sums than pounds must be in our reckonings.

**Direction 7. Take special heed that the common thief, your carnal self, either personal or in your relations, does not rob God of his expected due, and devour what he requires.**

It is not for nothing that God calls for the firstfruits. "Honor the Lord from your wealth and from the firstfruits of all your crops; then your barns will be filled completely, and your vats will overflow with new wine" (Proverbs 3:9-10). So Exodus 23:16, 19; 34:22, 26; Leviticus 2:12, 14; Nehemiah 10:35; Ezekiel 20:40; 44:30; 48:14. For if carnal self might first be served, its devouring greediness would leave God nothing. Though he who has godliness with contentment has enough, if he has only food and clothing, yet there will be but enough for themselves and their children, where men have many hundreds or thousands a year, if once it falls into this gulf. And indeed, as he who begins with God has the promise of his bountiful supplies, so he whose flesh must first be served catches such a dropsical thirst for more, that all will but serve it: and the devil contrives such necessities for these men, and such uses for all they have, that they have no more to spare than poorer men; and they can allow God no more than the leavings of the flesh, and what it can spare, which commonly is next to nothing. Indeed though holy uses in particular were satisfied with firstfruits and limited parts, God must have all, and the flesh (inordinately or finally) have none. Every penny which is laid out upon yourselves, and children, and friends, must be done as by God's own appointment, and to serve and please him. Watch closely, or else this thievish carnal self will leave God nothing.

**Direction 8. Prefer greater duties (_cæteris paribus_) before lesser; and labor to understand which is the greater, and to be preferred.**

Not that any real duty is to be neglected: but we call that by the name of duty which is materially good, and a duty in its season; but formally, indeed, it is no duty at all, when it cannot be done without the omission of a greater. As for a minister to be praying with his family, or comforting one afflicted soul, when he should be preaching publicly, is to do that which is a duty in its season, but at that time is his sin. It is an unfaithful servant who is doing some little chore, when he should be saving a beast from drowning, or the house from burning, or doing the greater part of his work.

**Direction 9. Prudence is exceedingly necessary in doing good, that you may discern good from evil, discerning the season, and measure, and manner, and among diverse duties, which must be preferred.**

Therefore labor much for wisdom, and if you lack it yourself, be sure to make use of theirs who have it, and ask their counsel in every great and difficult case. Zeal without judgment has not only entangled souls in many heinous sins, but has ruined churches and kingdoms, and under pretense of exceeding others in doing good, it makes men the greatest instruments of evil. There is scarcely a sin so great and odious, but ignorant zeal will make men do it as a good work. Christ told his apostles that those who killed them would think they did God service. And Paul bore record to the murderous, persecuting Jews, "that they are zealous for God, but their zeal is not in line with the truth" (Romans 10:2). The papists' murders of Christians under the name of heretics has recorded it to the world, in the blood of many hundred thousands, how ignorant, carnal zeal will do good, and what sacrifice it will offer up to God. [112]

**Direction 10. In doing good, prefer the souls of men before the body, _cæteris paribus_. To convert a sinner from the error of his way is to save a soul from death, and to cover a multitude of sins (James 5:20).**

And this is greater than to give a man an alms. As cruelty to souls is the most heinous cruelty, (as persecutors and soul-betraying pastors will one day know to their remediless woe,) so mercy to souls is the greatest mercy. Yet sometimes mercy to the body is in that season to be preferred (for everything is excellent in its season). As if a man is drowning or starving, you must not delay the relief of his body, while you are preaching to him for his conversion; but first relieve him, and then you may in season afterward instruct him. The greatest duty is not always to go first in time; sometimes some lesser work is a necessary preparation for a greater; and sometimes a bodily benefit may tend more to the good of souls than some spiritual work may. Therefore I say still, that prudence and an honest heart are instead of many directions: they will not only look at the immediate benefit of a work, but to its utmost tendency and remote effects.

**Direction 11. In doing good, prefer the good of many, especially of the church or commonwealth, before the good of one or few. [113]**

For many are more worth than one; and many will honor God and serve him more than one: and therefore both piety and charity require it. Yet this also must be understood with a _cæteris paribus_; for it is possible some cases of exception may be found. Paul's is a high instance, who "could have wished himself accursed from Christ" for the sake of the Jews, as judging God's honor more concerned in all of them than in him alone.

**Direction 12. Prefer a durable good that will extend to posterity, before a short and transitory good.**

As to build an almshouse is a greater work than to give an alms, and to erect a school than to teach a pupil; so to promote the settlement of the gospel and a faithful ministry is the greatest of all, as tending to the good of many, even to their everlasting good. This is the pre-eminence of good books before a transient speech, that they may be a more durable help and benefit. Look before you with a judicious foresight; and as you must not do that immediate good to a particular person which brings greater hurt to many; so you must not do that immediate good to one or many which is likely to produce a greater and more lasting hurt. Such blind reformers have used the church, as ignorant physicians use their patients, who give them a little immediate ease, and cast them into a greater misery, and seem to cure them with a dose of opium or the Jesuit's powder, when they are bringing them into a worse disease than that which they claim to cure. Oh when shall the poor church have wiser and foreseeing helpers!

**Direction 13. Let all that you do for the church's good be sure to tend to holiness and peace; and do nothing under the name of a good work which has an enmity to either of these.**

For these are to the church as life and health are to the body; and the increase of its welfare is nothing else but the increase of these. Whatever they claim, believe none who say they seek the good and welfare of the church, if they do not seek the promoting of holiness and peace: if they hinder the powerful preaching of the gospel, and the means that tend to the saving of souls, and the serious, spiritual worshiping of God, and the unity and peace of all the faithful; and if they either divide the faithful into sects and parties, or harass all who differ from them, and do not humor them in their notions;—take all these for such benefactors to the church as the wolf is to the flock, and as the plague is to the city, or the fever to the body, or the fire in the thatch is to the house. "The wisdom from above is first pure, then peaceable, gentle," etc. "But if you have bitter jealousy and selfishness in your hearts, do not boast and tell lies against the truth. Such wisdom does not come from above but is earthly, natural, demonic. For where there is jealousy and selfishness, there is disorder and every evil practice" (James 3:14-18).

**Direction 14. If you will do the good which God accepts, do that which he requires; and do not put the name of good works upon your sins, nor upon unnecessary things of your own invention; nor think that any good must be accomplished by forbidden means.**

None know what pleases God so well as himself. Our ways may be right in our own eyes, and carnal wisdom may think it has devised the fittest means to honor God, when he may abominate it, and say, Who required this at your hand? And if we will do good by sinning, we must do it in defiance of God, who is engaged against our sins and us (Romans 3:8). God does not need our lie for his glory: if papists think to find at the last day their foolish ceremonies, and superstition, and will-worship, their "Do not handle! Do not taste! Do not touch!" to be reckoned to them as good works; or if Jesuits or enthusiasts think to find their perjury, treasons, rebellions, or conspiracies numbered with good works; or the persecuting of the preachers and faithful professors of godliness to be good works; how lamentably will they find their expectations disappointed!

**Direction 15. Keep in the way of your place and calling, and do not take other men's works upon you without a call, under any claim of doing good.**

Magistrates must do good in the place and work of magistrates; and ministers in the place and work of ministers; and private men in their private place and work; and not one man step into another's place, and take his work out of his hand, and say, I can do it better: for if you should do it better, the disorder will do more harm than you did good by bettering his work. One judge must not step into another's court and seat, and say I will pass more righteous judgment. You must not go into another man's school, and say, I can teach your pupils better; nor into another's charge or pulpit, and say, I can preach better. The servant may not rule the master, because he can do it best; no more than you may take another man's wife, or house, or lands, or goods, because you can use them better than he. Do the good that you are called to.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows the Latin in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[111] Some think they merit by curing the hurts which they have caused themselves. Sed nequitia est, ut extrahas mergere, evertere ut suscites, includere ut emittas. Non enim beneficium injuriæ finis; nec unquam id detraxisse meritum est, quod ipse qui detraxit intulerat. Senec. de Benef. (But it is wickedness to plunge in order to drag out, to overturn in order to raise up, to shut in in order to let out. For a benefit is not the end of an injury; nor is it ever a merit to have taken away what the very one who took it away had inflicted. Seneca, *On Benefits*. Rendered loosely; the last clause is obscure.)

[112] "Sell all and give to the poor, and follow me." But do not sell all, unless you follow me: that is, unless you have a vocation in which you may do as much good with little means as with great. Lord Bacon's Essay 13.

[113] Absurdum est unum laute vivere, cum multi esuriunt. Quanto enim gloriosius est multis benefacere, quam magnifice habitare? Quanto prudentius in homines quam in lapides, et in aurum impensas facere. Clem. Alexand. 2. Pædag. 12. (English: It is absurd for one person to live sumptuously when many are hungry. For how much more glorious it is to do good to many than to dwell magnificently! How much wiser to spend on people than on stones and gold. Clement of Alexandria, *Paedagogus*, book 2, chapter 12.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible(R); see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). All NET wording was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Numbering:** Baxter's "_Direct._ IV." to "XV." are rendered Direction 4 to 15. The range opens at Direction 4 with no Grand Direction heading, so none is added. In the source, Direction 4's bold-style heading and the sentence after the dash ("Think not to offer God a sacrifice...") are one run-in paragraph; the dash is replaced by the heading break, as in the finished files. This is done for every Direction here.
- **Flag, Romans 10:2 (Direction 9):** The King James has "a zeal of God, but not according to knowledge," and Baxter's whole point is that zeal needs knowledge and judgment (hence "Zeal without judgment"). The NET has "zealous for God, but their zeal is not in line with the truth." "The truth" is not quite "knowledge": the NET speaks of zeal at odds with the truth, while Baxter's argument is about zeal lacking understanding. Baxter's argument still stands, but it leans on the King James word.
- **Flag, James 3:14-18 (Direction 13):** Baxter's King James has "bitter envying and strife," "sensual, devilish," and "confusion." The NET has "bitter jealousy and selfishness," "natural, demonic," and "disorder." "Strife" (quarreling, division) fits Baxter's charge that people "divide the faithful into sects and parties"; the NET's "selfishness" loses that sense. Likewise "sensual" is rendered "natural" in the NET (unspiritual, merely of this life), which is nearer to Baxter's own meaning for "carnal." The NET's verse 17 begins "But the wisdom...," and the quotation here drops "But" because Baxter begins mid-thought with "The wisdom." The NET's sentence breaks are kept inside the quotation; Baxter ran verses 14-18 together with "etc." and a skip.
- **Proverbs 3:9-10 (Direction 7):** NET "wealth" and "firstfruits"; Baxter has "substance" and "presses," where the NET has "vats." His list of references (Exodus, Leviticus, Nehemiah, Ezekiel) is converted to modern form and kept as printed; we have not checked each one against his point.
- **Flag, James 5:20 (Direction 10):** Baxter's "convert a sinner from the error of his way" is the King James phrasing; he does not put it in quotation marks, so it is kept as his own wording. The NET has "turns a sinner back from his wandering path will save that person's soul from death and will cover a multitude of sins." The sense is the same, but the NET's "turns back" is plainer than "convert," which is the word Baxter's argument about "souls" and "conversion" leans on.
- **Paul's wish (Direction 11):** Baxter's quotation, "could have wished himself accursed from Christ," is a paraphrase of the King James; he gives no reference. It is probably Romans 9:3 (not in Baxter's text). The NET reads "I could wish that I myself were accursed—cut off from Christ—for the sake of my people." Baxter's wording is kept in quotation marks, modernized only in spelling.
- **"Do not handle! Do not taste! Do not touch!"** (Direction 14): Baxter has "touch not, taste not, handle not" with no reference; it is Colossians 2:21 (not in Baxter's text). The NET reverses the order of his three verbs. We use the NET wording. "Will-worship" is the King James term in Colossians 2:23; it is kept as Baxter's word for worship that people invent for themselves.
- **Romans 3:8 (Direction 14):** a reference only; Baxter does not quote it. The NET reads "Let us do evil so that good may come of it." "God needs not our lie to his glory" probably echoes Romans 3:7 (not in Baxter's text; we have not checked).
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "those that killed them should think they did God service" (Direction 9; compare John 16:2, where the NET has "will think he is offering service to God"); "Who required this at your hand?" (Direction 14; possibly an echo of Isaiah 1:12, where the NET has "do you actually think I want this"; not confirmed, and not in Baxter's text); "godliness with contentment" and "food and raiment" (Direction 7; compare 1 Timothy 6:6, 8, not confirmed here); "everything is excellent in its season" (Direction 10; his own phrase, similar in spirit to Ecclesiastes 3:11, not confirmed).
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): pretend to claim or use as an excuse (Directions 4, 12, 13); improved to put (5); lesser sums stays as written; endeavored to made an effort (6); "Less sums than pounds" to "Smaller sums than pounds" (6); first-fruits to firstfruits (7); laid out to spent (7); narrowly to closely (7); char to chore (8); divers to diverse (9); famishing to starving (10); corporal to bodily (10); instead of to take the place of (10); present (ease, good) to immediate (12); worry to harass (13); conceits to notions (13); foppish to foolish (14); "professors of godliness" kept (Baxter's term for people who openly profess godly religion) (14); scholars to pupils (15); alms-house to almshouse (12).
- **Terms kept:** *cæteris paribus* (Latin, "other things being equal"). **talents** (the money entrusted to servants in Jesus' parable; Baxter uses it for every gift God gives). **materially / formally** (scholastic terms: an act can be good in its content yet not a duty in the circumstances). **inordinately or finally** (excessively, or as an ultimate end). **dropsical** (Baxter's "hydropic": like the unquenchable thirst of dropsy, a swelling illness). **carnal self** (see GLOSSARY). **commonwealth** (the community or state). **vocation / calling** (one's God-given place and work). **charge** (Direction 15: a pastor's congregation). **papists** (his word for Roman Catholics), **Jesuits**, **enthusiasts** (those who claim direct inspiration): his polemic against Rome and the sects, kept as he wrote it.
- **Jesuit's powder:** probably the powdered bark now called cinchona (source of quinine), which was a remedy of that name in his day; this is our understanding and we have not confirmed it for Baxter's use here.
- **Direction 7, punctuation:** the source has a closing parenthesis after "next to nothing." with no opening one; it is dropped in the modernized text. The sentence "Though he that hath godliness..." is kept in Baxter's order, which reads awkwardly.
- **Dashes:** the dash in Direction 13 (";--take all these") is Baxter's own mid-sentence punctuation and is kept as an em dash.
- **Direction 12:** "before" in "prefer... before" is Baxter's word for "over."
- **Direction 10:** the sentence "As cruelty to souls is the most heinous cruelty, (as persecutors..." keeps Baxter's parentheses.
- **Note 111** (Seneca, *De Beneficiis*): Baxter attaches this to the warning against offering God "a sacrifice of injury": the point is that harm done and then repaired earns no merit. "mergere" is printed in the source (as an infinitive; probably a form of "mergo," to plunge); not corrected. Seneca's reference is given only as "de Benef." in Baxter's text.
- **Note 112:** Baxter's "Lord Bacon's Essay 13" is presumably Francis Bacon's essay on goodness; the numbering of the essays varies by edition and we have not confirmed it. The quoted gospel words are an allusion (compare Matthew 19:21; not confirmed in this form); Baxter's gloss is his own.
- **Note 113** (Clement of Alexandria): we take "Clem. Alexand. 2. Pædag. 12" to be the *Paedagogus*, book 2, chapter 12, as the abbreviation suggests; we have not verified the quotation against an edition. "Lapides" and "aurum" are printed as in the source.
- **Cross-reference:** Directions 1 to 3 of this Grand Direction are in the previous file; Direction 15 is continued by Direction 16 onward in the next.
