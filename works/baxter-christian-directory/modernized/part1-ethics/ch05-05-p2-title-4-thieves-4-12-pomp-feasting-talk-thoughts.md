# Chapter 5: Title 4, the thieves of time (Thieves 4-12)

*This section continues Title 4, "The thieves or time-wasters to be watchfully avoided," beginning at its fourth Thief.*

**Thief 4.** Another time-wasting thief is unnecessary pomp and fussiness in retinue, attendance, house furniture, provision and entertainments, together with excess of courtesies and ceremony, and servitude to the humors and expectations of time-wasters. [294] I crowd them all together, because they are all but wheels of the same engine, to avoid wordiness. Here also I must head off the quibbles of the guilty, by telling you that I do not rebuke all that in the rich which I would rebuke if it were in the poor: I do not intend to level them, and judge them by the same measure. The rich are not so happy as to be so free as the poor, either from the temptation, or the seeming necessity and obligation: let others pity the poor; I will pity the rich, who seem to be pinched with harder necessities than the poor; even this seeming necessity of wasting their precious time in courtesies, fussiness, and pomp, which the happy poor may spend in the honest labors of their callings; in which they may at once be profitable to the commonwealth, and maintain themselves, and meditate or talk of holy things. But yet I must say that the rich shall give an account of time, and shall pay dearly for that which unnecessary excesses devour: and that instead of envying the grandeur and fussiness of others, and seeking to excel or equal them to avoid their scorn, they should contract and bring down all customs of excess, and show their high esteem of time, and detestation of time-wasting fussiness; and imitate the most sober, grave, and holy; and be a pattern to others of employing time in needful, great, and manly things; I say, manly, for so childish is this vice that men of gravity and business abhor it: and usually empty men that are guilty of it lay it all on the women, as if they were ashamed of it, or it were below them. What abundance of precious time is spent in unnecessary grandeur of attendance, and provisions! What abundance, under pretense of cleanliness and neatness, is spent in needless fussiness about rooms, and furniture, and accommodations, and matters of mere pride, vainglory, and ostentation, covered with the honest name of decency! What abundance is wasted in entertainments, and unnecessary visits, courtesies, ceremony, and servitude to the humors of empty men! I do not speak for filthiness, uncleanness, and unseemliness: I do not speak for a cynical moroseness or unsociableness. When conscience is awakened, and you come to yourselves, and approaching death shall better acquaint you with the worth of time, you will see a mean between these two; and you will wish you had most feared the time-wasting prodigal extreme. [295] It seems to me you should freely give me leave to say that though Martha had a better excuse than you, and was burdened about many things for the entertainment of such a guest as Christ himself (with all his followers), who looked for no fussiness, yet Mary is more approved of by Christ, who neglected all this, to redeem the time for the good of her soul, by sitting at his feet to hear his word: she chose the better part, which shall not be taken from her. Remember, I beg you, that one thing is necessary: I hope I may have leave to tell you that if by you or your servants, God, and your souls, and prayer, and reading the Scriptures, and the profitable labors of an honest calling, are all or any of them neglected, while you or they are tidying this room, or washing out that little spot, or setting straight the other wrinkle, or are taken up with feminine trifling, proud fussiness, this is the preferring of dust before gold, of the least before the greatest things: [296] and to say that decency is commendable is no excuse for neglecting God, your souls, or family, or leaving undone any one greater work, which you or your servants might have been doing in that while; I say, any work that is greater all things considered. Oh that you and your families would but live as those that see how fast death comes! how fast time goes! and what you have to do! and what your unready souls still lack! This is all that I desire of you: and then I warrant you, it would save you many a precious hour, and cut short your works of fussiness, and deliver you from your slavery to pride and the esteem of empty time-wasters.

**Thief 5.** Another time-wasting sin is needless and tedious feastings, gluttony, and tippling: which, being of the same litter, I set together. [297] I do not speak against moderate, seasonable, and charitable feasts: but alas, in this luxurious, pleasure-loving age, how commonly do men sit two hours at a feast, and spend two more in attending it before and after, and not using the time in any pious or profitable talk: yes, the rich spend an hour ordinarily in a common meal, while every meal is a feast indeed; and they fare as their predecessor (Luke 16) deliciously or sumptuously every day. Happy are the poor, that are free also from this temptation. You do not spend so much time in the daily addresses of your souls to God, and reading his word, and taking an account of the affairs of conscience, and preparing for death, as you do in stuffing your guts, perhaps at one meal. And in taverns and alehouses among the pots, how much time is wasted by rich and poor! O remember, while you are eating and drinking, what a corruptible piece of flesh you are feeding and serving; and how quickly those mouths will be filled with dust! and that a soul that is hurrying so fast to eternity should find no time to spare for empty things; and that you have important work enough to do, which if performed, will afford you a sweeter and a longer feast.

**Thief 6.** Another time-wasting sin is idle talk. What abundance of precious time this consumes! Listen to most men's talk when they are sitting together, or working together, or traveling together, and you shall hear how little of it is any better than silence: and if not better it is worse. So full are those persons of frivolity who are empty, even to silence, of anything that is good, that they can find and feed a conversation about nothing many hours and days together; and, as they think, with such fertility and floridness of style as deserves acceptance if not applause. I have often marveled at some wordy preachers, with how little matter they can handsomely fill up an hour! But one would wonder more to hear people fill up, not an hour, but a great part of their day, and of their lives, and that without any study at all, and without any holy and substantial subject, with words which, if you should write them all down and look them over, you would find that the sum and conclusion of them is nothing! How self-applaudingly and pleasingly they can extempore talk idly and of nothing a great part of their lives! I have heard many of them marvel at a poor unlearned Christian that can pray extempore many hours together in very good order and well-composed words. But are they not more to be marveled at, that can very handsomely talk of nothing ten times as long, with greater copiousness, and without repetitions, and that extempore, when they have not that variety of great commanding subjects to be the matter of their speech? I tell you, when time must be reviewed, the consumption of so much in idle talk will appear to have been no such venial sin as empty, careless sinners now imagine.

**Thief 7.** Another thief which by the aforesaid means would steal your time is empty and sinful company. Among whom a spiritual physician that goes to cure them, or a holy person that is full and resolute to bear down empty talk, I confess may well employ his time, when he is cast upon it, or called to it. But to dwell with such, or choose them as our intimates, or causelessly or for pleasure keep among them, will unavoidably lose abundance of your time. If you would do good, they will hinder you; if you will speak of good, they will divert you, or reproach you, or wrangle and quibble with you, or some way or other stop your mouths. They will by a stream of empty talk either bear down, and carry you on with them, or fill your ears, and interrupt and hinder the very thoughts of your minds by which you desire to profit yourselves, when they will not let you be profitable to others.

**Thief 8.** Another notorious time-wasting thief is needless, inordinate amusements and games, which are commonly stigmatized by the offenders themselves with the infamous name of pastimes, and masked with the deceitful title of recreations; such as are cards and dice, and stage-plays, and dancings, and revelings, and excesses in the most lawful amusements, especially in hunting, and hawking, and bowling, [298] etc. Whether all these are lawful or unlawful in themselves is nothing to the present question; but I am sure that the precious hours which they take up might have been spent on the saving of many a thousand souls, that by the loss of time are now undone and past recovery. Except malicious enemies of godliness, I scarcely know a more wretched sort of people on the earth, and more to be lamented, than those fleshly persons who, through the love of physical pleasure, waste many hours day after day in plays and gaming and voluptuous courses; while their miserable souls are dead in sin, enslaved to their fleshly lusts, unreconciled to God, and find no delight in him, or in his service, and cannot make a recreation of any heavenly work. How will it torment these unhappy souls to think how they played away those hours in which they might have been pleasing God, and preventing misery, and laying up a treasure in heaven! And to think that they sold that precious time for a little fleshly amusement, in which they should have been working out their salvation, and making their calling and election sure. But I have more to say to these later.

**Thief 9.** Another time-wasting thief is excess of worldly cares and business. These do not only, as some more disgraced sins, pollute the soul with deep stains in a little time, and then recede; but they dwell upon the mind, and keep possession, and keep out good: they take up the greatest part of the lives of those that are guilty of them. The world is first in the morning in their thoughts, and last at night, and almost all the day: the world will not give them leave to entertain any sober, fixed thoughts of the world to come; nor to do the work which all works should give place to. The world devours all the time almost that God and their souls should have: it will not give them leave to pray, or read, or meditate, or talk of holy things: even when they seem to be praying, or hearing the word of God, the world is in their thoughts; and as it is said, "They come to you in crowds, and they sit in front of you as my people. They hear your words, but do not obey them. For they talk lustfully, and their heart is set on their own advantage" (Ezekiel 33:31). In most families there is almost no talk nor doings but all for the world: these also will know that they had greater works for their precious time, which should always have had precedence over the world.

**Thief 10.** Another time-waster is empty ungoverned and sinful thoughts. When men are wearied with empty works and amusements, they continue unwearied in empty thoughts; when they lack company for empty talk and games, they can waste the time in idle, or lustful, or ambitious, or greedy thoughts alone without any company. In the very nighttime while they are awake, and as they travel by the way, yes, while they seem to be serving God, they will be wasting the time in useless thoughts: so that this devours a greater proportion of precious time than any of the former. When time must be reckoned for, what abundance will be found upon most men's accounts as spent in idle, sinful thoughts! O watch this thief; and remember, though you may think that an empty thought is but a little sin, yet time is not a little or contemptible commodity, nor to be cast away on so little a thing as idle thoughts; and to cheapen thus so choice a treasure is not a little sin; and that it is not a little work that you have to do in the time which you thus waste. And a daily course of idle thoughts wastes so great a measure of time that this aggravation makes it more heinous than many sins of greater infamy. But of this more in the next part.

**Thief 11.** Another dangerous time-wasting sin is the reading of empty books, play-books, romances, and invented histories; and also unprofitable studies, undertaken only for vainglory, or the pleasing of a carnal or curious mind. Of this I have spoken in my book of *Self-denial*. I do not speak here of how pernicious this vice is by corrupting the imagination and affections, and breeding a diseased appetite, and putting you out of relish for necessary things. But consider, before you spend another hour in any such books, whether you can comfortably give an account of it to God; and how precious the time is which you are wasting on such childish toys. You think the reading of such things is lawful; but is it lawful to lose your precious time? You say that your petty studies are desirable and laudable; but the neglect of far greater necessary things is not laudable. I discourage no man from laboring to know all that God has in any way revealed to be known; but I say as Seneca, We are ignorant of things necessary, because we learn things superfluous and unnecessary. Art is long and life is short: and he that does not have time for all should make sure of the greatest matters; and if he is ignorant of anything, let it be of that which the love of God, and our own and other men's salvation, and the public good least require, and can best spare. It is a pitiful thing to see a man waste his time in criticizing, or growing wise in the less necessary sciences and arts, while he is yet a slave of pride or worldliness, and has an unrenewed soul, and has not learned the mysteries necessary to his own salvation. But yet these studies are laudable in their season. But the fanatic studies of those that would pry into unrevealed things, and the lascivious employment of those that read love-books, and play-books, and empty stories, will one day appear to have been but an unwise expense of time for those that had so much better and more needful work to do with it. I think there are few of those that plead for it that would be found with such books in their hands at death, or will then find any pleasure in the remembrance of them.

**Thief 12.** But the master-thief that robs men of their time is an unsanctified, ungodly heart; for this loses time whatever men are doing: because they never truly intend the glory of God; and not having a right principle or a right end, their whole course is hellward; and whatever they do, they are not working out their salvation: and therefore they are always losing their time as to themselves, however God may use the time and gifts of some of them as a mercy to others. Therefore a new and holy heart, with a heavenly intention and design of life, is the great thing necessary to all that will savingly redeem their time.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows the Latin passages in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[294] Nihil mihi magis quam pompa displicet: non solum quia mala, et humilitati contraria, sed quia difficilis, et quieti adversa est. Petrarch. in Vita Sua. (Nothing displeases me more than pomp: not only because it is evil and contrary to humility, but because it is troublesome and hostile to quiet. Petrarch, in his *Life*.)

[295] Nimia omnia nimium exhibent negotium. (All things in excess cause excessive trouble.)

[296] Abundance of little things that have all their conveniences have all their inconveniences also, and take up our time, and so would shut out greater things if they are not cast aside themselves, and would become great sins by such a consumption of our time (Luke 10:42).

[297] Convivia, quæ dicuntur (cum sint commessationes modestiæ et bonis moribus inimicæ) semper mihi displicuerunt; laboriosum, et inutile ratus vocare et vocari, &c. Idem. (Banquets, as they are called (since they are revelries hostile to modesty and good morals), have always displeased me; I judged it laborious and useless to invite and to be invited, etc. The same [that is, Petrarch, as in note 294].)

[298] Laertius says of Solon that Thespim tragœdias agere et docere prohibuit, inutilem eas falsiloquentiam vocans. (... that he forbade Thespis to act and to produce tragedies, calling them useless false-speaking. Diogenes Laertius, on Solon.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **Title; Thief:** "Title" is Baxter's word ("Tit.") for one of the five main divisions of Chapter 5; this file is in Title 4. The "Thieves" (Baxter's "_Thief_ IV." and so on) are the numbered items of Title 4. Title 4 follows Direction 11 of Title 3 ("be acquainted with the great thieves that rob men of their time"), which introduces this list. The italic line under the heading is ours, not Baxter's. We render them as **Thief 4.** etc., keeping his numbering. Thieves 1-3 are in the preceding file.
- **Ezekiel 33:31:** the source prints "Ezek. xxxii. 31," but Ezekiel 32:31 is about Pharaoh; the quoted words are at 33:31. We corrected the reference. **NET flag:** Baxter's King James wording reads "with their mouth they show much love; but their heart goeth after their covetousness." The NET has "they talk lustfully, and their heart is set on their own advantage." The second half still carries Baxter's point (the heart set on gain while sitting under the word), but the NET's "talk lustfully" replaces the King James picture of hearers who profess much love with their mouths, and "their own advantage" is broader than his "covetousness" (greed), which is the word that ties the verse to his thief of worldly cares.
- **Luke 16 (Thief 5):** Baxter refers to the rich man of Luke 16:19 and gives the phrase "deliciously or sumptuously every day" without quotation marks; we keep his wording. The NET reads "who feasted sumptuously every day." His "deliciously or sumptuously" appears to offer two renderings of the same word.
- **Martha and Mary (Thief 4):** an unmarked reference to Luke 10:38-42, kept in Baxter's wording ("cumbered about many things," "one thing is necessary," "she chose the better part, which shall not be taken from her"). The NET of 10:41-42 reads "you are worried and troubled about many things, but one thing is needed. Mary has chosen the best part; it will not be taken away from her." Baxter's note 296 cites Luke 10:42.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "redeem the time" (Ephesians 5:16; Colossians 4:5); "laying up a treasure in heaven" (Matthew 6:20); "working out their salvation" (Philippians 2:12); "making their calling and election sure" (2 Peter 1:10); "dead in sin" (Ephesians 2:1); "you come to yourselves" (Luke 15:17).
- **fussiness:** Baxter's "curiosity" (and "curiosities," "curious") in Thief 4, meaning elaborate, over-careful refinement in dress, rooms, and entertainments, not inquisitiveness. In Thief 11, "a carnal or curious mind" keeps "curious," where it does mean inquisitive.
- **courtesies:** his "compliment," meaning formal civilities and ceremonial politeness, not praise. **grandeur:** his "state" (pomp, show of rank). **scorn:** his "obloquy." **wordiness:** his "prolixity." **head off the quibbles:** his "prevent the cavils" (prevent = anticipate). **rebuke:** his "reprove." **talk of:** his "confer of." **filthiness; unseemliness:** his "nastiness; uncomeliness." **moroseness:** his "morosity." **It seems to me:** his "Methinks." **burdened:** his "cumbered." **I beg you:** his "I pray you." **tidying:** his coinage "neatifying." **in that while:** kept, meaning "in the meantime." **lack:** his "want."
- **empty men; empty things; frivolity:** his "men of vanity," "vanity," and (in "so full are those persons of vanity") "vanity." See GLOSSARY for "vain, vanity." "Empty" is used throughout for his "vain."
- **feminine trifling** (Thief 4): Baxter's phrase, kept. In the same paragraph he says men who are guilty of this fussiness blame it all on the women; his generalizations about women are his and are rendered as he wrote them.
- **pleasure-loving:** his "sensual" (age); **physical pleasure:** his "sensual pleasure." **using; spent on:** his "improving" and "improved to" (making good use of time). **hurrying:** his "posting." **fertility:** his "fecundity." **look them over:** his "peruse them." **intimates:** his "familiars." **for pleasure:** his "for complacency," probably meaning for the pleasure of their company or to please them; we have not settled which. **quibble:** his "cavil." **later:** his "anon"; he does return to these persons later in the Directory, but we have not checked where. **cheapen:** his "vilify," in its older sense of making something vile or cheap, not slandering it. **invented histories:** his "feigned histories" (fiction presented as history). **imagination:** his "fancy." **always:** his "still" (continually).
- **tippling:** kept; habitual drinking of alcohol. **extempore:** kept; spoken without preparation.
- **venial** (Thief 6): kept as Baxter's technical term; in the scheme of mortal and venial sins, a venial sin is a lesser, pardonable one. His point is that idle talk is not as small a sin as people think.
- **amusements:** his "sports" per the project convention, including where the "sports" are hunting, hawking, and bowling.
- **"stigmatized ... with the infamous name of pastimes"** (Thief 8): Baxter's irony; the very name "pastime" (passing the time) brands these activities as time-wasting, so the offenders condemn them by what they call them.
- **criticizing** (Thief 11): kept; Baxter probably means minute textual or literary scholarship (the work of "critics" of texts), not finding fault.
- **"We are ignorant of things necessary..."** (Thief 11): Baxter attributes this to Seneca without a reference; the saying resembles a passage in Seneca's *Letters*, but we have not confirmed the source. **"Art is long and life is short":** the opening of the Hippocratic *Aphorisms* in its Latin form (*ars longa, vita brevis*); Baxter gives no source.
- **Self-denial** (Thief 11): Baxter's own book, probably his *Treatise of Self-Denial* (1660); we have not checked the passage he means.
- **Note 294:** "Petrarch. in Vita Sua" probably refers to Petrarch's autobiographical letter (often called the *Letter to Posterity*); we have not confirmed the wording there. Note 297's "Idem" ("the same") points back to Petrarch.
- **Note 295:** Baxter gives no author; the line reads like a proverb, and we have not identified a source.
- **Note 298:** Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of the Philosophers*, book 1, life of Solon (we have not checked the exact passage). Thespis was the traditional founder of Greek tragedy. "falsiloquentiam" is rendered "false-speaking" (lying talk).
- **Punctuation:** we added commas around "being of the same litter" (Thief 5), "as they think" (Thief 6), and "before you spend another hour in any such books" (Thief 11), and put Martha's "(with all his followers)" in parentheses without the surrounding commas. "empty ungoverned and sinful thoughts" (Thief 10) keeps Baxter's lack of commas.
