# Chapter 10: Title 3, Direction 2 (continued) and Direction 3: the benefits of zeal and the motives to it

*This section begins partway through Direction 2, at item 4 of Baxter's list of the excellencies and benefits of zeal; the previous file covers items 1–3.*

4. Zeal is much of the strength of duty; and makes it likeliest to attain its end. The prayer of the faithful that is effectual must be "fervent," James 5:16. Zeal must make us persistent petitioners, that will take no denial, if we would succeed, Luke 18:1–8. "The kingdom of heaven has suffered violence, and forceful people lay hold of it." We must "exert every effort to enter through the narrow door because many ... will try to enter and will not be able to." Not every one that strives is crowned, nor every one that runs wins the prize; but he that does it effectually so as to attain. No wonder if we are commanded to love God with all our heart, and soul, and might, which is a zealous love; for this is what overcomes all other love, and will constrain to dutiful obedience. As experience tells us, it is the zealous and diligent preacher that does good, when the cold and negligent do only a little; so it is in all other duties; the diligent hand makes rich, and God blesses those that serve him heartily with all their might. [592]

5. Zeal and diligence take the opportunity which sloth and negligence let slip. They are up with the sun, and "perform the deeds ... as long as it is daytime;" they "seek the Lord while he makes himself available; call to him while he is nearby;" they know the day of their visitation and salvation; they do not delay, but take the "acceptable time." When the slothful are still delaying and trifling, and do not hear God's voice while it is "called 'Today,'" but "harden their hearts," and sleep with their "lamps unsupplied," and do not knock till "the door was shut." [593] They stand and look at their work while they should do it; they are never in readiness when Christ and mercy are to be welcomed; they are always putting off their duty till some other time; till time is done, and their work undone, and they are undone forever.

6. Zeal and diligence are the best improvers of time and mercy; as they do not delay, but take the present time, so they do not loiter, but do their work to good purpose. As a speedy traveler goes farther in a day than a slothful one in many; so a zealous, diligent Christian will do more for God and his soul in a little time than a negligent dullard in all his life. It is a wonder to think what Augustine and Chrysostom did among the ancients! what Calvin, and Perkins, and Whitaker, and Reignolds, and Chamier, and many other Reformed theologians have done in a very little time! and what Suarez, and Vasquez, and Jansenius, and Tostatus, and Cajetan, and Aquinas, and many other papists have performed by diligence! When millions of men that have longer time go out of the world as unknown as they came into it; having never attained so much knowledge as might preserve them from the reproach of brutish ignorance, nor so much as might save their souls from hell: and when many that had diligence enough to get some praiseworthy abilities never had diligence enough to use them to any great benefit of others or themselves. Zeal and diligence are that fruitful, well-tilled soil where God sows his seed with best success; and which returns him for his mercies a hundredfold, Matthew 13:8, 23; and at his coming gives him his "money back with interest," Matthew 25:27, 28. But sloth and negligence are the grave of mercies, where they are buried till they rise up in judgment against the despisers and wasters of them. Aristotle and Plato, Galen and Hippocrates, improvers of nature, shall condemn these slothful neglecters and abusers of nature and grace; yes, their oxen and horses shall be witnesses against many that did not serve God with any such diligence as these beasts served them; yes, many fashionable gentlemen of great estates never did so much service for the common good in all their lives as their very beasts have done. Their abilities, their life, and all are lost by them.

7. Zeal and diligence are the victorious enemies of sin and Satan. They do not bear with sin: they are to it as a consuming fire is to the thorns and briers. Zeal burns up lust, and greed, and pride, and indulgence in physical pleasure. [594] It makes such work among our sins as diligent weeders do in your gardens; it pulls up the weeds and burns them. It does not stand dallying with sin, nor tasting or looking on the bait, nor disputing with and listening to the tempter; but casts away the impulse with abhorrence, and abstains from the very appearance of evil, and hates the garment spotted by the flesh, and at once quenches the sparks of concupiscence; it charges home, and so resists the devil that he flees: [595] when sloth and negligence cherish the sin, and encourage the tempter, and invite him by a cold resistance. The vineyard of the sluggard is overgrown with nettles; his heart swarms with noxious thoughts and lusts, and he does not resist them, but easily bears them. If he feels sinful thoughts possessing his mind, he does not rise up with zeal against them; he does not have the heart to cast them out, nor make any effectual resistance; he starves his soul with fruitless wishes, because his hands refuse to labor. [596] Negligence is the nurse of sin.

8. Zeal and diligence bear down all opposition against duty with power and success. Those impediments which stop a sluggard are as nothing before them; as the cart-wheels which go slowly are easily stopped by a little stone or anything in their way, when those that are in a swifter motion easily get over all. The lion that is in the sluggard's way is not so much as a barking puppy in the way of a diligent, zealous Christian. The cold does not hinder him from plowing. [597] A mere scorn, or mock, or threatening of a mortal man will dismay and stop a heartless hypocrite; which only serve as oil to the fire, to inflame the courage of the zealous so much more. The difficulties which seem insurmountable to the slothful are small matters to the zealous; he goes through that which the slothful calls impossible. And when the slothful sits still and says, I cannot do this or that, the zealous, diligent Christian does it.

9. Zeal and diligence take off the toil and irksomeness of duty, and make it easy. As a quick-spirited, diligent servant makes only a pleasure of his work, which a lazy servant does with pain and weariness; and as a spirited horse makes a pleasure of a journey, which a heavy nag goes through with pain; so reading, and hearing, and prayer are easy to a zealous soul, which to another are an unwelcome task and toil.

10. Zeal is faithful, and constant, and valiant, and therefore greatly pleases God: it sticks to him through persecution; the fire does not consume it; many waters do not quench it. But others are false-hearted: and those that have only a cold religion will easily be drawn or driven from their religion. They are so indifferent that a little more of the world put into the balance will weigh down Christ in their esteem. The hopes or fears of temporal things prevail with them against the hopes and fears of things eternal. No wonder therefore if God disowns such treacherous servants, and turns them away as unworthy of his family. [598]

**Direction 3. Let the great motives of holy zeal and diligence be pressed home and printed on your hearts: [599] and often read them over in some enlivening books, that you may remember them, and be affected with them.**

I have given you so many of these moving, exciting considerations in the third part of my *Saints' Rest*, and my *Saint or Brute*, and *Now or Never*, and in my sermon against *Making Light of Christ*, that I shall be only very brief in them at present.

*In the margin: Motives of zeal.*

1. When you grow cold and slothful, remember how great a Master you serve: should anything be done negligently for God? And remember how good a Master you serve; for whom you are certain that you can never do too much; nor so much as he deserves of you; nor will he ever allow you to be losers by him.

2. Remember that he is always present; in your dealings with others, in your prayers, your reading, and all your duties: and will you loiter in his sight? when a mere eye-servant will work while his master stands by.

3. One serious thought of the end and consequence of all your work, one would think, should put life into the dullest soul! Say to your sleepy, frozen heart, Is it not heaven that I am seeking? Is it not hell that I am avoiding? And can I be cold and slothful about heaven and hell? Must it not go with me forever according to how I now behave myself? And is this the best that I can do for my salvation? Is it not God that I have to please and honor? and shall I do it slothfully?

4. One thought of the exceeding greatness of our work, one would think, should make us be zealous and diligent! To think what abundance of knowledge we have to get! and how much of every grace we lack! and how many means we have to use! and how much opposition and many temptations to overcome! The humble sense of the weakness of our souls, and the greatness of our sins, should make us say that whatever the rich in grace may do, it is labor that befits the poor.

5. To remember how short our time of working is, and also how uncertain! How fast it flies away! how soon it will be at an end! And that all the time that ever we shall have to prepare for eternity is now! and that shortly there will be no praying, no hearing, no working any more on earth! [600] To look into the grave, to go to the house of mourning! to consider that this heart has only a little more time to think, and this tongue only a little more time to speak, and all will end in the endless recompense; I think this should enliven the coldest heart!

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). Every NET quotation below was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Where we are:** this file is in Chapter 10 (Directions for the Government of the Body), Part 1 (Directions about our labor and callings), Title 3 (Directions against sloth and laziness). "Title" (*Tit.* in the source) is Baxter's word for a heading or division within a Part. Neither "Title" nor the word "calling" (Baxter's word for one's vocation or occupation) occurs inside this range; the headings are in the earlier file.
- **James 5:16** (item 4): Baxter's "fervent" comes from the King James "the effectual fervent prayer of a righteous man availeth much." The NET reads "The prayer of a righteous person has great effectiveness," with no word for "fervent." Since his point rests on that word, we keep Baxter's "fervent" in his quotation marks and flag that it is not in the NET.
- **Matthew 11:12** (item 4): NET wording; Baxter gives no reference in the text (it is the first reference in note 592). The King James had "the kingdom of heaven suffereth violence, and the violent take it by force." Baxter reads this as earnest seekers taking the kingdom by force; the NET's "has suffered violence, and forceful people lay hold of it" can more easily be read as the kingdom being attacked by violent people, which would not support his point. The verse is disputed among interpreters.
- **Luke 13:24** (item 4): NET wording, with "I tell you" left out (marked "..."), since Baxter's quotation skips it. The King James had "strive to enter in at the strait gate." The NET's "exert every effort" keeps the sense of striving that Baxter leans on; "narrow door" replaces "strait gate." Reference not in the text (in note 592).
- **Unmarked allusions in item 4** (kept in Baxter's wording): striving and being crowned (2 Timothy 2:5); running and winning the prize (1 Corinthians 9:24); loving God with all heart, soul, and might (Deuteronomy 6:5); "the diligent hand makes rich" (Proverbs 10:4, King James "the hand of the diligent maketh rich"; NET "the one who works diligently becomes wealthy"); Luke 18:1–8 is the parable of the persistent widow.
- **John 9:4** (item 5): NET wording, "perform the deeds ... as long as it is daytime," with "of the one who sent me" skipped. The King James had "I must work the works of him that sent me, while it is day," from which Baxter's "work while it is day" is taken. The sense is unchanged.
- **Isaiah 55:6** (item 5): NET wording. The King James had "while he may be found ... while he is near"; the NET's "while he makes himself available" keeps the point that there is a limited time to seek him. Baxter's "and" between the clauses is not in the NET, which uses a semicolon.
- **"acceptable time"** (item 5): 2 Corinthians 6:2 (NET "now is the acceptable time"; King James "accepted time"). Reference not in Baxter's text.
- **Hebrews 3:13, 15** (item 5): "called 'Today'" is NET Hebrews 3:13. "harden their hearts" is kept in Baxter's wording, since he changes the person; the NET has "Do not harden your hearts" (Hebrews 3:8, 15), the same verb. Note 593 cites Hebrews 3:7, 15.
- **"lamps unsupplied," "the door was shut"** (item 5): the parable of the ten virgins (Matthew 25:1–13). "lamps unfurnished" is Baxter's phrase in quotation marks, not a verse; we changed "unfurnished" (in his day "not supplied," here with oil) to "unsupplied." "the door was shut" is NET Matthew 25:10. "visitation" alludes to Luke 19:44 (note 593 cites Luke 19:42).
- **Matthew 25:27** (item 6): Baxter's "own with usury" is the King James "I should have received mine own with usury." NET: "I would have received my money back with interest." We use the NET words with "my" changed to Baxter's "his" frame ("gives him his 'money back with interest'"). "Usury" then meant any interest, not only excessive interest, so the sense is unchanged.
- **improvers** (item 6): kept. In Baxter's English to "improve" something meant to put it to good use or turn it to profit (as in "improving time"), not only to make it better.
- **The writers named in item 6** (our identifications, probably right but not checked against Baxter's other works): Augustine and John Chrysostom (early church); Calvin, William Perkins, William Whitaker, "Reignolds" (probably John Rainolds of Oxford; we have not confirmed it), and Daniel Chamier (Reformed Protestants); Francisco Suárez, Gabriel Vázquez, Cornelius Jansen ("Jansenius"), Alonso Tostado ("Tostatus"), Cardinal Cajetan, and Thomas Aquinas (Roman Catholic). Baxter's spellings are kept. **papists:** Baxter's word for Roman Catholics, kept as his. **theologians:** Baxter's "divines."
- **well-tilled:** Baxter's "well-manured." In his day "manure" meant to cultivate or till land, not only to fertilize it. **hundredfold:** the parable of the sower (Matthew 13:8, 23).
- **wasters:** Baxter's "consumers" (those who use up and waste). **fashionable gentlemen:** Baxter's "gallants." **abilities:** Baxter's "parts" (natural gifts). **Aristotle, Plato, Galen, Hippocrates:** philosophers and physicians of pagan antiquity, whom Baxter calls "improvers of nature."
- **Item 7:** **impulse:** Baxter's "motion" (an inward prompting to sin). **concupiscence:** kept; the theologians' word for sinful desire. **charges home:** kept; a military phrase for pressing an attack all the way. **at once:** Baxter's "presently." **noxious:** Baxter's "noisome." **starves:** Baxter's "famisheth." **weeds:** Baxter's "tares," the word in the King James parable of the weeds (Matthew 13:24–30). **indulgence in physical pleasure:** Baxter's "sensuality" (see the Glossary).
- **Unmarked allusions in item 7** (kept in Baxter's wording): fire and briers (perhaps Isaiah 10:17 or 27:4; not cited by Baxter); "the very appearance of evil" (1 Thessalonians 5:22; NET "Stay away from every form of evil"); "the garment spotted by the flesh" (Jude 23; NET "hating even the clothes stained by the flesh"); resisting the devil so that he flees (James 4:7); the sluggard's vineyard (Proverbs 24:30–31); hands that refuse to labor (Proverbs 21:25). The NET's "every form of evil" at 1 Thessalonians 5:22 does not carry the "appearance" that Baxter's wording stresses.
- **Item 8:** the lion in the sluggard's way (Proverbs 22:13; 26:13); the sluggard who will not plow because of the cold (Proverbs 20:4; King James "by reason of the cold"). **mere:** Baxter's "very" (as in "a very scorn"). **puppy:** Baxter's "whelp." **insurmountable:** Baxter's "insuperable."
- **Item 9:** **spirited horse:** Baxter's "mettlesome horse." **nag:** Baxter's "jade" (a worn-out or inferior horse). **hearing:** hearing sermons.
- **Item 10:** many waters cannot quench it (Song of Songs 8:7); **family:** in Baxter's sense, a household including its servants.
- **"only":** Baxter's "but" in the sense "only" ("do but little," "do but serve," "have but a cold religion," "be but very brief," "has but a little more time").
- **Direction 3:** **pressed home:** Baxter's "set home" (driven firmly in). **enlivening:** Baxter's "quickening" (making alive); likewise "enliven" for his "quicken" in item 5. Baxter's own works are kept in italics: *Saints' Rest* (The Saints' Everlasting Rest), *Saint or Brute*, *Now or Never*, and his sermon *Making Light of Christ*.
- **Motives, items 1–5:** **dealings:** Baxter's "converse." **eye-servant:** one who works only while watched (compare "eye-service," Ephesians 6:6, Colossians 3:22, King James). **lack:** Baxter's "want." **befits:** Baxter's "becomes." **how many means:** Baxter's "how much means." **according to how:** Baxter's "according as." **I think:** Baxter's "methinks." "the house of mourning" alludes to Ecclesiastes 7:2.
- **Note 592:** the source prints "Prov. l. 4." Proverbs 1:4 does not fit; Proverbs 10:4 is the verse Baxter quotes ("the diligent hand makes rich"), so we print Proverbs 10:4, assuming "l." is a misreading of "x." The source's "Rom. xv. 33" is kept, though Romans 15:33 (NET "Now may the God of peace be with all of you. Amen.") does not obviously fit; Romans 15:30 (NET "to join fervently with me in prayer") may be meant, but we are not sure.
- **Note 594:** "Sam. ii. 23, 29" has no book number. 1 Samuel 2:23, 29 (Eli's weak rebuke of his sons) probably fits Baxter's point; we have not changed the reference.
- **Note 598:** "Heb. xx. 11" cannot be right, since Hebrews has 13 chapters. We are not sure what was meant and keep it as printed.
- **Note 599:** "cont." is unclear in the source (perhaps "contained" or "continued"); we keep it. Chapter 5 of this Part begins with "Directions for redeeming or well improving Time" (source line 29165 onward), so "before" probably means earlier in this book.
