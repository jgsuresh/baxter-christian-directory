# Chapter 6: Title 5, who are meant by the melancholy, and the signs of melancholy (1-25)

*This file begins Title 5. It ends partway through the 25th sign; the next file continues the same sentence and the list of signs.*

**Title 5. Directions to the melancholy about their thoughts.**

It is so easy and ordinary a thing for some weak-headed persons to cast themselves into melancholy, by overstraining either their thoughts or their affections, and the case of such is so exceedingly lamentable, that I think it necessary to give such some particular directions by themselves. [312] And the more so because I see some persons that are unacquainted with the nature of this and other diseases exceedingly abuse the name of God, and bring the profession of religion into scorn, by attributing all the effects and speeches of such melancholy persons to some great and notable operations of the Spirit of God, and from there draw observations of the methods and workings of God upon the soul, and of the nature of the legal workings of the spirit of bondage. (As some other such have made public the prophecies, the possessions and dispossessing of hysterical women, as I have read, especially in the writings of the friars.) I do not call those melancholy who are rationally sorrowful for sin, and aware of their misery, and anxious about their recovery and salvation, though it is with as great seriousness as the faculties can bear; as long as they have sound reason, and the imagination, fantasy, or thinking faculty is not impaired or diseased: but by melancholy I mean this diseased impairment, hurt, or error of the imagination, and consequently of the understanding, which is known by these following signs (which yet are not all in every melancholy person). [313]

1. They are commonly exceedingly fearful, causelessly or beyond what there is cause for: everything which they hear or see is ready to increase their fears, especially if fear was the first cause, as ordinarily it is.

2. Their fantasy most errs in magnifying their sin, or dangers, or unhappiness: every ordinary infirmity they are ready to speak of with dismay, as a heinous sin; and every possible danger they take for probable, and every probable one for certain; and every little danger for a great one; and every calamity for an utter undoing.

3. They are always given to excess of sadness: some weeping they do not know why, and some thinking it ought to be so: and if they should smile or speak merrily, their hearts strike them for it, as if they had done wrong.

4. They place most of their religion in sorrowing and austerities to the flesh.

5. They are continual self-accusers, turning all manner of accusation against themselves, which they hear, or read, or see, or think of: quarreling with themselves for everything they do, as a contentious person does with others.

6. They are always supposing themselves forsaken by God, and are prone to despair: they are just like a man in a wilderness, forsaken by all his friends and comforts, forlorn and desolate: their continual thought is, I am undone, undone, undone!

7. They are always thinking that the day of grace is past, and that it is now too late to repent or to find mercy. If you tell them of the tenor of the gospel, and offers of free pardon to every penitent believer, they cry out still, Too late, too late, my day is past; not considering that every soul that truly repents in this life is certainly forgiven.

8. They are often tempted to gather despairing thoughts from the doctrine of predestination, and to think that if God has reprobated them, or has not elected them, all that they can do, or that all the world can do, cannot save them; and next they strongly imagine that they are not elected, and so that they are past help or hope: not knowing that God elects no man separately or simply to be saved, but jointly to believe, repent, and to be saved; and so to the end and means together; and that all that will repent and choose Christ and a holy life are elected to salvation, because they are elected to the means and condition of salvation, which, if they persevere, they shall enjoy. To repent is the best way to prove that I am elected to repent.

9. They never read or hear of any miserable instance, but they are thinking that this is their case. If they hear of Cain, of Pharaoh given up to hardness of heart, or only read that some are vessels of wrath fitted to destruction, or that they have eyes and do not see, ears and do not hear, hearts and do not understand, they think, This is all spoken of me; or, This is just my case. If they hear of any terrible example of God's judgments on anyone, they think it will be so with them. If any die suddenly, or a house is burned, or any is driven mad, or dies in despair, they think it will be so with them. The reading of Spira's case causes or increases melancholy in many; the ignorant author having described a plain melancholy, contracted by the trouble of sinning against conscience, as if it were a damnable despair of a sound understanding.

10. And yet they think that no one ever was as they are. I have had a great many in a few weeks with me, almost exactly in the same case, and yet every one says that no one ever was as they.

11. They are utterly unable to rejoice in anything; they cannot grasp, believe, or think of anything that is comforting to them. They read all the threats of the word with keen feeling and application, but the promises they read over and over, without taking notice of them, as if they had not read them; or else say, They do not belong to me: the greater the mercy of God is, and the riches of grace, the more miserable am I that have no part in them. They are like a man in continual pain or sickness, that cannot rejoice, because the feeling of his pain forbids him. They look on husband, wife, friends, children, house, goods, and all without any comfort; as one would do that is going to be executed for some crime.

12. Their consciences are quick in telling them of sin, and urging them to any dejection as a duty; but they are dead to all duties that tend to consolation; as to thanksgiving for mercies, praises of God, meditating on his love, and grace, and Christ, and promises: press them ever so hard on these, and they do not feel their duty, nor make any conscience of it, but think it is a duty for others, but unsuitable to them.

13. They always say that they cannot believe, and therefore think they cannot be saved: because commonly they mistake the nature of faith, and take it to be a believing that they themselves are forgiven and in favor with God, and shall be saved; and because they cannot believe this (which their disease will not allow them to believe), therefore they think that they are not believers: whereas saving faith is nothing but such a belief that the gospel is true, and Christ is the Savior to be trusted with our souls, as causes our wills to consent that he be ours and that we be his, and so to sign the covenant of grace. Yet while they thus consent, and would give a world to be sure that Christ was theirs, and to be perfectly holy, yet they think they do not believe, because they do not believe that he will forgive or save them.

14. They are always displeased and discontented with themselves; just as a peevish, contrary person is apt to be with others: see one that is hard to please, and is finding fault with everything that he sees or hears, and offended at everyone that comes in his way, and suspicious of everybody that he sees whispering; and just so is a melancholy person against himself; suspecting, and displeased and finding fault with all.

15. They are much given to solitude, and weary of company for the most part.

16. They are given up to fixed musings, and long, poring thoughts to little purpose: so that deep musings and thinkings are their chief employments, and much of their disease.

17. They are much averse to the labors of their callings, and given to idleness; either to lie in bed, or sit thinking unprofitably by themselves.

18. Their thoughts are most upon themselves, like the millstones that grind on themselves when they have no grain: so one thought begets another: their thoughts are taken up about their thoughts: when they have been thinking irregularly, they think again what they have been thinking on: they do not meditate much on God (unless on his wrath), nor heaven, nor Christ, nor the state of the church, nor anything outside them (ordinarily); but all their thoughts are contracted and turned inwards on themselves: self-troubling is the sum of their thoughts and lives.

19. Their thoughts are all perplexed like tangled yarn or silk; or like a man in a maze, or wilderness, or that has lost himself and his way in the night: he is poring and groping about, and can make little of anything, but is bewildered, and muddled, and entangled the more; full of doubts and difficulties, out of which he cannot find the way.

20. He is endless in his scruples: afraid lest he sin in every word he speaks, and in every thought, and every look, and every meal he eats, and all the clothes he wears: and if he thinks to amend them, he is still scrupling his supposed amendments: he dares neither travel, nor stay at home, neither speak, nor be silent; but he is scrupling all; as if he were wholly composed of self-perplexing scruples.

21. From this it comes about that he is greatly given to superstition; to make many laws for himself that God never made him; and to ensnare himself with needless vows, and resolutions, and hurtful austerities; "Do not handle! Do not taste! Do not touch!"; and to place his religion much in such outward, self-imposed tasks; [314] to spend so many hours in this or that act of devotion; to wear such clothes, and go without others that are finer; to go without all food that pleases the appetite, with much of the like. A great deal of the perfection of popish devotion proceeded from melancholy, though their government comes from pride and greed.

22. They have lost the power of governing their thoughts by reason; so that if you convince them that they should cast out their self-perplexing, unprofitable thoughts, and turn their thoughts to other subjects, or be vacant, they are not able to obey you: they seem to be under a necessity or constraint; they cannot cast out their troublesome thoughts; they cannot turn away their minds; they cannot think of love and mercy; they can think of nothing but what they do think of, any more than a man with the toothache can keep from thinking of his pain.

23. They usually grow from this to an inability for any private prayer or meditation; their thoughts are at once cast all into a confusion when they should pray or meditate; they scatter abroad a hundred ways; and they cannot keep them upon anything: for this is the very point of their disease; a disordered, confused fantasy, with a weak reason which cannot govern it. Sometimes terror drives them from prayer; they dare not hope, and therefore dare not pray: and usually they dare not receive the Lord's supper; here they are most fearful of all; and if they do receive it, they are cast down with terrors, fearing that they have taken their own damnation, by receiving unworthily.

24. From this they grow to a great aversion to all holy duty: fear and despair make them go to prayer, hearing, reading, as a bear to the stake; and then they think they are haters of God and godliness, attributing the effects of their disease to their souls; when yet at the same time, those of them that are godly would rather be freed from all their sins, and be perfectly holy, than have all the riches or honor in the world.

25. They are usually so taken up with busy and earnest thoughts (which being all perplexed, only strive with themselves, and contradict one another) that they feel it just as if something were speaking within them, and all their own violent thoughts were the pleadings and impulse of some other; and therefore they are accustomed to attribute all their fantasies either to some extraordinary actions of the devil, or to some extraordinary promptings of the Spirit of God: and they are used to express themselves in such words as these, It was set upon my heart, or it was said to me, that I must do thus and thus; and then it was said, I must not do this or that; and I was told I must do so or so. ## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **Title:** "Title" is Baxter's word for a heading or division of the chapter ("_Tit._" in the source). Chapter 6 has six Titles; this file begins Title 5, addressed to the melancholy. It contains no numbered Directions yet: it is Baxter's preface to the Title, saying whom he means by "the melancholy," followed by his numbered list of signs, which runs past the end of this file (the 26th sign begins in the next file).
- **melancholy:** kept throughout (see GLOSSARY.md). Baxter defines it here himself: not serious, rational sorrow for sin, but a "diseased impairment, hurt, or error of the imagination, and consequently of the understanding." It overlaps with, but should not be equated with, any modern clinical diagnosis. His account belongs to the medicine of his day (see note 313 on "black bile").
- **Colossians 2:21 (sign 21; important):** Baxter quotes the King James, "touch not, taste not, handle not," and points in note 314 to Colossians 2:18-23. We use the NET wording of verse 21, "Do not handle! Do not taste! Do not touch!" The NET reverses the King James order (touch, taste, handle) and sets the words as three exclamations; it also places them in quotation marks as the slogans of the teachers Paul is rejecting. That suits Baxter's use (self-imposed rules that God never made), so the sense he leans on is kept, but the order of the three words is not his. The NET's verse 23, "self-imposed worship and humility achieved by an unsparing treatment of the body," is close to Baxter's "self-imposed tasks" and "hurtful austerities." The semicolon that follows the quotation is Baxter's.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording, not given references in his text): "the spirit of bondage" (Romans 8:15); Cain (Genesis 4); Pharaoh "given up to hardness of heart" (Exodus 7-14); "vessels of wrath fitted to destruction" (Romans 9:22, King James wording; the NET has "the objects of wrath prepared for destruction"); "eyes and do not see, ears and do not hear, hearts and do not understand" (compare Matthew 13:13-15; Mark 8:18; Isaiah 6:9-10); "taken their own damnation, by receiving unworthily" (1 Corinthians 11:27-29, where the King James has "eateth and drinketh damnation to himself"; the NET reads "eats and drinks judgment against himself"). These references are not in Baxter's text.
- **legal workings of the spirit of bondage** (paragraph 1): kept. "Legal" means "of the law": the convicting, fearful work of the law on the conscience before (or apart from) the comfort of the gospel, which Puritan writers often discussed as a stage in conversion. Baxter's complaint is that people mistake melancholy for this work of the Spirit.
- **hysterical women; the friars** (paragraph 1): kept as Baxter wrote. "Hysterical" reflects the medicine of his day; the remark about the friars' writings is his polemic against Rome, kept unchanged. **made public:** his "divulged." **dispossessing:** kept; casting out of supposed evil spirits.
- **impaired; impairment** (paragraph 1): his "crazed; craziness," in the older sense of cracked, broken, or impaired, not the modern slang. **fantasy:** kept, here and in signs 2, 23, and 25 ("fantasies"); in Baxter's psychology, the faculty that forms images, nearly the same as "imagination." **necessary:** his "requisite." **And the more so because:** his "And the rather because." **attributing:** his "imputing" (also signs 24, 25). **from there:** his "thence." **aware of:** his "sensible of." **anxious about:** his "solicitous about." **affections:** kept (see GLOSSARY.md). **every melancholy person:** the source prints "every melancholy persons," a misprint we have corrected.
- **magnifying** (sign 2): his "aggravating," in its older sense of making heavier or worse, not "annoying." **with dismay:** his "with amazement" (in its older sense of alarm or bewilderment).
- **always:** Baxter's "still" in its older sense of continually (signs 3, 6, 7, 14). In sign 7 ("they cry out still") and sign 20 ("he is still scrupling") we keep "still," which reads naturally in modern English and carries the same sense of continuing.
- **given to** (signs 3, 15, 21): his "addicted to," in the older sense of devoted or inclined to, without the modern sense of physical dependence. **strike them:** his "smite them." **done wrong:** his "done amiss."
- **supposing themselves forsaken by God** (sign 6): his "apprehending themselves forsaken of God." **undone:** kept (ruined, lost).
- **the day of grace** (sign 7): kept; the time in which God's offer of mercy is open to a person. **tenor:** kept; the general drift or content.
- **reprobated** (sign 8): kept as a theological term (rejected or passed over by God in predestination). **strongly imagine:** his "strongly conceit." **jointly:** his "conjunctly." Baxter's point is that election is to the means (faith and repentance) as well as to the end (salvation).
- **only read** (sign 9): his "do but read." **driven mad:** his "distracted" (in its older sense, out of one's mind). **Spira:** probably Francesco Spira, an Italian lawyer of the sixteenth century whose despair and death after recanting his Protestant faith were widely reported in English devotional literature; we have not confirmed which account Baxter means by "the ignorant author." His judgment of that author is his own.
- **a great many** (sign 10): his "abundance." **exactly:** his "just."
- **grasp** (sign 11): his "apprehend." **comforting:** his "comfortable." **threats:** his "threatenings." **keen feeling:** his "quick sense."
- **urging them to** (sign 12): his "putting them upon." **press them ever so hard:** his "put them never so hard." **make any conscience of it:** kept; to treat it as a matter of conscience.
- **sign** (sign 13): his "subscribe" (sign one's name to), used of the covenant of grace. **allow:** his "suffer" (see GLOSSARY.md). **because commonly:** his "because that commonly."
- **contrary** (sign 14): his "froward" (perverse, hard to deal with). **hard to please:** his "hard to be pleased."
- **solitude** (sign 15): his "solitariness."
- **grain** (sign 18): his "grist." **outside them:** his "without them."
- **perplexed** (signs 19, 25): kept; in Baxter's day it also meant entangled, which is the picture in sign 19. **tangled:** his "ravelled." **muddled:** his "moidered," a dialect word for confused or bewildered.
- **scruples; scrupling** (sign 20): kept; anxious doubts of conscience about whether an act is sinful.
- **popish** (sign 21): kept; Baxter's word for Roman Catholic, and his polemic, kept as written. **comes:** his "come" (an older plural or subjunctive form). **go without:** his "forbear" (twice). **food:** his "diet." **greed:** his "covetousness" (see GLOSSARY.md). **ensnare:** his "insnare." **From this it comes about:** his "Hence it comes to pass."
- **convince** (sign 22): kept, in the sense of persuade (see CONVENTIONS.md). **vacant:** kept; with the mind unoccupied. **keep from thinking:** his "forbear to think." **with the toothache:** his "in the tooth-ache."
- **from this** (signs 23, 24): his "hence." **inability for:** his "disability to." **at once:** his "presently" (see CONVENTIONS.md). **disordered:** his "distempered." **most fearful:** his "fearfulest." **the Lord's supper:** capitalization as in the source.
- **aversion** (sign 24): his "averseness." **as a bear to the stake:** kept; from bear-baiting, in which a bear was tied to a stake and set upon by dogs.
- **accustomed to** (sign 25): his "wont to." **actions:** his "actings." **promptings:** his "motions" (in its older sense of inward impulses). **only strive:** his "do but strive." The commas around Baxter's parenthesis have been dropped (light comma cleanup).
- **Footnote 312:** Baxter's cross-reference "part iii. against despair"; we have not identified which section of the *Directory* he means or checked it.
- **Footnote 313:** the Latin is Baxter's summary of the Stoic view as reported by Diogenes Laertius in his life of Zeno (probably book 7 of the *Lives of the Philosophers*); we have not checked the wording against the Greek text. "Black bile" (*atra bilis*) is the humor that the older medicine held to cause melancholy, which is where the word comes from.
- **Source ends mid-sentence:** the range ends at "saying to them all that"; the sentence continues in the next file ("they are thinking").
