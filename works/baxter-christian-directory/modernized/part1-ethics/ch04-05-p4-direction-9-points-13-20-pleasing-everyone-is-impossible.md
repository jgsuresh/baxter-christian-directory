# Chapter 4: Directions against Inordinate Man-pleasing, Direction 9, points 13-20

*This range begins partway through Direction 9 ("If nothing else will cure this disease, at least let the ..."), at its numbered sub-point 13, and ends at the end of sub-point 20; the next file continues the same Direction.*

13. Every man living shall unavoidably be engaged by God himself, in some duties which are very liable to misconstruction, and will have an outside and appearance of evil, to the offense of those that know not all the inside and circumstances. And hence it comes to pass, that a great part of history is little worthy of regard; because the actions of public persons are discerned only by halves by most that write of them. They write most by hearsay; or know but the outside and appearances of things, and not the spirit, and life, and reality of the case. Men do not have the choosing of their own duties, but God makes them by his law and providence: and it pleases him often to try his servants in this way: many of the circumstances of their actions shall remain unknown to men, that would justify them if they knew them, and account them as notorious, scandalous persons, because they know them not. How like to evil was the Israelites' taking the goods of the Egyptians! and how likely to lay them open to their censure! So was Abraham's attempt to sacrifice his son: and so was David's eating the showbread, and dancing almost naked before the ark; Christ's eating and drinking with tax collectors and sinners; Paul's circumcising Timothy, and purifying in the temple; with abundance of such like, which fall out in the life of every Christian. No wonder if Joseph thought once of putting Mary away, till he knew the evidence of her miraculous conception; and how liable was she to censure, by those that knew it not! Oh, therefore, how empty is the judgment of man! And how contrary is it frequently to the truth! And with what caution must history be read! And oh how desirable is the great day of God, when all human censure shall be justly censured!

14. The perversity of many is so great, that they require contradictions and impossibilities of you, to tell you that they are resolved never to be pleased by you. If John uses fasting, they say, "He has a demon": if Christ comes "eating and drinking," they say, "Look at him, a glutton and a drunk, a friend of tax collectors and sinners" (Matthew 11:18-19). If your judgment and practice are conformable to superiors, especially if they have admitted of a change, you shall be judged mere rascals and temporizers: if they are not, you shall be judged disobedient, stubborn, and seditious. If you speak fairly and pleasingly, they will call you flatterers and deceivers: if you speak more freely, though in a necessary case, they will say you rail. If I accept promotion, they will say, I am ambitious, proud, and worldly: if I refuse it, (however modestly,) they will say, I am discontented, and have seditious designs. If I do not preach when I am forbidden, I shall be accused of forsaking the calling I undertook, and obeying man against God: if I do preach, I shall be accounted disobedient and seditious. If a friend or kinsman asks me to help him to some place or promotion which he is not fit for, or which would tend to another's wrong; if I should grant his request, I shall be taken for dishonest, that by partiality wrong another; if I deny it to him, I shall be called unnatural or unfriendly, and worse than an unbeliever. If I give to the poor as long as I have it, I shall be censured for ceasing when I have no more: those that do not know whether you have it to give or not, will be displeased if you do not; and if many years you should maintain them freely, it is all as nothing as soon as you cease, either because your stock is spent, or because some other is made the necessary object of your charity. If you are wronged in your estate, if you go to law, they will say, you are contentious; if you let go your estate to avoid contention, they will say, you are silly fools or idiots. If you do any good works of charity to the knowledge of men, they will say, you are hypocrites, and do it for applause; if you do it secretly, that no one knows of it, they will say, you are greedy, and have no good works, and though you make a greater profession of religion, you do no good; and others shall be censured so also for your sakes. If you are pleasant and merry, they will censure you as light and empty: if you are more grave and sad, they will say, you are melancholy or discontent. In a word, whatever you do, be sure by some it will be condemned; and do or not do, speak or be silent, you shall certainly displease, and never escape the censures of the world.

15. There is among men so great an opposition of judgments, and dispositions, and interests, that they will never agree among themselves; and if you please one, the rest will be thereby displeased. [183] He that you please is an enemy to another; and therefore you displease his enemy by pleasing him. Sometimes, state differences divide kingdoms into parties, and one party will be displeased with you if you are of the other, and both if you are neutral, or dislike them both; and each party thinks their cause will justify any accusations they can charge you with, or odious titles they can give you, if not any sufferings they can bring upon you. Church differences and sects have been found in all ages, and you cannot be of the opinion of every party; when the world abounds with such variety of notions, you cannot be of all at once. And if you are of one party, you must displease the rest; if you are of one side in disputed opinions, the other side accounts you erroneous: and how far will the supposed interest of their cause and party carry them! One half of the Christian world, at this day, condemns the other half as schismatical at least, the other half doing the like for them. And can you be papists, and protestants, and Greeks, and everything? If not, you must displease as many as you please. Yes, more; if changeable men shall change ever so often, they will expect that you change as fast as they, and whatever their contrary interests require, you must follow them in; one year you must swear, and another you must unswear all again: whatever cause or action they engage in, be it ever so devilish, you must approve of it and support it, and all that they do you must say is well done. In a word, you must teach your tongue to say or swear anything, and you must sell your innocence, and hire out your consciences wholly to their service, or you cannot please them. Micaiah must say with the rest of the prophets, "Go, and prosper," or else he will be hated, as not prophesying good of Ahab, but evil (1 Kings 22:8). And how can you serve all interests at once? It seems the providence of God has, as if on purpose, wheeled about the affairs of the world, to try and shame man-pleasers and temporizers in the sight of the sun. It is evident then, that if you will please all you must at once both speak and be silent, and verify contradictions, and be in many places at once, and be of all men's minds, and for all men's way. For my part, I mean to see the world a little better agreed among themselves, before I will make it my ambition to please them. If you can reconcile all their opinions, and interests, and temperaments, and dispositions, and make them all of one mind and will, then hope to please them.

16. If you excel in any one virtue or duty, even that shall not excuse you from the contrary defamation, so unreasonable are malicious men. Nothing in the world can secure you from censorious, slanderous tongues. [184] The perfect holiness of Jesus Christ could not secure him from being called a gluttonous person and a drunkard, and a friend of tax collectors and sinners. His wonderful contempt of worldly dignities and honors, and his subjection to Caesar, could not secure him from being slandered and crucified as Caesar's enemy. The great piety of the ancient Christians did not excuse them from the common slander, that they met together for filthiness in the dark; nor from the cry of the rabble, _Tollite impios_, Away with the ungodly, because they were against the worshiping of idols. I have known those that have given all that ever they had to the poor except their food and necessaries, and yet (though it was to a considerable value) have been reproached as unmerciful, by those that have not had what they expected. Many a one has been defamed with scandalous rumors of uncleanness, that have lived in untainted chastity all their lives. The most eminent saints have been defamed as guilty of the most horrid crimes, which never entered into their thoughts. The principal thing that ever I bent my studies and care about, has been the reconciling, unity, and peace of Christians, and against quarrelsomeness, uncharitableness, turbulence, and division; and yet some have been found, whose interest and malice have commanded them to charge me with that very sin, which I have spent my days, my zeal, and study against. How often have contrary factions charged me with perfectly contrary accusations! I can scarcely remember the thing that I can do in all the world, that some will not be offended at; nor the duty so great and clear, that some will not call my sin; nor the self-denial so great, (to the hazard of my life,) which has not been called self-seeking, or something completely contrary to what it was indeed. Instead therefore of serving and pleasing this malicious, unrighteous world, I scorn their blind and unjust censures, and appeal to the most righteous God. [185]

17. If you have a design for a name of honor when you are dead, consider what power a successful faction may have to corrupt the history of your life, and represent you to posterity perfectly contrary to what you are; and how impossible it is for posterity to know whose history is the product of malicious, shameless lies, and whose is the narrative of impartial truth. What contrary histories are there of particular persons and actions written by men of the same religion: as of Pope Gregory VII and the emperors that contended with him; and about Pope Joan, and many like cases, where you may read scores of historians on one side and on the other. [186]

18. Remember that the holiest saints or apostles could never please the world, nor escape their censures, slanders, and cruelties; no, nor Jesus Christ himself. And can you think by honest means to please them better than Christ and all his saints have done? You do not have the wisdom that Christ had to please men, and to avoid offense. You do not have the perfect innocence and blamelessness that Christ had; you cannot heal their sicknesses and infirmities, and do that good to them to please and win them, as Jesus Christ did; you cannot convince them, and constrain them to reverence you by manifold miracles, as Jesus Christ did. Can you imitate such an excellent pattern as is set you by the holy, patient, charitable, tireless apostle Paul? (Acts 20; 1 Corinthians 4, 9; 2 Corinthians 4, 5, 6, 10, 11, 12.) If you cannot, how can you please them that would not be pleased by such inimitable works of love and power? The more Paul "loved" some of his hearers, "the less he was loved" (2 Corinthians 12:15). They used him "as an enemy for telling them the truth" (Galatians 4:16). Though he "became all things to all people," he could "save but some," nor "please but some" (1 Corinthians 9:22). And what are you that you should better please them?

19. Godliness, virtue, and honesty itself will not please the world, and therefore you cannot hope to please them by that which is not pleasing to them. Will men be pleased by that which they hate? and by the actions which they think accuse them and condemn them? And if you will be ungodly and vicious to please them, you sell your souls, your conscience, and your God, to please them. God and they are not pleased with the same ways. And which do you think should first be pleased? If you displease him for their favor, you will buy it dear. [187]

20. They are not pleased with God himself; indeed, no man does displease so many and so much as he. And can you do more than God to please them? or can you deserve their favor more than he? They are daily displeased with his works of providence: one would have rain, when another would have none; one would have the winds to serve his voyage, and another would have them in a contrary end; one party is displeased, because another is pleased and exalted; every enemy would have his cause succeed, and the victory to be his; every contender would have all go on his side. God must be ruled by them, and fit himself to the interest of the most unjust, and to the will of the most vicious, and do as they would have him, and be a servant to their lusts, or they will not be pleased with him. And his holy nature, and his holy word, and holy ways, displease them more than his ordinary providence. They are displeased that his word is so precise and strict, and that he commands them so holy and so strict a life, and that he threatens all the ungodly with damnation: he must alter his laws, and make them more loose, and fit them to their fleshly interest and lusts, and speak as they would have him, without any difficulties, before they will be pleased with them (unless he alter their minds and hearts). And how do you think they will be pleased with him at last, when he fulfills his threatenings? when he kills them, and turns their bodies to dust, and their guilty souls to torment and despair?

Scultet. Curric. p. 46.

[186] Fama liberrima principum judex. Seneca in Consolat. ad Martiam. (English: Reputation is the freest judge of princes. Seneca, in the Consolation to Marcia.)

[187] Aristides having got the surname of Just, was hated by the Athenians, who decreed to banish him: and every one that voted against him being to write down his name, a rustic that could not write came to Aristides to ask him to write down Aristides' name. He asked him whether he knew Aristides; and the man answered, No; but he would vote against him because his name was Just. Aristides concealed himself, did what the man asked, and wrote his own name in the roll and gave it him: so easily did he bear it to be condemned of the world for being just. Plutarch in Aristide. It was not only Socrates that was thus used, says Laertius, Nam Homerum velut insanientem drachmis quinquaginta mulctarunt, Tyrtæumque mentis impotem dixerunt, &c.--(English, rendered loosely: For they fined Homer fifty drachmas as if he were out of his mind, and said that Tyrtaeus was not in control of his mind, and so on.) Then, after a dash in Baxter's text: "Which of the prophets have not your fathers persecuted?" Matthew 23.

## Translator's notes

- **Place in the argument:** this file continues Direction 9 ("If nothing else will cure this disease ...") from its sub-point 13; the Direction's heading is in an earlier file and is not repeated here. The numbered sub-points are Baxter's own.
- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible, fetched from labs.bible.org; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). Verse texts quoted below are the NET wording.
- **Matthew 11:18-19 (important):** Baxter has "If John use fasting ... he hath a devil." The NET has "John came neither eating nor drinking, and they say, 'He has a demon!'" The NET says nothing of "fasting" itself; Baxter's "uses fasting" is his own summary. NET "demon" replaces the King James "devil." Baxter's "gluttonous person, and a wine-bibber" is NET "a glutton and a drunk," and "publicans" is NET "tax collectors." The NET's exclamation marks are dropped inside the quotation so that Baxter's colon and sentence stay as he had them. The same Gospel allusion in sub-point 16 (unquoted) is modernized the same way, with "wine-bibber" rendered "drunkard."
- **1 Kings 22 (Micaiah):** Baxter cites verse 8 and quotes "Go, and prosper." His wording is kept. The NET of 22:8 has the king of Israel saying he despises Micaiah "because he does not prophesy prosperity for me, but disaster," which fits Baxter's "not prophesying good of Ahab, but evil" (his paraphrase; the NET does not name Ahab in this verse). The quoted words are not in verse 8. The NET of 22:12 reads "Attack Ramoth Gilead! You will succeed; the Lord will hand it over to the king," and 22:15 has Micaiah answering "Attack! You will succeed; the Lord will hand it over to the king." So "Go, and prosper" is Baxter's own rendering of the prophets' words, not the NET wording. The NET loses the single word "prosper" that Baxter's phrase turns on, though "succeed" carries the same sense.
- **2 Corinthians 12:15:** Baxter's quoted words "loved" and "the less he was beloved" are his paraphrase of the King James; "beloved" is changed to "loved" (modern "beloved" means "dear one"). The NET reads: "If I love you more, am I to be loved less?" Baxter turns Paul's rhetorical question into a statement of fact about Paul's experience. His wording is kept and flagged.
- **Galatians 4:16:** Baxter's "as an enemy for telling them the truth" is a paraphrase (third person). The NET reads: "have I become your enemy by telling you the truth?" Baxter's wording is kept in his quotation marks.
- **1 Corinthians 9:22:** The NET reads "I have become all things to all people, so that by all means I may save some." Baxter's quoted words "became all things to all men" are kept in his extent, with "men" modernized to "people" as the NET has it. His "save but some" and "please but some" go beyond the verse: the "but" and the "please" are his, and the verse itself speaks only of saving. His claim that Paul could "please but some" is flagged as his gloss, not Paul's words. (He cites 1 Corinthians 9:22 only.)
- **Cited only:** Acts 20; 1 Corinthians 4, 9; 2 Corinthians 4, 5, 6, 10, 11, 12; Acts 6:15 and 7:55-60 (note 184); "Matthew 23" (note 187).
- **Note 187, "Which of the prophets have not your fathers persecuted?":** Baxter gives "Matt. xxiii." The words closely match the question in Acts 7:52 (NET: "Which of the prophets did your ancestors not persecute?"). Matthew 23:31 (NET) has "you are descendants of those who murdered the prophets," which is the same idea but not the same words. We have kept Baxter's reference and wording and only flag the doubt; we have not changed the reference.
- **Unmarked allusions (kept in Baxter's wording):** the Israelites taking the Egyptians' goods (compare Exodus 12:35-36); Abraham and Isaac (Genesis 22); David and the showbread (compare 1 Samuel 21:1-6) and dancing before the ark (compare 2 Samuel 6:14-16); Christ eating with tax collectors and sinners; Paul circumcising Timothy (compare Acts 16:3) and purifying in the temple (compare Acts 21:26); Joseph and Mary (compare Matthew 1:18-19); "worse than an infidel" (compare 1 Timothy 5:8); Christ slandered and crucified as Caesar's enemy (compare John 19:12). These references are ours, not in Baxter's text.
- **Word swaps (Baxter's word, then ours):** by the halves, only by halves; seemings, appearances; shew-bread, showbread; publicans, tax collectors; vain (empty), empty (13, 14); knaves, rascals; refractory, stubborn; dissemblers, deceivers; preferment, promotion; covetous, greedy; infidel, unbeliever; contrariety, opposition; neuters, neutral; conceits, notions; mutable, changeable; countenance (support), support; complexions, temperaments; innocency, innocence; unblamableness, blamelessness; unimitable, inimitable; unwearied, tireless; vulgar calumny, common slander; unpeaceableness, quarrelsomeness; turbulency, turbulence; contemn, scorn; prevailing, successful; scarce, scarcely; worshipping, worshiping; saith, says; desire (ask), ask, and fulfilled the man's desire, did what the man asked (note 187); "as it had been," "as if it had been" (note 184); clean contrary, completely contrary; controverted, disputed; "as of purpose," "as if on purpose." "Yea" was also modernized in place ("Yea, more" became "Yes, more"; "yea" became "indeed").
- **Kept as Baxter's terms:** **temporizers** (people who shift their position to suit the times or the powers of the day); **papists** (his word for Roman Catholics, here set beside "protestants, and Greeks," probably the Eastern Churches); **seditious** and **schismatical**; **rail** (to speak abusively); **melancholy** (see GLOSSARY.md). His remarks on the divisions of the Christian world are his own.
- **"verify contradictions"** (15): left as written; Baxter means that to please everyone you would have to make contradictory things true at once.
- **"in a contrary end"** (20): left as printed; it seems to mean "to a contrary end," that is, a contrary purpose or direction, but we have not changed it.
- **"light and vain"** (14) is rendered "light and empty," meaning frivolous.
- **Pope Gregory VII and Pope Joan** (17): Baxter's examples of historians contradicting one another. We add no claim about either; he gives no references in the text.
- **Latin in the body:** "_Tollite impios_" is kept as printed with Baxter's own English following ("Away with the ungodly").
- **Latin, note 185:** the Latin is kept as printed. Baxter's "Laert." is read as Diogenes Laertius (a probable reading; we give "Laertius"); "Socrat." as Socrates. Page numbers are Baxter's and are not checked.
- **Latin, note 186:** "Fama" here is public reputation or report; the draft translation "reputation" is a loose reading.
- **Latin, note 187:** the Greek and Roman names (Aristides, Plutarch, Homer, Tyrtaeus) are as Baxter has them; "mulctarunt" is a form of "mulctare" (to fine). Baxter's "clown" is rendered "rustic" (an unlettered country person). Baxter's "decreed to banish him" and "voted against him" describe the Athenian ostracism, as he tells it from Plutarch; we have not checked it.
- **Note 183:** Heidelberg, Pitiscus, "Irenicon," "Scultet. Curric.": names and abbreviations left as printed; we have not identified the book beyond what Baxter gives. "the reformed" is capitalized as "the Reformed" (the Protestant churches, probably those of the Calvinist tradition).
- **Misprint in the source:** none seen.
