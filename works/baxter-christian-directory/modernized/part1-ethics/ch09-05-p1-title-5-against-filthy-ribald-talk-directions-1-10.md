# Chapter 9: Title 5, Against Filthy and Ribald Talk (Directions 1–10 and Two Objections)

**Title 5. Special directions against filthy, ribald, scurrilous talk.**

**Direction 1. The chief direction against this filthy sin is general; to get out of a graceless state, and get a heart that fears God, and then you dare not be guilty of such impudence: God is not so despised by those that fear him.**

**Direction 2. Do not cease your holy communion with God in his worship, especially in secret, and do not be strange to him, and seldom with him.**

And then you dare not so pollute those lips that are accustomed to speak seriously to God. What! talk of lust and filthiness with that tongue that spoke but even now to the most holy God! God's name and presence will awe you, and cleanse you, and show you that his temple should not be so defiled, and that he has not called you to uncleanness but to holiness; and that a filthy tongue is unsuitable to the holy praise of God: but while the rest of your life is nothing but serving the devil and the flesh, no wonder if ribaldry seems a fit language for you.

**Direction 3. Cleanse your hearts of emptiness and filthiness; and then your tongues will be more clean.**

It is an empty or unchaste heart that makes an unchaste tongue.

**Direction 4. Remember what a shame it is to open and proclaim that filthiness of your heart which you might have concealed.**

Christ tells us how to interpret your words, that your mouth speaks from what fills your heart (Luke 6:45). And what need have you to tell people that it is the rutting season with you? and that lust and filthiness are the inhabitants of your mind? If you are not so far past all shame as to commit fornication in the open streets, why will you talk of it there?

**Direction 5. Remember that filthy talk is but the approach to filthy acts.**

It is but your breaking the shell of modesty, that you may eat the kernel of the vomiting nut. This is the tendency of it, whether you intend it or not. Can you be offended with him that believes you do that villainy in secret which you talk of openly? or that takes you to be preparing yourself for a whore? If the deed is bad, your making a jest of it cannot be good.

**Direction 6. Remember that you bid defiance to godliness and honesty: an "unwholesome word" grieves the Spirit of God (Ephesians 4:29, 30; 5:4).**

Can you expect that the Holy Spirit should dwell and work in so filthy a room, and with such filthy company? Dare you go pray or read the Scripture, or speak of any holy thing, with those lips that talk of filthy ribaldry? Do you find yourself fit to go to prayer after such talk? Or rather, do you not allow all that hear you to think that you renounce God and godliness, and never use any serious worship of God at all? And if you do claim to worship him with that filthy tongue, what can you expect in answer to your prayers, but a vengeance worse than Nadab and Abihu's (Leviticus 10:1–3)? "Shall sweet water and bitter come from the same fountain?" (James 3:11). Do you bless God, and talk filthily with the same tongue? and think he will not be avenged on your hypocrisy?

**Direction 7. Consider how you bid defiance also to common civility.**

You do that which civil heathens would be ashamed of; as if you had a design to reduce England to the customs of cannibals and savages in America, that go naked, and are past shame.

**Direction 8. Observe what service you do the devil, for the corrupting of others; [548] as if he had hired you to be a tutor in his academy, or one of his preachers, to draw the minds of the hearers from modesty, and prepare them for the brothels.**

Especially people can scarcely have more dangerous wildfire cast into their imaginations than by hearing rotten, filthy talk. And will you be one of Venus's priests?

**Direction 9. Remember how little need there is of your effort.**

Are not lust and filthiness so natural, and the minds of all unsanctified and uncleansed ones so prone to it, that they need no tutor, nor instigator, nor pander to their lusts? This fire is easily kindled; the bellows of your scurrility are needless to make such gunpowder burn.

**Direction 10. At once lament before God and man the filthiness that your tongue has been guilty of, and wash heart and tongue in the blood of Christ; and flee from the company and conversation of the obscene, as you would do from a plague house, or any infectious, pestilential air.**

And if you hear such rotten talk, reprove it, or be gone, and let them see that you hate it, and fear God.

*Objection.* But, says the filthy mouth, I mean no harm; may we not jest and be merry?

*Answer.* What! have you nothing to jest with but dung, and filth, and sin, and the defilement of souls, and the offending of God? Would you be unclean before the king, or cast dung in men's faces, and say, I mean no harm, but am in jest?

*Objection.* But, says he, those that are so demure are as bad in secret, and worse than we.

*Answer.* What! is a chaste tongue a sign of an unchaste life? Then you may as equally take a meek and quiet tongue to be a sign of an angry man; or a lying tongue to be a sign of a true man. Would the king take that excuse from you, if you talk treason openly, and say, Those that do not are yet in secret as bad as I? I think he would not take that for an excuse.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md).
- **Title:** "Tit." in the source is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," his word for a heading or division of the chapter. Chapter 9 ("Directions for the Government of the Tongue") has six Titles; this file begins Title 5, against filthy talk. Baxter's heading reads "Filthy, Ribald, Scurrilous Talk": "ribald" means coarse and indecent; "scurrilous" (and "scurrility" in Direction 9) means coarsely joking or abusive.
- **Luke 6:45** (Direction 4): Baxter gives the verse as indirect speech, in the King James wording "out of the abundance of the heart his mouth speaketh." We use the NET wording, "his mouth speaks from what fills his heart," put into the second person as Baxter has it ("your mouth ... your heart"). The sense he relies on (words show what is in the heart) is the same. "interpret" is Baxter's "expound."
- **Ephesians 4:29–30; 5:4** (Direction 6): Baxter quotes the King James "corrupt communication" (4:29). The NET has "unwholesome word," which we use, adding "an" for grammar. The King James "corrupt" fits his theme of rotten, filthy talk a little more pointedly than "unwholesome," but the sense is close. Verse 30 (NET) reads "And do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God"; Baxter's "grieves the Spirit of God" is his summary of verses 29–30. Ephesians 5:4 (NET) names "vulgar speech, foolish talk, or coarse jesting," which is exactly his subject; he cites it but does not quote it.
- **James 3:11** (Direction 6): Baxter's "Shall sweet water and bitter come from the same fountain?" is his own free version of the verse (the King James has "Doth a fountain send forth at the same place sweet water and bitter?"). We keep his wording, modernized. The NET reads: "A spring does not pour out fresh water and bitter water from the same opening, does it?" The point is unchanged. His following question ("Do you bless God, and talk filthily with the same tongue?") echoes James 3:9–10 (unmarked allusion; reference not in Baxter's text).
- **Leviticus 10:1–3** (Direction 6): cited, not quoted. Nadab and Abihu, sons of Aaron, offered "strange fire" (NET) before the Lord and were consumed by fire from his presence.
- **Unmarked allusions:** "he has not called you to uncleanness but to holiness" (Direction 2) echoes 1 Thessalonians 4:7; "his temple" (Direction 2) echoes 1 Corinthians 3:16–17 and 6:19. References not in Baxter's text.
- **Note 548** (Direction 8): a bare reference. The NET reads: Do not be deceived: "Bad company corrupts good morals."
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): "impudency" to "impudence"; "use to speak" to "are accustomed to speak"; "vanity" to "emptiness," and "vain" to "empty" (Direction 3; see the Glossary: vain means empty, worthless, frivolous, not conceited); "rutting-moon" to "rutting season" (the mating season of animals in heat); "villany" to "villainy"; "pretend" to "claim"; "discourse" to "talk"; "the Holy Ghost" to "the Holy Spirit"; "stews" to "brothels"; "fantasies" to "imaginations" (the faculty of imagination, not daydreams); "endeavour" to "effort"; "presently" to "at once"; "fly" to "flee"; "converse" to "conversation" (social dealings); "pest-house" to "plague house" (a house where victims of plague or other infectious disease were isolated); "I think no harm" to "I mean no harm"; "I trow" to "I think."
- **honesty** (Direction 6): kept. In Baxter's English, "honesty" often meant decency and chastity as well as truthfulness, and that is probably the leading sense here, next to "modesty" in Direction 5.
- **the vomiting nut** (Direction 5): kept as Baxter wrote it. This is probably the *nux vomica*, a poisonous seed (the source of strychnine) whose Latin name means "vomiting nut." His image: filthy talk breaks the shell of modesty so as to get at a poisonous kernel.
- **Venus's priests** (Direction 8): Venus was the Roman goddess of love and sexual desire; Baxter's phrase means promoters of lust.
- **cannibals and savages in America, that go naked** (Direction 7): Baxter's own words and his period's view of the native peoples of the Americas, kept as he wrote them.
- **whore** (Direction 5) and **dung** (Answer to the first Objection): Baxter's blunt words, kept as he wrote them.
- **"But, says the filthy mouth" / "says he"**: Baxter's way of introducing the objector; kept.
