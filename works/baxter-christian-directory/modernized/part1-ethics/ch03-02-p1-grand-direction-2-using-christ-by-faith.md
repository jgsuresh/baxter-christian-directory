# Chapter 3: Grand Direction II (part 1 of 2)

**Grand Direction 2. Diligently labor in that part of the life of faith which consists in the constant use of Christ as the means of the soul's access to God, acceptance with him, and comfort from him: and do not think of coming to the Father, but by him.**

To talk and boast of Christ is easy, and to use him for the increase of our carnal security, and boldness in sinning: but to live in the daily use of Christ to those ends of his office, to which he is by us to be made use of, is a matter of greater skill and diligence than many self-esteeming professing Christians are aware of. What Christ himself has done, or will do, for our salvation, is not directly the thing that we are now considering; but what use he requires us to make of him in the life of faith. He has told us that his flesh is food indeed, and his blood is drink indeed; and that unless we eat his flesh and drink his blood, we have no life in us. Here is our use of Christ, expressed by eating and drinking his flesh and blood, which is by faith. [83] The general parts of the work of redemption, Christ has himself performed for us without asking our consent, or imposing upon us any condition on our part, without which he would not do that work: as the sun illuminates and warms the earth whether it will or not, and as the rain falls on the grass without asking whether it consents, or will be thankful; so Christ, without our consent or knowledge, took our nature, and fulfilled the law, and satisfied the offended Lawgiver, and merited grace, and conquered Satan, death, and hell, and became the glorified Lord of all: but for the exercise of his graces in us, and our advancement to communion with God, and our living in the strength and joys of faith, he is himself the object of our duty, even of that faith which we must daily and diligently exercise upon him: and thus Christ will profit us no further than we make use of him by faith. It is not a forgotten Christ that objectively comforts or encourages the soul; but a Christ believed in, and skillfully and faithfully used to that end. It is objectively (principally) that Christ is called our wisdom (1 Corinthians 1:30). The knowledge of him, and the mysteries of grace in him, is the Christian or divine philosophy or wisdom, in opposition to the empty philosophy which the learned pagans boasted of. And therefore Paul determined to know nothing but Christ crucified, that is, to make a display of no other knowledge, and to glory in nothing but the cross of Christ, and so to preach Christ as if he knew nothing else but Christ. See 1 Corinthians 1:23; 2:2; Galatians 6:14. And it is objectively that Christ is said to dwell in our hearts by faith (Ephesians 3:17). Faith keeps him still upon the heart by continual thought, application, and use: as a friend is said to dwell in our hearts, whom we continually love and think of.

Christ himself teaches us to distinguish between faith in God (as God) and faith in himself (as Mediator): John 14:1, "Do not let your hearts be distressed. You believe in God;" (or, do you believe in God?) "believe also in me." These set together are the sufficient cure of a troubled heart. [84] It is not faith in God as God, but faith in Christ as Mediator, that I am now to speak of; and that not as it is inherent in the understanding, but as it is operative on the heart and in the life: and this is not the smallest part of the life of faith, by which the just are said to live. Every true Christian must in his measure be able to say, with Paul, "I have been crucified with Christ, and it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me. So the life I now live in the body, I live because of the faithfulness of the Son of God, who loved me and gave himself for me" (Galatians 2:20). The pure Godhead is the beginning and the end of all; but Christ is "the image of the invisible God, the firstborn over all creation, for all things in heaven and on earth were created in him—all things, whether visible or invisible, whether thrones or dominions, whether principalities or powers—all things were created through him and for him. He himself is before all things, and all things are held together in him. He is the head of the body, the church, as well as the beginning, the firstborn from the dead, so that he himself may become first in all things" (Colossians 1:15–18). In him it is that "you who used to be far away have been brought near by the blood of Christ. For he is our peace ... to reconcile them both in one body to God through the cross, by which the hostility has been killed. And he came and preached peace to you who were far off and peace to those who were near, so that through him we both have access in one Spirit to the Father. So then you are no longer foreigners and noncitizens, but you are fellow citizens with the saints and members of God's household" (Ephesians 2:13, 14, 16–19). "In him" it is that "we have boldness and confident access to God by way of Christ's faithfulness" (Ephesians 3:12). He is "the way, and the truth, and the life"; "No one comes to the Father except through me" (John 14:6). It is "by the blood of Jesus" that "we have confidence" (and liberty) "to enter the sanctuary ... by the fresh and living way that he inaugurated for us through the curtain, that is, through his flesh." Because "we have a great priest over the house of God," we may "draw near with a sincere heart in the assurance that faith brings," etc. (Hebrews 10:19–22). By him it is that we have "obtained access into this grace in which we stand, and we rejoice in the hope of God's glory" (Romans 5:1–2). So that we must have "all our communion with God through him."

Supposing what I have said of this subject in my *Directions for a Sound Conversion*, Direction 5, (which I hope the reader will read carefully,) I shall here briefly name the uses which we must make of Christ by faith, in order to our holy fellowship with God: [85] but I must tell you that it is a doctrine which requires a prepared heart, that has life within to enable it to relish holy truth, and to dispose it to diligence, delight, and constancy in practice. An unfeeling reader will feel but little savor in it, and a sluggish reader who allows it to die as soon as it has touched his ears or imagination, will fall short of the practice and the pleasure of this life. He must have faith who will live by faith: and he must have the heart and nature of a child, who will take pleasure in loving, reverent, and obedient fellowship with a father.

1. The darkness of ignorance and unbelief is the great impediment of the soul that desires to draw near to God. When it does not know God, or does not know man's capacity of enjoying him, and how much he regards the heart of man; or does not know by what way he must be sought and found; or when he doubts the certainty of the word which declares the duty of the hopes of man: all this, or any of this, will suppress the ascending desires of the soul, and clip its wings, and break the heart of its holy aspirations after God, by killing or weakening the hopes of its success.

Here then make use of Jesus Christ, the great Revealer of God and his will to the blinded world, and the great Confirmer of the divine authority of his word. Life and immortality are brought more fully to light by the gospel, than ever they were by any other means. Moses and the prophets did bring with their doctrine sufficient evidence of its credibility. But Christ has brought both a fuller revelation, and a fuller evidence to help belief. An inspired prophet, who proves his inspiration to us, is a credible messenger: but when God himself shall come down into flesh, and associate with man, and teach him the knowledge of God, and the way to life, and tell him the mysteries of the world to come, and seal his testimony with unquestionable proofs, who will not learn from such a Teacher? and who will deny belief to such a Messenger, except absurd, unreasonable men? Remember, then, when ignorance or unbelief would hinder your access to God, that you have the ablest Teacher and the surest Witness to acquaint you with God in all the world. If God had sent an angel from heaven, to tell you what he is, and what he requires of you, and what he will do for you, would it not be very acceptable to you? But he has done much more; he has sent his Son: [86] the Deity itself has appeared in flesh: he who has seen God, and he who is God, has come among men to acquaint them with God. His testimony is more sure and credible than any angel's. Hebrews 1:1–3, "After God spoke long ago in various portions and in various ways to our ancestors through the prophets, in these last days he has spoken to us in a son." John 1:18, "No one has ever seen God. The only one, himself God, who is in closest fellowship with the Father, has made God known." We have "never heard his voice nor seen his form" (John 5:37). "(Not that anyone has seen the Father except the one who is from God—he has seen the Father)" (John 6:46). "No one knows the Father except the Son and anyone to whom the Son decides to reveal him" (Matthew 11:27). What more can we desire, that is short of the sight of the glory of God, than to have him revealed to us by a messenger from heaven, and such a messenger as himself has seen him, and is God himself? Plato and Plotinus may describe God to us according to their dark conjectures; something we may discern of him by observing his works; but Christ has declared what he saw, and what he knew, beyond all possibility of mistake. And lest his own testimony should seem questionable to us, he has confirmed it by a life of miracles, and by rising from the dead himself, and ascending visibly to heaven; and by the Holy Spirit, and his miraculous gifts, which he gave to the messengers of his gospel. Had it been no more than his resurrection from the dead, it would have been enough to prove the utter unreasonableness of unbelief.

2. It is also a great impediment to the soul in its approach to God, that infinite distance disables us from conceiving of him rightly. We say, as Elihu, "God is great—beyond our knowledge!" (Job 36:26). And, indeed, it is impossible that mortal man should have any adequate apprehensions of his essence. But in his Son he has come down to us, and shown himself in the clearest mirror that ever revealed him. Think of him therefore as he appeared in our flesh; as he showed himself in his holiness and goodness to the world. You may have positive thoughts of Jesus Christ; though you may not think that the Godhead was flesh, yet you may think of it as it appeared in flesh. It may quiet the understanding to conceive of God as incarnate, and to know that we cannot yet know him as he is, or have any adequate conceptions of him. These may delight us till we reach to more.

3. It hinders the soul's approach to God, when the infinite distance makes us think that God will not regard or take notice of such contemptible worms as we: we are ready to think that he is too high for our fellowship or delight. In this case the soul has no such remedy, as to look to Christ; and see how the Father has regarded us, and set his heart upon us, and sent his Son to seek and save us. Oh wonderful, astonishing condescension of eternal love! Believe that God assumed flesh to make himself familiar with man; and you can never question whether he regards us, or will hold communion with us.

4. It hinders our comfortable access to God, when we are deterred by the glory of his infiniteness and majesty. As the eye is not able to gaze upon the sun, unless it is overshadowed; so the soul is afraid of the majesty of God, and overwhelmed by it, when it should be delighted in it. Against this there is no such remedy, as to behold God appearing to us in his Son, where his majesty is veiled, and where he approaches us familiarly in our nature, to invite us to him with holy confidence and reverent boldness. Christ did not appear in a terrible form: women dared talk with him; beggars, and cripples, and diseased people dared ask his help; sinners dared eat with him: the proud scorned him, but the lowly were not frightened from him. He "emptied himself by taking on the form of a slave," that he might associate familiarly with the lowliest, and those of no reputation. Though we may not debase the Godhead, to imagine that it is humbled in glory, as it was on earth, in the flesh of Christ; yet this condescension is unspeakable encouragement to the soul to come with boldness to God, that was frightened from him.

5. When the guilt of sin frightens us from God, and we are thinking that God will not accept such great offenders as we have been, then Christ is our remedy, who has paid our debt, and borne our stripes, and procured and sealed us a pardon by his blood. [87] Shall pardoned sins drive us from him who pardons them? He has justified us by his righteousness. The curse and damnation are terrible indeed; but he has taken them away, and given us a free discharge.

6. The weaknesses also of our souls in duty, are often a great discouragement to us, in our approaches to the most holy, jealous God. To find so little knowledge of God, so little love to him, such cold desires, such wandering and distracted thoughts, such dull requests: it is hard to have lively and thankful apprehensions of God's acceptance of such defective, lame meditations or prayers; but we are apt to think that he will abhor both them and us, and that he can take no pleasure in them, yes, that it is as good not to pray at all. Here faith has full relief in Christ: two things it can say from him to encourage the fearful soul: (1.) That our acceptance with the Father is through the merits of his Son; and he is worthy, though we are unworthy. If we have but the worthiness of faith, and repentance, and sincere desire, Christ has the worthiness of perfect holiness and obedience for us. We do not go to the Father in our own names, but in his; and whatever we ask the Father in the name of Christ according to his will, he will give it to us (John 16:23; 14:13; 15:16). (2.) That all the weaknesses of our souls and services are forgiven us through Christ: he has undertaken to answer for them all, and to justify us from all such accusations. By faith you may, as it were, hear Christ speaking thus for your encouragement: Go boldly, poor sinner, into my Father's presence: do not fear the guilt of your sins, nor the imperfection of your prayers; as long as you truly repent of them, and desire to be delivered from them, and trust in me, I am your worthiness; my righteousness is perfect without spot; I have taken all your faults and failings upon me; I have undertaken to answer for all the imperfections of your holy things: sincerity is your endowment; perfection is mine: trust me in the performance of the trust which I have undertaken.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../../../NOTICE.md). All NET wording was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Galatians 2:20 (NET flag):** King James "the faith of the Son of God" (Baxter's wording, and the basis of his talk of faith in Christ) becomes in the NET "the faithfulness of the Son of God." The NET reading can be taken as Christ's own faithfulness, which shifts the sense Baxter leans on, namely our believing in Christ. The NET also omits Baxter's "nevertheless I live; yet not I," and has "in the body" where he had "in the flesh."
- **Ephesians 3:12 (NET flag):** King James "through faith in him" becomes "by way of Christ's faithfulness." Same shift as above; Baxter uses the verse for our faith as the way of access. The NET has "in whom" where Baxter's text has "In him it is that"; we kept his lead-in and quoted the NET after it.
- **Romans 5:1-2 (NET flag):** Baxter's "access by faith into this grace" has no "by faith" in the NET text of verse 2 (we have not checked whether the NET treats the missing words as a textual variant). His argument that access is by faith still stands from verse 1 ("declared righteous by faith"), which we did not quote. He printed "1, 2" but quotes only verse 2; we kept his reference.
- **Colossians 1:15-18:** Baxter printed "Col. i. 16-19," but his quotation begins at verse 15 and ends at verse 18 ("pre-eminence"), so we corrected the reference. NET "firstborn over all creation" for King James "first-born of every creature," and "first in all things" for "pre-eminence."
- **Ephesians 2:13, 14, 16-19:** Baxter's quotation is a loose blend of the King James, with his own gloss "both Jew and gentile" and "we who were sometime far off." We used NET wording for the extent he quotes, marked the skipped parts with "...", and did not carry over his glosses. The NET's "to reconcile them both in one body" is where his "Jew and gentile" explanation fits.
- **Hebrews 10:19-22:** NET "confidence" (Baxter "boldness"), "sanctuary" ("the holiest"), "curtain" ("vail"), "sincere heart" ("true heart"), "the assurance that faith brings" ("full assurance of faith"). "(and liberty)" is Baxter's own gloss, kept in parentheses. The NET word order differs, so his "by the blood of Jesus that we have boldness" is rearranged in quotation marks.
- **Hebrews 1:1-3:** Baxter's reference is "1-3" but his quotation ends at "by his Son" (in verse 2); we kept his reference and quoted only through verse 2. The NET reads "in a son" (lowercase), where Baxter has "by his Son."
- **John 1:18 (NET flag):** King James "the only-begotten Son ... in the bosom of the Father, he hath declared him" becomes "The only one, himself God, ... in closest fellowship with the Father, has made God known." The NET's "himself God" agrees with Baxter's next sentence ("he who is God"), but "only-begotten Son" is gone from the quoted text.
- **John 14:1:** The NET reads "You believe in God" as a statement. Baxter's parenthetical "(or, believe ye in God?)" offers another way to read it as a question, and he leaves the verse open between readings. We rendered it "do you believe in God?" We have not checked the NET's own footnote on this verse.
- **John 14:6:** Baxter turns the verse into third person ("He is..."). The NET has Jesus speaking, so we quoted the NET after "He is" and set the second part off separately.
- **John 5:37; 6:46; Matthew 11:27:** Baxter's quotations are fragments and loose; we used NET wording for the parts he draws on. In John 6:46 the NET puts the whole verse in parentheses. In Matthew 11:27 we start at "no one knows the Father," skipping the first half of the verse, as Baxter does.
- **Job 36:26:** NET "God is great—beyond our knowledge!" for King James "Behold, God is great, and we know him not." The sense is close, but the NET says God is beyond our knowledge, not that we do not know him. The attribution to Elihu is Baxter's.
- **Philippians 2:7 (NET flag; no reference in Baxter's text):** Baxter quotes "took upon him the form of a servant, and made himself of no reputation." The NET has "emptied himself by taking on the form of a slave." Baxter's next clause ("those of no reputation") leans on the King James phrase "of no reputation," which the NET does not use. We kept his clause as he wrote it.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording, no reference added): "his flesh is food indeed ... drink indeed ... we have no life in us" (John 6:53–55; Baxter's paraphrase, not a quotation; his "meat" is our "food"); "the just are said to live" (probably Habakkuk 2:4 / Romans 1:17, not confirmed); "Life and immortality are brought more fully to light by the gospel" (2 Timothy 1:10); "to seek and save us" (Luke 19:10); "borne our stripes" (Isaiah 53:5); "Moses and the prophets"; "all our communion with God through him" (a quotation with no source given; perhaps in the spirit of Ephesians 2:18, not confirmed).
- **John 16:23; 14:13; 15:16:** Baxter gives only the references with his paraphrase "whatever we ask the Father in the name of Christ"; we kept his paraphrase and converted the references.
- **1 Corinthians 1:30; Ephesians 3:17; 1 Corinthians 1:23; 2:2; Galatians 6:14:** Baxter gives references without quotation marks (paraphrase), so we kept his wording and converted the references.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): labour, labor; peruse, read carefully; affrighteth / frighted, frightens / frightened; meat, food; professors, professing Christians; heathens, pagans; vain, empty; ostentation, a display; cogitation, thought; improvement, use; fantasy, imagination; senseless (reader), unfeeling; savour, savor; suffereth, allows; infirmities, weaknesses; converse (noun/verb, meaning fellowship or association), fellowship / associate; glass, mirror; discourse (verb), talk; contemned, scorned; frighted, frightened; durst, dared; "Holy Ghost," Holy Spirit; "it had been enough," "it would have been enough."
- **Kept as Baxter's terms:** carnal security (confident carelessness; see CONVENTIONS); **objectively** (as the object of our knowledge and faith, as opposed to something working in us); **Mediator**; **apprehensions** (understanding or grasp; he uses it as a plain term for what the mind takes hold of); **jealous** (God's jealous love).
- **Source oddities:** "the duty of the hopes of man" (item 1) is Baxter's phrase, kept as written; it is a little unclear, probably meaning the duty that the word sets before man's hopes. In note [84], "heres" is printed (Latin for "heir"); the sense seems to call for a word like "hebes" (dull) or "hæreticus" (heretic), but we have not confirmed this and rendered it "[dull?]". "Dr. Boy's postil" (kept as printed): we have not identified the work or the author beyond Baxter's reference to Luther; "postil" probably means a set of comments on Scripture readings (not confirmed).
- **Paul. Scaliger, *Thes.*** (note 83): we have not identified the work; read as printed.
- **Eulogius** (note 85): Baxter's attribution (Eulogius of Toledo). **Didymus of Alexandria** (note 85): Baxter's attribution; we have not checked the passage.
- **Ambrose on 2 Corinthians 13:13** (note 86): Baxter's attribution and reference, kept as printed; we have not checked the passage or the verse numbering.
- **Acosta** (note 84): see Chapter 2, Direction 7 for the same author. Baxter's note is used here for the point that a person is baptized on a brief profession of faith.
- **Direction 5 of *Directions for a Sound Conversion*:** Baxter's cross-reference; not checked.
