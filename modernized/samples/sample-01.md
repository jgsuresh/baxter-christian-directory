# Samples: first pass at modernizing

Each sample shows Baxter's original followed by the modernized rendering, so
you can judge fidelity and voice side by side. The side-by-side format is for
review only; the final files will contain just the modernized text.

---

## Sample 1: The Introduction (Part 1, opening, paragraphs 1–3)

**Original** (lines ~1990–2010)

> The eternal God having made man an intellectual and free agent, able to
> understand and choose the good, and refuse the evil; to know, and love, and
> serve his Maker, and by adhering to him in this life of trial, to attain to
> the blessed sight and enjoyment of his glory in the life to come, hath not
> been wanting to furnish him with such necessaries, without which these ends
> could not successfully be sought. When we had lost our moral capacity of
> pleasing him, that we might enjoy him, he restoreth us to it by the
> wonderful work of our redemption. In Christ he hath reconciled the world
> unto himself; and hath given them a general act of oblivion, contained in
> the covenant of grace, which nothing but men's obstinate and final
> unwillingness can deprive them of.

**Modernized**

> The eternal God made human beings intelligent and free: able to understand
> and choose what is good, and to refuse what is evil. He made us to know,
> love, and serve our Maker, and by holding fast to him in this life of
> testing, to come at last to the blessed sight and enjoyment of his glory in
> the life to come. And he did not leave us without what we needed to pursue
> those ends. When we lost our moral ability to please him, and so to enjoy
> him, he restored it to us through the wonderful work of our redemption. In
> Christ he has reconciled the world to himself. He has granted a general
> pardon, written into the covenant of grace, and nothing can take it from
> people except their own stubborn and final unwillingness.

**Original**

> But so great is the blindness and obstinacy of men, that the greatest part
> refuse consent; being deceived by the pleasures, and profits, and honours
> of this present world; and make their pretended necessities or business the
> matter of their excuses, and the unreasonable reasons of their refusal,
> negligence, and delays, till death surprise them, and the door is shut; and
> they knock, and cry for mercy and admittance, when it is too late, Matt.
> xxv. 10-12.

**Modernized**

> But human blindness and stubbornness are so great that most people refuse.
> They are deceived by the pleasures, profits, and honors of this present
> world. They make their supposed necessities and their busyness the
> grounds for their excuses, the unreasonable reasons for their refusing,
> neglecting, and putting off, until death catches them by surprise and the
> door is shut. Then they knock and cry for mercy and admission, when it is
> too late (Matthew 25:10–12).

> **Notes:** "The greatest part" is Baxter's way of saying "the majority."
> "General act of oblivion" is a legal term for an amnesty. I rendered it as
> "general pardon" and kept the legal image. "Honours" is a standard British
> spelling in 1673; I used the American spelling per the style guide.

## Sample 2: Direction VII on delighting in God (Part 1, Ch. 3)

A sharper, more rhetorical passage. This tests whether the modernization
keeps Baxter's heat.

**Original** (lines ~18030–18062, abridged for this sample)

> _Direct._ VII. Corrupt not your minds and appetites with contrary
> delights.--Addict not yourselves to fleshly pleasures: taste nothing that is
> forbidden. Sorrow itself is not such an enemy to spiritual delights, as
> sensual, sinful pleasures are. O leave your beastly and your childish
> pleasures, and come and feast your souls on God, Isa. lv. 1-3. [...] Can it
> be any thing but the disease and wickedness of thy heart, that can make a
> play, or a feast, or drunken, wanton company, more pleasant to thee than
> God? What a heart is that which thinketh it a toil to meditate on God and
> heaven; and thinks it a pleasure to think of the baits of pride and
> covetousness! [...] It were not so vile for a child to delight more in a
> dog than in his parents, or a husband to delight more in the ugliest harlot
> than in his wife, as it is for a man to delight more in fleshly vanities
> than in God. Will you be licking up this dung, when you should be solacing
> your souls in angelical pleasures, and foretasting the delights of heaven?

**Modernized**

> **Direction 7. Don't spoil your mind and appetites with rival delights.**
> Don't give yourselves over to the pleasures of the flesh, and don't taste
> anything that is forbidden. Even sorrow is not as great an enemy to
> spiritual joy as sinful, sensual pleasures are. Leave behind your animal
> and childish pleasures, and come feast your souls on God (Isaiah 55:1–3).
> [...] What can it be but sickness and wickedness of heart that makes a
> play, or a party, or the company of drunken, loose people more pleasant to
> you than God? What kind of heart finds it a burden to meditate on God and
> heaven, yet finds it a pleasure to think about the bait of pride and
> greed? [...] It would not be as shameful for a child to love a dog more
> than his parents, or for a husband to take more delight in the ugliest
> prostitute than in his wife, as it is for a person to take more delight in
> worldly vanities than in God. Will you keep licking up this filth when you
> could be refreshing your souls with angelic pleasures and getting a
> foretaste of heaven's joys?

> **Notes:** Baxter's "thy" and "you" shift in this passage (he slips into
> "thy heart" when he gets personal). I normalized to "you." "Beastly" =
> "animal," which is the intended sense (pleasures we share with animals),
> and I avoided "beastly" as modern slang for "awful." "Dung" is a plain word
> for filth; "filth" is a milder choice and is easy to switch to "dung" if
> you want to keep the shock. See open question 2 below.

## Sample 3: Directions VIII–IX on melancholy and impatience (Part 1, Ch. 3)

This is the kind of passage biblical counselors cite. It also includes
dated language and a generalization about women that needs careful handling.

**Original** (lines ~18064–18100)

> _Direct._ VIII. Take heed of a melancholy habit of body; for melancholy
> people can scarce delight in any thing at all, and therefore not in God.
> Delight is as hard to them, as it is to a pained member to find pleasure,
> or a sick stomach to delight in the food which it loathes. They can think
> of God with trouble, and fear, and horror, and despair; but not with
> delight.
>
> _Direct._ IX. Take heed of an impatient, peevish, self-tormenting mind,
> that can bear no cross; and of overvaluing earthly things, which causeth
> impatience in the want of them. [...]
>
> Some women, and passionate, weak-spirited men, especially in sickness, are
> so peevish, and of such impatient minds, that their daily work is to
> disquiet and torment themselves. One can scarce tell how to speak to them,
> or look at them, but it offendeth them. And the world is so full of
> occasions of provocation, that such persons are like to have little
> quietness. It is unlike that these should delight in God, who keep their
> minds in a continual, ulcerated, galled state, incapable of any delights
> at all, and cease not their self-tormenting.

**Modernized**

> **Direction 8. Be careful about a melancholy bodily condition, for
> melancholy people can hardly find delight in anything, and so they cannot
> find it in God.** Delight is as hard for them as it is for an injured limb
> to feel pleasure, or for a sick stomach to enjoy food it can't stand. They
> can think of God with distress, fear, dread, and despair, but not with
> delight.
>
> **Direction 9. Guard against an impatient, irritable, self-tormenting
> mind that can't bear any hardship, and against overvaluing earthly things,
> which causes impatience when we lack them.** [...]
>
> Some women, and some emotional, weak-spirited men, especially when they are
> sick, are so touchy and so impatient that their daily work is to upset and
> torment themselves. You can hardly tell how to speak to them, or even look
> at them, without offending them. And the world is so full of things that
> provoke that people like this are unlikely to have any peace. It is
> unlikely that people who keep their minds in a constant raw, sore, rubbed
> state, unable to enjoy anything and never ceasing to torment themselves,
> will take delight in God.

> **Notes:**
> - "Melancholy habit of body": Baxter treats melancholy as partly bodily
>   (he wrote elsewhere about its physical roots; see his *Directions to the
>   Melancholy* referenced in the Part 1 text). I used "bodily condition" to
>   keep that sense and avoided "depression," which is a different
>   modern category. Open question 3.
> - "Passionate" here means "easily stirred up by emotion," not "enthusiastic."
> - "Unlike" in "It is unlike that these should delight" = "unlikely." I
>   kept the sentence's logic: his point is that self-torment crowds out
>   delight in God.
> - "Some women, and passionate, weak-spirited men": I kept both groups
>   faithfully. A modern reader may find "weak-spirited" harsh; it's Baxter's
>   judgment and I left it, with "emotional" for "passionate" to avoid the
>   wrong sense.

## Open questions for you

1. **Register.** These lean "plain modern American English" and keep his
   exclamations. Should I go lighter (closer to the original wording) or more
   freely (shorter sentences, more contemporary idiom)?
2. **Blunt words** ("dung," "harlot," "whore"): keep them as-is, soften
   slightly, or use accurate modern equivalents?
3. **Melancholy:** keep "melancholy" with a standing note, or translate
   (e.g., "a heavy, fearful frame of mind")? Counselors who cite Baxter often
   care about this one.
4. **Format:** Do you want Baxter's bold direction headings as I've done, or
   plain run-in text like the original?
5. **Scripture references:** I converted them to "Isaiah 55:1–3." Fine?
