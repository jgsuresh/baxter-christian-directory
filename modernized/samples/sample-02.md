# Samples, round 2: words only

Same passages as [sample-01](sample-01.md), but this time **only words are
modernized**. Sentence boundaries, punctuation, clause order, and
Baxter's lists are left alone. What changed:

- thou/thee/thy → you/your; -eth/-est endings → plain verbs
- archaic or shifted-meaning words → modern equivalents (each one noted)
- British spellings → American; Scripture references → "Isaiah 55:1–3" form
- small grammar glue forced by the above (for example "he hath" → "he has";
  "till death surprise them" → "until death surprises them")

Words I deliberately left alone: *whore*, *harlot*, *dung*, *covetousness*,
*wretches*, and other blunt or traditional terms that a modern reader still
understands. Baxter's wording is part of the point.

## Sample 1: The Introduction (Part 1, opening)

**Original**

> The eternal God having made man an intellectual and free agent, able to
> understand and choose the good, and refuse the evil; to know, and love, and
> serve his Maker, and by adhering to him in this life of trial, to attain to
> the blessed sight and enjoyment of his glory in the life to come, hath not
> been wanting to furnish him with such necessaries, without which these ends
> could not successfully be sought. When we had lost our moral capacity of
> pleasing him, that we might enjoy him, he restoreth us to it by the
> wonderful work of our redemption. In Christ he hath reconciled the world
> unto himself; and hath given them a general act of oblivion, contained in
> the covenant of grace, which nothing but men's obstinate and final
> unwillingness can deprive them of.

**Modernized**

> The eternal God having made man a rational and free agent, able to
> understand and choose the good, and refuse the evil; to know, and love, and
> serve his Maker, and by adhering to him in this life of trial, to attain to
> the blessed sight and enjoyment of his glory in the life to come, has not
> failed to provide him with such necessities, without which these ends could
> not successfully be sought. When we had lost our moral capacity of pleasing
> him, that we might enjoy him, he restores us to it by the wonderful work of
> our redemption. In Christ he has reconciled the world to himself; and has
> given them a general amnesty, contained in the covenant of grace, of which
> nothing but men's obstinate and final unwillingness can deprive them.

**Original**

> But so great is the blindness and obstinacy of men, that the greatest part
> refuse consent; being deceived by the pleasures, and profits, and honours
> of this present world; and make their pretended necessities or business the
> matter of their excuses, and the unreasonable reasons of their refusal,
> negligence, and delays, till death surprise them, and the door is shut; and
> they knock, and cry for mercy and admittance, when it is too late, Matt.
> xxv. 10-12.

**Modernized**

> But so great is the blindness and obstinacy of men, that the majority
> refuse consent; being deceived by the pleasures, and profits, and honors
> of this present world; and make their supposed necessities or business the
> matter of their excuses, and the unreasonable reasons of their refusal,
> neglect, and delays, until death surprises them, and the door is shut; and
> they knock, and cry for mercy and admittance, when it is too late (Matthew
> 25:10–12).

> **Word notes**
> - *intellectual* → *rational*: Baxter means a being that reasons and
>   chooses, not a scholarly type.
> - *hath not been wanting to furnish* → *has not failed to provide*.
> - *necessaries* → *necessities*.
> - *act of oblivion* → *amnesty*: this was the legal term for a pardon that
>   "forgets" past offenses (the 1660 Act of Oblivion was in living memory
>   when Baxter wrote).
> - *the greatest part* → *the majority*.
> - *pretended* → *supposed*: in his day "pretended" meant "alleged," not
>   "faked."
> - *negligence* → *neglect*.

## Sample 2: Direction VII on delighting in God (Part 1, Ch. 3), full text

**Original**

> _Direct._ VII. Corrupt not your minds and appetites with contrary
> delights.--Addict not yourselves to fleshly pleasures: taste nothing that is
> forbidden. Sorrow itself is not such an enemy to spiritual delights, as
> sensual, sinful pleasures are. O leave your beastly and your childish
> pleasures, and come and feast your souls on God, Isa. lv. 1-3. Away with the
> delights of lust, and pride, and covetousness, and vain sports, and
> gluttony, and drunkenness, if ever you would have the solid and durable
> delights! Think not of joining both together. Bethink yourselves: can it be
> any thing but the disease and wickedness of thy heart, that can make a
> play, or a feast, or drunken, wanton company, more pleasant to thee than
> God? What a heart is that which thinketh it a toil to meditate on God and
> heaven; and thinks it a pleasure to think of the baits of pride and
> covetousness! What a heart is that which thinks that sensuality,
> wantonness, and vanity are the pleasure of their families, which must not
> be turned out; and that godliness, and heavenly discourse and exercises,
> would be the sadness and trouble of their families, which must not be
> brought in, lest it mar their mirth; that thinks it an intolerable toil and
> slavery to love God, and holiness, and heaven, and to be employed for them;
> and thinks it a delightful thing to love a whore, or excess of meat, or
> drink, or sports! Can you say any thing of a man that is more disgraceful,
> unless you say he is a devil? It were not so vile for a child to delight
> more in a dog than in his parents, or a husband to delight more in the
> ugliest harlot than in his wife, as it is for a man to delight more in
> fleshly vanities than in God. Will you be licking up this dung, when you
> should be solacing your souls in angelical pleasures, and foretasting the
> delights of heaven? Oh how justly will God thrust away such wretches from
> his everlasting presence, who so abhor his ways and him! Can they blame him
> for denying them the things which they hate, or set so light by, as to
> prefer a lust before them? If they were not haters of God and holiness,
> they would never be so averse even to the delights which they should have
> with him.

**Modernized**

> **Direction 7.** Do not corrupt your minds and appetites with contrary
> delights.--Do not addict yourselves to fleshly pleasures: taste nothing
> that is forbidden. Sorrow itself is not such an enemy to spiritual
> delights, as sensual, sinful pleasures are. O leave your animal and
> childish pleasures, and come and feast your souls on God (Isaiah 55:1–3).
> Away with the delights of lust, and pride, and covetousness, and vain
> sports, and gluttony, and drunkenness, if ever you would have the solid and
> lasting delights! Do not think of joining both together. Consider
> carefully: can it be anything but the disease and wickedness of your
> heart, that can make a play, or a feast, or drunken, loose company, more
> pleasant to you than God? What a heart is that which thinks it a toil to
> meditate on God and heaven; and thinks it a pleasure to think of the baits
> of pride and covetousness! What a heart is that which thinks that
> sensuality, looseness, and vanity are the pleasure of their families,
> which must not be turned out; and that godliness, and heavenly
> conversation and exercises, would be the sadness and trouble of their
> families, which must not be brought in, lest it spoil their mirth; that
> thinks it an intolerable toil and slavery to love God, and holiness, and
> heaven, and to be put to work for them; and thinks it a delightful thing to
> love a whore, or excess of food, or drink, or sports! Can you say anything
> of a man that is more disgraceful, unless you say he is a devil? It would
> not be so vile for a child to delight more in a dog than in his parents, or
> a husband to delight more in the ugliest harlot than in his wife, as it is
> for a man to delight more in fleshly vanities than in God. Will you be
> licking up this dung, when you should be refreshing your souls in angelic
> pleasures, and getting a foretaste of the delights of heaven? Oh how justly
> will God thrust such wretches away from his everlasting presence, who so
> abhor his ways and him! Can they blame him for denying them the things
> which they hate, or value so lightly that they prefer a lust to them? If
> they were not haters of God and holiness, they would never be so averse
> even to the delights which they should have with him.

> **Word notes**
> - *Corrupt not* → *Do not corrupt*; *Think not* → *Do not think*;
>   *Addict not* → *Do not addict*.
> - *beastly* → *animal*: it means pleasures we share with beasts. Modern
>   "beastly" would read as "awful."
> - *durable* → *lasting*.
> - *Bethink yourselves* → *Consider carefully*.
> - *wanton(ness)* → *loose(ness)*: modern "wanton" leans toward "random" or
>   "reckless"; Baxter means unrestrained and lewd. Easy to revisit.
> - *discourse* → *conversation* (in the sense of talk).
> - *mar* → *spoil*; *meat* → *food*; *employed for them* → *put to work for
>   them*.
> - *solacing* → *refreshing*; *angelical* → *angelic*; *foretasting* →
>   *getting a foretaste of*.
> - *were not so vile* → *would not be so vile*.
> - *set so light by* → *value so lightly*; *prefer a lust before them* →
>   *prefer a lust to them*.
> - Left as is: *whore*, *harlot*, *dung*, *wretches*, *vanities*.

## Sample 3: Directions VIII–IX on melancholy and impatience (Part 1, Ch. 3), full text

**Original**

> _Direct._ VIII. Take heed of a melancholy habit of body; for melancholy
> people can scarce delight in any thing at all, and therefore not in God.
> Delight is as hard to them, as it is to a pained member to find pleasure,
> or a sick stomach to delight in the food which it loathes. They can think
> of God with trouble, and fear, and horror, and despair; but not with
> delight.
>
> _Direct._ IX. Take heed of an impatient, peevish, self-tormenting mind,
> that can bear no cross; and of overvaluing earthly things, which causeth
> impatience in the want of them. Make not too great a matter of fleshly pain
> or pleasure.--Otherwise your minds will be called to a continual attendance
> on the flesh, and taken up with continual desires, or cares, or fears, or
> griefs, or pleasures; and will not be permitted to solace themselves with
> God. The soul that would have pure and high delights, must abstract itself
> from the concernments of the flesh; and look on your body, as if it were the
> body of another, whose pain or pleasure you can choose whether you will
> feel. When Paul was rapt up into the third heaven, and saw the things
> unutterable, he was so far freed from the prison of sense, that he knew not
> whether he was in the body or out of it. As the separated souls, that see
> the face of God and the Redeemer, do leave the body to be buried, and to rot
> in darkness, and feel not all this to the interrupting of their joys; so
> faith can imitate such a death to the world, and such a neglect of the
> flesh, and some kind of elevating separation of the mind, to the things
> above. If in this near conjunction you cannot leave the body to rejoice or
> suffer alone, yet, as itself is but a servant to the soul, so let not its
> pain or pleasure be predominant, and control the high operations of the
> soul. A manly, valiant, believing soul, though it cannot abate the pain at
> all, nor reconcile the flesh to its calamity, yet it can do more,
> notwithstanding the pain, to its own delight, than strangers will believe.
>
> Some women, and passionate, weak-spirited men, especially in sickness, are
> so peevish, and of such impatient minds, that their daily work is to
> disquiet and torment themselves. One can scarce tell how to speak to them,
> or look at them, but it offendeth them. And the world is so full of
> occasions of provocation, that such persons are like to have little
> quietness. It is unlike that these should delight in God, who keep their
> minds in a continual, ulcerated, galled state, incapable of any delights at
> all, and cease not their self-tormenting.

**Modernized**

> **Direction 8.** Beware of a melancholy bodily constitution; for melancholy
> people can scarcely delight in anything at all, and therefore not in God.
> Delight is as hard to them, as it is to a painful limb to find pleasure, or
> a sick stomach to delight in the food which it loathes. They can think of
> God with trouble, and fear, and horror, and despair; but not with delight.
>
> **Direction 9.** Beware of an impatient, peevish, self-tormenting mind,
> that can bear no hardship; and of overvaluing earthly things, which causes
> impatience when we lack them. Do not make too great a matter of fleshly
> pain or pleasure.--Otherwise your minds will be called to a continual
> attendance on the flesh, and taken up with continual desires, or cares, or
> fears, or griefs, or pleasures; and will not be permitted to find comfort
> in God. The soul that would have pure and high delights, must withdraw
> itself from the concerns of the flesh; and look on your body, as if it were
> the body of another, whose pain or pleasure you can choose whether you will
> feel. When Paul was caught up into the third heaven, and saw the things
> unutterable, he was so far freed from the prison of the senses, that he did
> not know whether he was in the body or out of it. As the separated souls,
> that see the face of God and the Redeemer, leave the body to be buried, and
> to rot in darkness, and feel none of this to the interruption of their
> joys; so faith can imitate such a death to the world, and such a neglect of
> the flesh, and some kind of elevating separation of the mind, to the things
> above. If in this close union you cannot leave the body to rejoice or
> suffer alone, yet, as it is only a servant to the soul, so do not let its
> pain or pleasure be dominant, and control the high operations of the soul.
> A manly, valiant, believing soul, though it cannot lessen the pain at all,
> nor reconcile the flesh to its calamity, yet can do more, in spite of the
> pain, toward its own delight, than those unacquainted with it will believe.
>
> Some women, and emotional, weak-spirited men, especially in sickness, are
> so peevish, and of such impatient minds, that their daily work is to
> disquiet and torment themselves. One can scarcely tell how to speak to
> them, or look at them, without offending them. And the world is so full of
> occasions of provocation, that such persons are likely to have little
> quietness. It is unlikely that these should delight in God, who keep their
> minds in a continual, ulcerated, galled state, incapable of any delights at
> all, and never cease their self-tormenting.

> **Word notes**
> - *Take heed of* → *Beware of*.
> - *habit of body* → *bodily constitution*: "habit" meant a settled
>   physical condition or temperament. *Melancholy* is kept: it is Baxter's
>   own term, and "depression" would import modern categories. See the
>   standing note below.
> - *pained member* → *painful limb*; *scarce* → *scarcely*.
> - *cross* → *hardship*; *in the want of them* → *when we lack them*.
> - *causeth* → *causes*; *solace themselves with God* → *find comfort in God*.
> - *abstract* → *withdraw*; *concernments* → *concerns*.
> - *rapt up* → *caught up* (as in 2 Corinthians 12:2–4).
> - *the prison of sense* → *the prison of the senses*; *knew not whether* →
>   *did not know whether*.
> - *to the interrupting of* → *to the interruption of*.
> - *conjunction* → *union*; *predominant* → *dominant*; *abate* → *lessen*;
>   *notwithstanding* → *in spite of*; *to its own delight* → *toward its own
>   delight*.
> - *strangers* → *those unacquainted with it*: people with no experience of
>   this.
> - *passionate* → *emotional*: "passionate" meant swayed by feeling, not
>   enthusiastic.
> - *but it offendeth them* → *without offending them*; *like to have* →
>   *likely to have*; *unlike* → *unlikely*; *cease not* → *never cease*.
> - Left as is: *peevish*, *galled*, *ulcerated*, and the groups Baxter names
>   ("some women, and ... weak-spirited men"), since this round changes only
>   words, not his claims.
>
> **Standing note for *melancholy*:** in Baxter's day this meant a lasting
> low, fearful, or brooding state, understood as partly rooted in the body.
> It overlaps with, but is not the same as, modern depression or anxiety.

## Open questions

1. Is this the right level? Reading it, it is close to the original and
   still carries a few stiff constructions ("is it as hard to them as it is
   to a painful limb..."). I left them because fixing them means touching
   sentence structure. Say the word if you want a middle path.
2. *Direction* labels: I turned "_Direct._ VII." into **Direction 7.** and
   made it bold. Okay, or would you rather keep the Roman numerals?
3. Keep the blunt words (*whore*, *harlot*, *dung*) as they are?
4. Keep *melancholy* with a standing note, or replace it?
