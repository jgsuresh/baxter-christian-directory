# Chapter 8: Part 4, Title 2, practical directions against tippling and drunkenness (2–16)

*This file begins at Direction 2 of Baxter's "Practical Directions against Tippling and Drunkenness" (Part 4, Title 2); the heading and Direction 1 are in the previous file. This file ends Part 4; Part 5 follows.*

**Direction 2. Also fall down on your knees to God, and earnestly beg him to keep you that day from temptations, and bad company, and from all your fleshly desires and excess; and especially that he would renew your nature, and give you a hatred of the sin.**

**Direction 3. Keep yourself in the constant employments of your calling, and do not spend one quarter of an hour in idleness, and do not allow leisure to your thoughts, so much as to think of your drink and pleasures; much less to your body to follow it.**

God has commanded you, whoever you are, to labor six days, and in the sweat of your brows to eat your bread, and has forbidden idleness and negligence in your calling: avoid this, and it will help you much.

**Direction 4. Do not reckon upon long life, but think how quickly death will come, and that for all you know you may die that day; and how dreadful a case it would prove to you, to be found among tipplers, or to die before you are truly converted.**

Think of this before you go out of your doors; and think of it as you are going to the alehouse: look on the cup and the grave together: the dust of those bones will be wholesome spice to you. Remember when you see the wine, or ale, how unlike it is to that black and loathsome liquor which your blood and humors will be turned into when you are dead. Remember that the hand that takes the cup must shortly be scattered bones and dust; and the mouth that drinks it down must shortly be an ugly hole; and the palate, and stomach, and brain that are delighted by it must shortly be stinking puddle: and that the graves of drunkards are the field or garden of the devil, where corpses are sown to rise at the resurrection to be fuel for hell.

**Direction 5. When you are tempted to the alehouse, call up your reason, and remember that there is a God that sees you, and will judge you, and that you have an endless life of joy or torment shortly to possess, and that you have sinned thus too long already, and that without sound repentance your case is desperate, and that you are far from true repentance while you go on in sin.**

Ask yourself, Have I not sinned long enough already? Have I not long enough abused mercy? Shall I make my case beyond remedy, and cast away all hope? Does not God stand by, and see and hear all? Am I not stepping by death into an endless world? Think of these things, and use your reason, if you are a man, and have reason to use.

**Direction 6. Exercise yourself daily in repenting for what is past; and that will preserve you for the time to come.**

Confess your former sin to God with sorrow, and beg forgiveness for it with tears and groans. If you make light of all that is past, you are prepared to commit more. Think as you go about your work, how grievously you have sinned against your knowledge and conscience; in the sight of God; against all his mercies; and how obstinately you have gone on, and how unthankfully you have rejected mercy, and neglected Christ, and refused grace. Think what would have become of you, if you had died in this case; and how exceedingly you are indebted to the patience of God, that he did not cut you off, and did not cast you into hell, and that he has provided and offered you a Savior, and is yet willing to pardon and accept you through his Son, if you will but resolutely return, and live in faith and holiness. These penitent thoughts and exercises will kill your sin and cure you. Fast and humble yourself for what you have done already: as the holy apostle says, "So, since Christ suffered in the flesh, you also arm yourselves with the same attitude because the one who has suffered in the flesh has finished with sin, in that he spends the rest of his time on earth concerned about the will of God and not human desires. For the time that has passed was sufficient for you to do what the non-Christians desire. You lived then in debauchery, evil desires, drunkenness, carousing, drinking bouts, and wanton idolatries. So they are astonished when you do not rush with them into the same flood of wickedness, and they vilify you. They will face a reckoning before Jesus Christ who stands ready to judge the living and the dead" (1 Peter 4:1–5).

**Direction 7. Keep from the place and company: "do not be sharers with them. ... Do not participate in the unfruitful deeds of darkness, but rather expose them" (Ephesians 5:7, 11).**

You cannot deny that you are able to do this if you will. Can you not stay at home and not come near them? If you are willing to escape, do not run into the snare.

**Direction 8. Stop at the first cup: do not be drawn on by little and little: as the sluggard says, Yet a little more sleep; so the drunkard says, Yet a little more drink; I will take but one cup more.**

Understand your due measure, that you may know what is excess: to an ordinary healthy body, that does not very much labor and sweat, a quart in a day is enough; to cold and phlegmatic persons it is too much: the old rule was, *Prima ad sitim, secunda ad hilaritatem, tertia ad voluptatem, quarta ad insaniam*: The first cup is for thirst, the second for mirth, the third for physical pleasure, the fourth for madness. Especially you that have drunk too much so long should rather drink less than other men: your souls require it for penitence and for prevention; your bodies require it, to cure the undigested matter already heaped up.

**Direction 9. Avoid the tempting ceremonies of drunkards, such as drinking healths, or urging others to pledge them, or drink more.**

Plutarch says that when Agesilaus was made the master of a feast, and was to prescribe the laws for drinking, his law was, If there is wine enough, give every one what he asks for; if not enough, divide it equally; by which means none were tempted or urged to drink, and the intemperate were ashamed to ask for more than others. As among witches, so among drunkards, the devil has his laws and ceremonies, and it is dangerous to practice them.

**Direction 10. Go to your sinful companions to their houses, and tell them plainly and seriously that you repent of what you have done already, and that you are ashamed to remember it; and that now you perceive that there is a righteous God, and a day of judgment, and an endless punishment to be thought on, and that you are resolved you will be voluntarily mad no more; and that you will not sell your soul and Savior for a merry cup; and beg them for the sake of Christ, and of their souls, to join with you in repentance and reformation; but let them know that if they will not, you come to take your leave of them, and are resolved you will no more be their companion in sin, lest you be their companion in hell.**

If you are willing indeed to repent and be saved, do this at once and plainly; and do not shrink from their displeasure or reproach: if you will not, say you will not, and say no more you cannot; but say, I will keep my sin and be damned: for that is the English of it.

**Direction 11. Suppose when the cup of excess is offered you, that you saw these words, sin and hell, written upon the cup, and saw the devil offering it to you, and urging you to drink, and saw Christ bleeding on the cross, and calling to you, O do not drink that which costs so dear a price as my blood!**

Strongly imprint this supposition on your mind: and it is not unreasonable; for certainly sin is in your cup, and hell is next to sin; and it is the devil that urges you on, and it is Christ unseen that would dissuade you.

**Direction 12. Suppose that there were mortal poison in the cup that is offered you: ask yourself, Would I drink it if there were poison in it?**

If not, why should I drink it when sin is in it, and hell is near it? and the supposition is not idle. It is written of Cyrus that when Astyages observed that at a feast he drank no wine, and asked him the reason, he answered, because he thought there was poison in the cup, for he had observed some that drank out of it lost their speech or understanding, and some of them vomited, and therefore he feared it would poison him: in any case, it is poison to the soul.

**Direction 13. Look soberly upon a drunken man, and think whether that is a desirable plight for a wise man to put himself into.**

See how ugly he looks, with heavy eyes, and a slobbering mouth, stinking with drink or vomit, staggering, falling, spewing, bawling, talking like a madman, pitied by wise men, hooted at by boys, and madly reeling on towards hell. And also look upon some wise and sober man, and see how composed and seemly is his countenance and bearing; how wise his words, how regular his actions, how calm his mind; envied by the wicked, but revered by all that are impartial. And then consider which of these it is better to be like. Says Basil, Drunkenness makes men sleep like the dead, and wake like the sleeping. It turns a man into a useless, offensive, filthy, hurtful, and devouring beast.

**Direction 14. If all this will not serve the purpose, if you are but willing, I can teach you a cheap restraint, and tell you of a medicine that is good against drunkenness and excess.**

Resolve that after every cup of excess you will drink a cup of the juice of wormwood, or of carduus, or centaury, or germander; at least, as soon as you come home and grow wiser, that this shall be your penance; and keep on this course but a little while, and your appetite will rather choose to be without the drink than to bear the penance. Do not shrink from it; if your reason is not strong enough for a manly cure, drench yourself like a beast, and use such a cure as you are capable of; and in time it may bring you to be capable of a better. And I can assure you, a bitter draught is a very cheap remedy to prevent a sin.

**Direction 15. If all this will not serve, I have yet another remedy if you are but willing: confess yourself unfit to govern yourself, and give up yourself to the government of some other; your wife, your parents, or your friend.**

And here these things are to be done: 1. Engage your wife, or friend, to watch over you, and not to allow you to go to the alehouse, nor to drink more than is profitable to your health. 2. Deliver your purse to them, and keep no money yourself. 3. Drink no more at home but what they give you, and leave it to them to judge what measure is best for you. 4. When you are tempted to go to the alehouse, tell your wife or friend, that they may watch you. Even as you would call for help if thieves were robbing you. 5. Give leave to your wife or friend to charge the ale-sellers to give you no drink; and go yourself when you are in your right mind, and charge them yourself to give you none; and tell them that you are not yourself, or in your right wits, when you desire it. If these means seem now too hard to you, and you will sin on, and venture upon the wrath and curse of God and upon hell, rather than you will use them, remember hereafter that you were damned because you would be damned, and that you chose the way to hell to escape these troubles, and take what you get by it; but do not say you could not help it, for I am sure you can do this if you will. You will lock your door against thieves; lock your mouth also against a more dangerous thief, that would rob you of your reason and salvation. Says Basil, If his master does but box or beat his servant, he will run away from the strokes; and will you not run away from the drink that would break your brains and understanding?

**Direction 16. But the saving remedy is this, study the love of God in Christ, and the riches of grace, and the eternal glory promised to holy souls, till you are in love with God, and heaven, and holiness, and have found sweeter pleasure than your excess, and then you will need no more directions.**

Read Ephesians 5:18.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). All NET wording was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Labels:** this file sits in Part 4 of Chapter 8, Title 2 (against drunkenness). "Tit." in the source is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," a heading or division within a Part. The Directions are numbered as Baxter numbers them within his "Practical Directions against Tippling and Drunkenness."
- **Direction headings:** where a Direction's whole paragraph, or its first sentence, is the directive (Directions 2, 7, 8, 10, 11), the full sentence is set in bold so that no sentence is split; Direction 2 has no further text.
- **1 Peter 4:1–5** (Direction 6): NET wording. Baxter's reference stood before the quotation in the source; it is moved after it, following the project's practice. Differences from the King James Baxter quotes: the King James has "Christ hath suffered *for us* in the flesh," which the NET lacks (probably a difference in the Greek text followed; we have not confirmed this); "the will of the Gentiles" is "what the non-Christians desire"; the King James list "lasciviousness, lusts, excess of wine, revellings, banquetings" is "debauchery, evil desires, drunkenness, carousing, drinking bouts," so the drinking words Baxter wants are still there; "excess of riot" is "flood of wickedness"; "speaking evil of you" is "they vilify you"; and where the King James has "who shall give account to him that is ready to judge the quick and the dead," the NET names "Jesus Christ who stands ready to judge the living and the dead." The sense Baxter leans on (the past time of excess is enough; arm yourself to have done with sin) is unchanged.
- **Ephesians 5:7, 11** (Direction 7): NET wording; the NET's verse 7 begins "Therefore," which Baxter's quotation does not include, and "..." marks the verses he skips. The King James "Have no fellowship with" is "Do not participate in," and "reprove them" is "expose them." Baxter's point (keep away from the company) still stands; the NET's "expose" is less clearly a spoken rebuke than "reprove."
- **Ephesians 5:18** (Direction 16, cited only). NET: "And do not get drunk with wine, which is debauchery, but be filled by the Spirit." The King James has "wherein is excess"; Baxter's word "excess" throughout this section matches the King James.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "to labor six days" (Exodus 20:9, NET "For six days you may labor and do all your work"); "in the sweat of your brows to eat your bread" (Genesis 3:19, NET "By the sweat of your brow you will eat food"); "as the sluggard says, Yet a little more sleep" (compare Proverbs 6:10 and 24:33, NET "A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to relax"); Baxter's words are not a quotation of either verse.
- **The Latin rule** (Direction 8): *Prima ad sitim, secunda ad hilaritatem, tertia ad voluptatem, quarta ad insaniam*. Baxter translates it himself in the next clause; his translation is kept. We have not traced the "old rule" to a source. His "sensual pleasure" is rendered "physical pleasure," following the project's practice for "sensual" (Latin *voluptatem*, pleasure).
- **humors** (Direction 4): the body's fluids in the older medicine (blood, phlegm, choler, melancholy). **cold and phlegmatic persons** (Direction 8): people whose constitution was thought to be dominated by phlegm, a "cold" humor; kept as Baxter's medical language. These are his period's medical ideas, not claims we make.
- **a quart in a day** (Direction 8): Baxter's measure, presumably of ale or beer, the ordinary drink of his day; kept. An English quart of the period was roughly a liter, but we have not checked the exact ale measure.
- **the undigested matter** (Direction 8): Baxter's "crudities," an older medical word for food or humors not properly digested and thought to cause disease.
- **wormwood, carduus, centaury, germander** (Direction 14): bitter herbs used in medicine. "Carduus" is probably the "blessed thistle" (*carduus benedictus*); we have not confirmed which plant Baxter meant. Their bitterness is the point of the "penance."
- **drench yourself like a beast** (Direction 14): "drench" here is the farriers' word for dosing an animal with a draught of medicine; kept.
- **Plutarch and Agesilaus** (Direction 9); **Cyrus and Astyages** (Direction 12); **Basil** (Directions 13 and 15): the anecdotes and sayings are Baxter's. The Cyrus story may come from Xenophon's *Cyropaedia*, and the Basil sayings may come from Basil's homily against drunkenness, but we have not checked either source.
- **"As among witches, so among drunkards"** (Direction 9): Baxter's comparison, kept as he wrote it.
- **"if you will"** (Directions 7, 15) and **"if you will not, say you will not"** (Direction 10): "will" means "choose to" or "are willing to," which is Baxter's running theme in this section (see the paragraph before Direction 1 in the previous file); kept. **"you were damned because you would be damned"** (Direction 15): "would" means "chose to"; kept.
- **"for that is the English of it"** (Direction 10): Baxter's idiom for "that is what it means in plain words"; kept.
- **"voluntarily mad"** (Direction 10): Baxter's way of calling drunkenness a madness chosen by the drinker; kept.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): beg of him → beg him; ill company → bad company; for aught thou knowest → for all you know; sowed → sown; remediless → beyond remedy; beg forgiveness of it → beg forgiveness for it; what had become of thee → what would have become of you; beholden → indebted; resolvedly → resolutely; healthful → healthy; crudities → undigested matter; presently → at once; stick not at / do not stick at → do not shrink from; beseech → beg; puts thee on → urges you on; the supposition is not vain → is not idle (that is, not groundless); drunk out of it → drank out of it; however (at the end of Direction 12) → in any case (Baxter means "whatever the case with Cyrus's reason"); ill-favouredly → ugly; slabbering → slobbering; withal → also; comely → seemly; gesture → bearing (in Baxter's day "gesture" meant the whole carriage of the body); reverenced → revered; bethink thee → consider; noisome → offensive; serve turn → serve the purpose; hold on → keep on; suffer → allow; take that thou gettest → take what you get.
- **Words kept:** tippling and tipplers (habitual drinking, short of outright drunkenness); alehouse; calling (occupation); drinking healths and pledging (toasts, and answering a toast by drinking); excess; merry cup; countenance; draught; ale-sellers; charge (instruct, order); box (strike with the hand); right wits; puddle (foul, muddy liquid); spewing; bawling; hooted at; manly cure.
- **"Drink no more at home but what they give you"** (Direction 15, item 3): "but" means "than"; kept.
- **"Even as you would call for help if thieves were robbing you."** (Direction 15, item 4): printed as a separate sentence in the source; kept as printed.
