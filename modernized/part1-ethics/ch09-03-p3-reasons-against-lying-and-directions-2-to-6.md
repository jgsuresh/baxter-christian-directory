# Chapter 9: Title 3, the last reasons against lying, and Directions 2 to 6

*This section begins partway through Direction 1 ("Be well informed of the evil of the sin of lying"), at its sixth reason.*

6. Lying destroys human interaction, and brings most pernicious confusion into the affairs of mankind. If truth is excluded, men cannot buy and sell, and trade, and live together. It would be sufficient to destroy their rational interaction if they had no tongues; but much more to have false tongues: silence does not open the mind at all; lying does not open it when it claims to open it, and falsely represents it to be what it is not. And therefore though you say that your lies do no such hurt; yet seeing this is the nature and tendency of lying as such, it is just and merciful in the righteous God to banish all lying by the strictest laws: [505] as the whole nature of serpents is so far at enmity with the nature of man that we hate and kill them though they never did hurt us, because it is in their nature to hurt us; so God has justly and mercifully condemned all lying, because its nature tends to the desolation and confusion of the world; and if any indulgence were given to it, all iniquity and injustice would at once like a flood overwhelm us all.

7. Lying tends directly to perjury itself. It is the same God that forbids them both: and when once the heart is hardened in the one, it is but a step further to the other. Cicero could observe that he that is accustomed to lie will easily be perjured. A seared conscience that tolerates one will easily be brought to bear the other.

8. There is a partiality in the liar that condemns himself, and the sin in another, which in himself he justifies; for there is no man that would have another lie to him. As Augustine says, I have known many that would deceive, but never any that would be deceived. [506] If it is good, why should not all others lie to you? If it is bad, why will you lie to others? Is not your tongue under the same law as theirs? Do you like it in your children and in your servants? If not, it should seem much worse to you in yourself, as you are most concerned in your own actions.

9. Judge what lying is by your own desire and expectation to be believed. Would you not have men believe you, whether you speak truth or not? I know you would; for the liar loses his end if he is known to lie, and is not believed. And is it a reasonable desire or expectation in you to have men believe a lie? If you would be believed, speak that which is to be believed.

10. Lying makes you always unbelievable, and so useless or dangerous to others; for he that will lie leaves men uncertain whether he ever speaks truth, unless there is better evidence of it than his credibility. As Aristotle says, A liar gets this by lying, that nobody will believe him when he speaks the truth. How shall I know that he speaks truth today who lied yesterday? unless open repentance recovers his credibility. Truth will defend itself, and credit him that owns it at last; but falsehood is indefensible, and will shame its patrons. Petrarch says excellently, As truth is immortal, so a fiction and lie does not endure long: dissembled matters are quickly revealed; as the hair that is combed and set with great diligence is ruffled with a little blast of wind; and the paint that is laid on the face with a great deal of labor is washed off with a little sweat: the craftiest lie cannot stand before the truth; but is transparent to him that closely looks into it; everything that is covered is soon uncovered: shadows pass away; and the native color of things remains: it is a great labor to keep hidden long. No man can live long under water; he must come forth, and show the face which he concealed. [507] At the furthest God at the day of judgment will lay open all.

**Direction 2. If you would avoid lying, beware of guilt. [508]**

Unclean bodies need a cover; and are most ashamed to be seen. Faultiness causes lying; and lying increases the fault. When men have done that which they are afraid or ashamed to make known, they think there is a necessity of using their art to keep it secret. But wit and craft are no good substitute for honesty; such patches make the tear much worse. But because the corrupted heart of man will be thus working and flying to deceitful tricks, prevent the cause and occasion of your lying. Do not commit the fault that needs a lie. Avoiding it is much better than hiding it, if you were sure to keep it ever so close. As indeed you are not; for commonly truth will come to light. It is the best way in the world to avoid lying, to be innocent; and do nothing which fears the light: truth and honesty do not blush, nor desire to be hidden. Children and servants are much addicted to this crime: when their folly, or unruliness, or appetites, or slothfulness, or carelessness has made them faulty, they at once study a lie to hide it with; which is to go to the devil to entreat him to defend or cover his own works. But wise, and obedient, and careful, and diligent, and conscientious children and servants have need of no such miserable tricks.

**Direction 3. Fear God more than man, if you would not be liars. [509]**

The excessive fear of man is a common cause of lying; this makes children so apt to lie, to escape the rod; and most persons that are exposed to much hurt from others are in danger of lying to avoid their displeasure. But why do you not fear God more, whose displeasure is unspeakably more terrible? Your parents or master will be angry, and threaten to correct you; but God threatens to damn you; and his wrath is a consuming fire: no man's displeasure can reach your souls, and extend to eternity: will you run into hell to escape punishment on earth? Remember, whenever you are tempted to escape any danger by a lie, that you run into a thousandfold greater danger, and that no hurt that you escape by it can possibly be half so great as the hurt it brings. It is as foolish a course as to cure the toothache by cutting off the head.

**Direction 4. Bring down your pride, and overmuch regard for the thoughts of men, if you would not be liars.**

Pride makes men so desirous of reputation, and so impatient of the hard opinion of others, that all the honest efforts of the proud are too little to obtain the reputation they desire, and therefore lying must make up the rest. Shame is so intolerable a suffering to them that they make lies the familiar cover of their nakedness. He that has not riches has pride, and would be thought somebody, and therefore will set out his estate by a lie. He that has not eminence of parentage and birth, if he has pride, will make himself a gentleman by a lie. He that is a contemptible person at home, if he is proud, will make himself honorable among strangers by a lie. He that lacks learning, degrees, or anything that he would be proud of will try by a lie to supply his wants: even as wanton women by the actual lie of painting would make themselves beautiful, through a proud desire to be esteemed. Especially he that commits a shameful crime, if he is proud, will rather venture on a lie than on the shame. But if your pride is cured, your temptation to lying will be as nothing; you will be so indifferent in matters of honor or reputation as not to venture your souls on God's displeasure for it: not that any should be shameless, or utterly regardless of their reputation; but none should overvalue it, nor prefer it before their souls, nor seek it by unlawful means. Avoid shame by well-doing, and spare not: (only see that you have a higher end.) Seneca says, There are more that abstain from sin through shame than through virtue or a good will: it is well when virtue is so much in credit, and vice in discredit, that those that have not the virtue would gladly have the name, and those that will not leave the vice would escape the shame; and it is well that there are human motives to restrain them that do not care for divine ones. But as human motives cause no saving virtues; so devilish and wicked means are far from preventing any pernicious hurt, being the certain means to bring it about. [510]

**Direction 5. Avoid ambition, and human, unnecessary dependence, if you would avoid lying.**

For the ambitious give themselves up to men; and therefore flattering must be their trade; and how much lying is necessary to the composition of flattery, I need not tell you. Truth is seldom taken for the fittest instrument of flattery. It is on the contrary the common road to hatred: *Libere et sine adulatione veritatem prædicantes, et gesta pravæ vitæ arguentes, gratiam non habent apud homines*, says Ambrose. They that preach truth freely and without flattery, and reprove the deeds of a wicked life, do not find favor with men. *Veritatem semper inimicitiæ persequuntur*: [511] Hatred is the shadow of truth, as envy is of happiness. When Aristippus was asked why Dionysius spoke so much against him, he answered, for the same reason that all other men do; implying that it was no wonder if the tyrant was impatient of truth and plain dealing, when it is so with almost all mankind: they are so culpable that all but flatterers seem to handle them too hard, and hurt their sores. And in this lies much of the misery of great men, that few or none deal truly with them, but they are flattered into perdition: Seneca says, *Divites cum omnia habent, unum illis deest; scilicet qui verum dicat: si enim in clientelam fælicis hominis potentumque perveneris, aut veritas aut amicitia perdenda est*: One thing rich men lack when they have all things, that is, a man to speak the truth: for if you become the dependent or client of prosperous or great men, you must cast away (or lose) either the truth or their friendship. [512] Jerome thought that therefore Christ did not have a house to put his head in, because he would flatter nobody, and therefore nobody would receive him in the city. And the worst of all is that where flattery reigns, it is taken for a duty, and the neglect of it for a vice: as Jerome (*ad Cel.*) says, *Quodque gravissimum est, quia humilitatis ac benevolentiæ loco ducitur, ita fit ut qui adulari nescit, aut invidus aut superbus reputetur*, that is, And, which is most grievous, because it passes for humility and kindness, it comes to pass that he that cannot flatter is taken to be envious or proud. But the time will come that the flatterer will be hated even by him whom his deceptive praises pleased. Deceit and lies please the flattered person but a while; even till he finds the bitterness of the effects, and the fruit has told him that it was but a sugared kind of enmity: and therefore he will not long be pleased with the flatterer himself. Flattery always appears at last to be but *perniciosa dulcedo*, as Augustine calls it. The same Augustine says (on Psalm 59), There are two sorts of persecutors, the opposer (or dispraiser) and the flatterer: but the tongue of the flatterer hurts more than the hand of the persecutor. [513] And do not think that any man's greatness or favor will excuse you or keep you harmless in your lies; for God that avenges them is greater than the greatest. Augustine says (book *On Lying*), *Quisquis autem esse aliquod genus mendacii, quod peccatum non sit putaverit, decipiet semetipsum turpiter, cum honestum se deceptorem arbitretur aliorum*, that is, Whoever thinks that there is any kind of lie that is no sin deceives himself foully, while he thinks himself an honest deceiver of others. "Do not become slaves of men" (1 Corinthians 7:23), if you would be true.

**Direction 6. Do not love greed, if you would not be liars. [514]**

A lie will seem to a greedy man an easy means to obtain his gain, to get a good bargain, or pass off a cracked commodity for more than it is worth. *Rupere fœdus, impius lucri furor, et ira præceps.* Seneca, *Hippolytus*. He that loves money better than God and conscience will for money displease God and conscience, by this or any other sin.

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[505] It was one of the Roman laws, tab. 12. Qui falsum testimonium dixisse convictus erit, e saxo Tarpeio dejiciatur. (Whoever is convicted of having given false testimony, let him be thrown down from the Tarpeian rock.)

[506] Hic autem homines fallunt et falluntur: miseriores sunt cum mentiendo fallunt, quam cum mentientibus credendo falluntur. Usque adeo tamen rationalis natura refugit falsitatem, et quantum potest devitat errorem, ut falli nollint, etiam quicunque amant fallere. August. Enchirid. c. 17. (Here, however, men deceive and are deceived: they are more wretched when they deceive by lying than when they are deceived by believing liars. Yet rational nature so far shrinks from falsehood, and avoids error as much as it can, that even those who love to deceive are unwilling to be deceived. Augustine, *Enchiridion*, chapter 17.)

[507] Petrarch, book 1 of *On the Solitary Life* [*De vita solitaria*].

[508] Sæpe delinquentibus promptissimum est mentiri. Cicer. (For those who do wrong, lying is often the readiest thing. Cicero.)

[509] Ille veritatis defensor esse debet, qui cum recte sentit, loqui non metuit, nec erubescit. Ambr. (He ought to be a defender of the truth who, when he judges rightly, is not afraid to speak, nor ashamed. Ambrose.) Liars are valiant against God, and cowards against men. Montaigne's *Essays*.

[510] Avoid both the extremes, which Petrarch mentions: Nam ut multi qui se bonos, sic aliqui qui se malos fingerent sunt reperti; quod vel humani favoris pestilentem auram; vet invisam bonorum temporalium sarcinam declinarent. Quod de Ambrosio lectum est. (How like friendship is flattery! It does not only imitate it but surpasses it: it wins favor by the very thing by which it harms. Seneca.)

## Translator's notes

- **Title:** "Tit." is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," a heading or division. Chapter 9 ("Directions for the Government of the Tongue") is divided into six Titles; this file continues Title 3, against lying. Baxter's Roman numerals for the Directions (II.-VI.) are given as Arabic numbers.
- **Where this file begins:** the range opens at reason 6 of Direction 1, whose earlier reasons are in the preceding file. We have not checked that file.
- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **1 Corinthians 7:23:** NET wording ("Do not become slaves of men"), fetched from labs.bible.org. Baxter quotes the King James "Be not the servants of men." The NET's "slaves" is stronger than "servants," and "become" makes it a warning against falling into that state. Baxter's point (do not make yourself dependent on men, so as to flatter them) survives, but in the KJV the word "servants" carried his idea of a dependent client more gently. In Paul the verse concerns not becoming slaves of men because believers were bought with a price.
- **Notes 513 and 514** are references only, converted to modern form. We have not quoted the verses, since Baxter does not.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "his wrath is a consuming fire" (compare Hebrews 12:29; Deuteronomy 4:24); "Christ had not a house to put his head in" (compare Matthew 8:20; Luke 9:58); "everything that is covered is soon uncovered" in the Petrarch quotation (compare Luke 12:2, though Baxter gives it as Petrarch's).
- **interaction** (reason 6, twice): Baxter's "converse," meaning social dealings and communication between people. **pernicious** kept. **at once:** his "presently." **flood:** his "inundation."
- **is accustomed to lie** (reason 7): Baxter's "is used to lie." Cicero's saying is kept as Baxter gives it; we have not located the passage.
- **Augustine:** Baxter's "Austin," the usual English form of the name in his day. The saying in reason 8 is tied to note 506 (*Enchiridion*, chapter 17), which says much the same thing.
- **unbelievable** (reason 10): Baxter's "incredible," in its older sense, "not to be believed." **closely:** his "nearly." **revealed:** his "opened" ("dissembled matters are quickly opened"). **dissembled** kept (disguised, hidden under a false appearance). **must:** his "must needs." **owns** kept (acknowledges, stands by). **At the furthest:** kept, meaning "at the latest."
- **Aristotle; Petrarch; Seneca; Aristippus and Dionysius:** sayings kept as Baxter gives them; we have not checked them against the sources. Note 507 gives the Petrarch passage as from *On the Solitary Life*, book 1.
- **beware of guilt** (Direction 2): Baxter's "take heed of guilt." **tricks:** his "shifts" (twice). **tear:** his "rent." **ever so close:** his "never so close." **unruliness:** his "wantonness," here of children and servants, meaning wild, undisciplined behavior. **at once:** his "presently." **conscientious:** his "conscionable."
- **exposed** (Direction 3): Baxter's "obnoxious," in its older sense, "liable" or "exposed" (to hurt).
- **Bring down** (Direction 4): Baxter's "Get down." **regard for:** his "regard of." **efforts:** his "endeavours." **obtain; bring it about:** his "procure." **lacks:** his "wanteth." **try:** his "endeavour." **shameless:** his "impudent." **gladly:** his "fain."
- **set out his estate** (Direction 4): kept; "set out" means display or puff up, and "estate" means his wealth and standing.
- **He that has not riches has pride** (Direction 4): left as Baxter printed it ("He that hath not riches, hath pride"); the sense seems to be "he that has no riches but has pride."
- **wanton women ... the actual lie of painting:** kept as Baxter wrote. "Painting" is face-painting, that is, cosmetics; "wanton" here means vain or loose. This is his own judgment, kept as his.
- **Avoid shame by well-doing, and spare not: (only see that you have a higher end.)** Punctuation kept as printed.
- **on the contrary** (Direction 5): Baxter's "contrarily." **implying:** his "intimating." **in this lies:** his "herein lieth." **lack:** his "want." **receive:** his "entertain." **deceptive:** his "fallacious." **always:** his "ever." **that is:** his "i. e." (twice). **keep you harmless:** his "save thee harmless."
- **Latin in Direction 5:** Baxter translates most of his Latin himself, and his English is kept. Where he does not:
  - *Veritatem semper inimicitiæ persequuntur* (Enmities always pursue the truth). Baxter follows it with a looser English saying ("Hatred is the shadow of truth..."), not a translation. Note 511 points to Jerome on Galatians 4, presumably on Galatians 4:16 ("Have I become your enemy by telling you the truth?"); we have not confirmed it.
  - *perniciosa dulcedo* (a deadly sweetness).
  - In the Seneca quotation, *fælicis* is printed for *felicis*; left as printed.
- **Jerome (*ad Cel.*)**: Baxter's "Hieron. (ad Cel.)," probably the letter to Celantia printed among Jerome's letters; we have not confirmed it. "Hierom" and "Hieron." are both Jerome.
- **Augustine on Psalm 59:** Baxter's "in Psal. lix." Augustine used the Latin (Vulgate) numbering of the Psalms, so this is probably Psalm 60 in English Bibles; we have not checked it. **book *On Lying*:** Baxter's "li. de Mendac.," Augustine's *De Mendacio*.
- **greed; greedy** (Direction 6): Baxter's "covetousness," "covetous." **obtain:** his "procure." **pass off:** his "put off." **cracked commodity:** a damaged article for sale.
- ***Rupere fœdus, impius lucri furor, et ira præceps.* Seneca, *Hippolytus*** (not translated by Baxter): "The impious frenzy for gain and headlong anger have broken the bond." Baxter's "Sen. Hip." points to Seneca's tragedy usually called *Phaedra*, sometimes *Hippolytus*; we have not checked the line.
- **Note 505:** "tab. 12" refers to the Twelve Tables, the early Roman law code, as Baxter gives it; we have not checked which table holds this law.
- **Note 512:** "Athænus" is probably Athenaeus; "canum" is read as "gray-haired" (old); the line is rendered loosely. Cicero "Rhet. li. 1" is as Baxter gives it; we have not checked it.
