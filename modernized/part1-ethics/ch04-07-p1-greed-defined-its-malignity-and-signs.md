# Chapter 4: Directions against Greed, Direction 1 (Lawful and Sinful Love of Riches, the Malignity of Greed, and Its Signs)

*This range opens the section Baxter heads "Directions against Covetousness, or Love of Riches, and against Worldly Cares," and ends with the signs of greed (Item IV of Direction 1).*

**Directions against Greed, or Love of Riches, and against Worldly Cares.** [241]

I shall say but little on this subject now, because I have written a Treatise of it already, called *The Crucifying of the World by the Cross of Christ*; in which I have given many directions (in the preface and treatise) against this sin.

**Direction 1. Understand well the nature and malignity of this sin; both what it is, and why it is so great and perilous.** I shall here show you, 1. What love of riches is lawful. 2. What it is that is unlawful; and in what this sin of greed or worldliness does consist. 3. Wherein the malignity or greatness of it lies. 4. The signs of it. 5. What counterfeits of the contrary virtue hide this sin from the eyes of worldlings. 6. What false appearances of it cause many to be suspected of greed unjustly.

*In the margin: Lawful love of creatures.*

I. All love of the creature, the world, or riches, is not sin: for, 1. The works of God are all good, as such; and all goodness is lovable. As they are related to God, and his power, and wisdom, and goodness are imprinted on them, so we must love them, even for his sake. 2. All the impressions of the attributes of God appearing on his works, make them as a mirror, in which at this distance we must see the Creator; and their sweetness is a drop from him, by which his goodness and love are tasted. And so they were all made to lead us up to God, and help our minds to converse with him, and kindle the love of God in our breasts, as a love-token from our dearest friend; and thus, as the means of our communion with God, the love of them is a duty, and not a sin. 3. They are naturally the means of sustaining our bodies, and preserving life, and health, and alacrity; and as such, our sensory part has a love to them, as every beast has to its food; and this love in itself is not of a moral kind, and is neither a virtue nor a vice, until it is either used in obedience to our reason, (and so it is good,) or in disobedience to it (and so it is evil). 4. The creatures are necessary means to support our bodies, while we are doing God the service which we owe him in the world; and so they must be loved, as a means to his service; though we cannot say properly that riches are ordinarily thus necessary. 5. The creatures are necessary to sustain our bodies in our journey to heaven, while we are preparing for eternity; and thus they must be loved as remote helps to our salvation. And in these two last respects we call it in our prayers "our daily bread." 6. Riches may enable us to relieve our needy brethren, and to promote good works for church or state. And thus also they may be loved; so far as we must be thankful for them, so far we may love them; for we must be thankful for nothing but what is good.

*In the margin: Greed, what it is.*

II. But worldliness, or sinful love of riches, is, 1. When riches are loved and desired, and sought more for the flesh than for God or our salvation; even as the matter or means of our worldly prosperity, that the flesh may lack nothing to please it, and satisfy its desires. [242] Or that pride may have enough with which to support itself, by gratifying and obliging others, and living at those rates, and in that splendor, as may show our greatness, or further our domination over others. 2. And when we therefore desire them in that proportion which we think most suitable to these carnal ends, and are not content with our daily bread, and that proportion which may sustain us as passengers to heaven, and tend most to the securing of our souls, and to the service of God. So that it is the end by which a sinful love of riches is principally to be discerned; when they are loved for pride or flesh-pleasing, as they are the matter of a worldly, bodily happiness, and not principally for God and his service, and servants, and our salvation. And indeed, as pleasure-seekers love them, they should be hated.

*In the margin: Worldliness when predominant.*

Worldliness is either predominant, and so a certain sign of death; or else mortified, and in a subdued degree, consistent with some saving grace. Worldliness predominant, as in the ungodly, is, when men that do not have a lively belief of the everlasting happiness, nor have laid up their treasure and hopes in heaven, take the pleasure and prosperity of this life for that happiness which is highest in their esteem, and dearest to their hearts, and therefore love the riches of the world, or full provisions, as the matter and means of this their temporal happiness. [243] Worldliness in a mortified person, is, when he that has laid up his treasure in heaven, and practically esteems his everlasting hopes above all the pleasure and prosperity of the flesh, and seeks first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and uses his estate principally for God and his salvation, has yet some remnants of inordinate desire for the prosperity and pleasure of the flesh, and some inordinate desire of riches for that end; which yet he hates, laments, resists, and so far subdues, that it is not predominant, against the interest of God and his salvation. [244] Yet this is a great sin, though it is forgiven.

*In the margin: The malignity of it.*

III. The malignity or greatness of this sin consists in these points (especially when it is predominant). 1. The love of the world, or of riches, is a sin of deliberation, and not of mere rashness or sudden passion: worldlings contrive the attaining of their ends. 2. It is a sin of interest, love, and choice, set up against our chief interest: it is the setting up of a false end, and seeking that; and not only a sin of error in the means, or a seeking the right end in a mistaken way. 3. It is idolatry, [245] or a denying God, and deposing him in our hearts, and setting up his creatures in his stead, in that measure as it prevails. The worldling gives that love and that trust to the creature, which are due to God alone; he delights in it instead of God, and seeks and holds it as his happiness instead of God: and therefore, so far as any man loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him (1 John 2:15). And the friendship of the world is enmity to God. 4. It is a contempt of heaven; when it must be neglected, and a miserable world preferred. 5. It shows that unbelief is powerful in the heart so far as worldliness is powerful: for if men practically believed the heavenly glory, and the promise of it, they would be carried above these present things. 6. It is a debasing of the soul of man, and using it like the brutes, while it is principally set upon the serving of the flesh, and on a temporal happiness, and neglects its eternal happiness and concerns.

7. It is a perverting of the very drift of a man's life, as employed in seeking a wrong end, and not only of some one faculty or act: it is a habitual sin of the state and course of mind and life, and not only a particular actual sin. 8. It is a perverting of God's creatures to an end and use clean contrary to that which they were made and given for; and an abusing God by his own gifts, by which he should be served and honored; and a destroying our souls with those mercies which were given us for their help and benefit. This is the true character of this heinous sin. In a word, it is the forsaking God, and turning the heart from him, and alienating the life from his service, to this present world, and the service of the flesh. Fornication, drunkenness, murder, swearing, perjury, lying, stealing, etc. are very heinous sins. But a single act of one of these, committed rashly in the violence of passion, or temptation, does not speak such a malignant turning away of the heart habitually from God, as to say a man is greedy, or a worldling.

*In the margin: Signs of worldliness.*

IV. The signs of greed are these: 1. Not preferring God and our everlasting happiness before the prosperity and pleasure of the flesh; but valuing and loving fleshly prosperity above its worth. [246] 2. Esteeming and loving the creatures of God as provision for the flesh, and not to further us in the service of God. 3. Desiring more than is needful or useful to further us in our duty. 4. An inordinate eagerness in our desires after earthly things. 5. Distrustfulness, and anxious cares, and contrivances for time to come. 6. Discontent, and trouble, and a complaining at a poor condition, when we have no more than our daily bread. 7. When the world takes up our thoughts inordinately: when our thoughts will more easily run out upon the world, than upon better things: and when our thoughts of worldly plenty are more pleasant and sweet to us, than our thoughts of Christ, and grace, and heaven; and our thoughts of want and poverty are more bitter and grievous to us, than our thoughts of sin and God's displeasure. 8. When our speech is freer and sweeter about prosperity in the world, than about the concerns of God and our souls. 9. When the world bears sway in our families and dealings, and shuts out all serious effort in the service of God, and for our own and others' souls: or at least cuts short religious duties, and is preferred before them, and thrusts them into a corner, and makes us slightly huddle them over. 10. When we are dejected overmuch, and impatient under losses, and setbacks, and worldly injuries from men. 11. When worldly matters seem sufficient to engage us in contentions, and to make us break peace: and we will by lawsuits seek our right, when greater hurt is more likely to follow to our brother's soul, or greater wrong to the cause of religion, or the honor of God, than our right is worth. 12. When in our trouble and distress we fetch our comfort more from the thoughts of our provisions in the world, or our hopes of supply, than from our trust in God, and our hopes of heaven. [247] 13. When we are more thankful to God or man for outward riches, or any gift for the provision of the flesh, than for hopes or helps in order to salvation; for a powerful ministry, good books, or timely instructions for the soul. 14. When we are quiet and pleased if we do but prosper, and have plenty in the world, though the soul is miserable, unsanctified, and unpardoned. 15. When we are more careful to provide a worldly than a heavenly portion, for children and friends, and rejoice more in their bodily than their spiritual prosperity, and are troubled more for their poverty than their ungodliness or sin. 16. When we can see our brother in need, and shut up the bowels of our compassion, or can part with no more than mere superfluities for his relief: when we cannot spare that which makes only for our better being, when it is necessary to preserve his being itself; or when we give unwillingly or sparingly. [248] 17. When we will venture upon sinful means for gain, such as lying, overreaching, deceiving, flattering, or going against our consciences, or the commands of God. 18. When we are too much in expecting generosity from others, and think that all we buy from should sell cheaper to us than they can afford, and do not consider their loss or want, so that we have the gain: nor are content if they are never so bountiful to others, if they are not so to us. [249] 19. When we make too much fuss in the world for riches, taking too much upon ourselves, or striving for promotion, and flattering great ones, and envying any that are preferred before us, or get that which we expected. 20. When we hold our money faster than our innocence, and cannot part with it for the sake of Christ, when he requires it; but will stretch our consciences and sin against him, or forsake his cause, to save our estates; or will not part with it for the service of his church, or of our country, when we are called to it. 21. When the riches which we have, are used only for the pampering of our flesh, and superfluous provision for our posterity, and nothing but some inconsiderable crumbs or small bits are employed for God and his servants, nor used to further us in his service, and toward the laying up of a treasure in heaven. These are the signs of a worldly, greedy wretch.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Section heading and Baxter's word "covetousness":** per the project glossary, *covetousness* becomes *greed* throughout, including in the section heading and in the phrase "worldly, covetous wretch" (now "worldly, greedy wretch"). In Baxter's heading the same sin is also called "love of riches" and "worldliness."
- **1 John 2:15 (Item III, point 3):** Baxter gives no quotation marks. His "so far as any man loveth the world, the love of the Father is not in him" is a gloss that includes the verse's closing words. The NET reads "Do not love the world or the things in the world. If anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him." Baxter's "so far as" (a matter of degree) is his own addition to the verse; the NET has "if anyone." His wording is kept, modernized, and the reference converted.
- **"The friendship of the world is enmity to God" (Item III, point 3):** this has no reference in the text, but footnote [245] gives James 4:4. It is Baxter's paraphrase, kept in his words. The NET reads "friendship with the world means hostility toward God." The NET rendering "hostility" for the King James "enmity" does not change the sense Baxter leans on. Reference added here, not in Baxter's text.
- **Proverbs 23:4 (note [242]):** Baxter's "Labour not to be rich" is the King James. The NET has "Do not wear yourself out to become rich; be wise enough to restrain yourself." The NET puts the stress on exhausting effort rather than on the effort itself, a slightly different emphasis. Only the NET's first clause is given, as Baxter's was.
- **Scripture references only in footnotes** (not looked up for content, given as Baxter has them, converted to modern form): Philippians 3:7-9; James 1:10; Philippians 4:11; 1 Timothy 6:8; Luke 14:26, 33; Matthew 6:19-21, 33; John 6:27; Luke 12:19, 20; 18:22, 23; Ephesians 5:5; Colossians 3:5; Romans 13:14; Philippians 3:19; Ezekiel 33:31; Jeremiah 9:23; Job 1:21; 1 Timothy 6:17, 18; Malachi 3:8, 9. We have not checked whether 1 Timothy 3:8 and Judges 7:21 (note [246] and [248]) bear on the point Baxter cites them for; they are kept as printed.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "our daily bread" (Matthew 6:11; Luke 11:3); "laid up their treasure ... in heaven" and "seeketh first the kingdom of God and his righteousness" (Matthew 6:19-21, 33); "shut up the bowels of our compassion" (compare 1 John 3:17); "passengers to heaven" (his image of life as a journey).
- **Word swaps:** felicity to happiness; amiable to lovable; sensitive (part) to sensory; temerity to rashness; corporal to bodily; sensualists to pleasure-seekers; carking cares to anxious cares; repining to complaining; liker to more likely; ado to fuss; conversation/converse ("in our families and converse") to dealings; concernments to concerns; crosses to setbacks; driblets to small bits; liberality to generosity; preferment to promotion; innocency to innocence; American spelling (honour to honor, splendour to splendor, law-suits to lawsuits, etc.); glass (a mirror) to mirror; "have not a lively belief" to "do not have a lively belief."
- **Terms kept:** *worldling(s)* (a person devoted to worldly things); *predominant* (ruling over the other desires; his term for the state in which the love of riches rules); *mortified* (put to death, subdued; see earlier notes); *inordinate* (beyond proper order or measure); *creature(s)* (anything God has made, not only animals); *carnal*; *bowels of compassion* (the seat of pity, as in the King James); *great ones* (people of rank and power); *huddle them over* (hurry through them carelessly); *brethren*; *alacrity* (cheerful readiness).
- **Item I, points 3 and 4:** Baxter distinguishes two kinds of love, one of the "sensory part" (natural appetite, as an animal loves food) which is neither virtue nor vice until reason governs it, and a rational love. Left as written.
- **"Treatise ... called 'The Crucifying of the World by the Cross of Christ'":** Baxter's own title, kept in italics.
- **Note [241]:** "Sixt. 3." and "Bibl. Pat." are abbreviations whose full forms we have not confirmed (possibly Sixtus III and a *Bibliotheca Patrum*, but this is a guess). Baxter himself says the author is "accused of Pelagianism" (a teaching that denies the need of God's grace). Rendered as printed.
- **Note [249]:** "Cic. 1. ad Heren." probably refers to the work addressed to Herennius (the *Rhetorica ad Herennium*); we have not confirmed this or checked the passage. "Offic." probably means Cicero's *De Officiis* (unconfirmed). "Idem" means "the same author." "Duæ" is printed for "duae." Phocion and Alexander are Baxter's example (presumably the Athenian Phocion and Alexander the Great, though he does not say so); we have not checked the story.
- **Sidenote:** the source has the italic marking "_Signs of worldliness._"; rendered as the other margin notes. The margin note "Covetousness what." is rendered "Greed, what it is."
