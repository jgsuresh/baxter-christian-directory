# Chapter 2: Direction 3

**Direction 3. Labor to understand the true method of divinity, and see truths in their respective degrees and order; that you do not take the last for the first, nor the lesser for the greater. Therefore see that you are well grounded in the catechism; and do not refuse to learn some catechism that is sound and full, and keep it in memory while you live.** [49]

Method, or right order, exceedingly helps understanding, memory, and practice. [50] Truths have a dependence on each other; the lesser branches spring out of the greater, and those out of the stock and root. Some duties are but means to other duties, or subservient to them, and to be measured accordingly; and if it is not understood which is the chief, the other cannot be referred to it. When two things materially good come together, and both cannot be done, the greater must take place, and the lesser is no duty at that time, but a sin, since it is preferred to the greater. Therefore it is one of the commonest difficulties among cases of conscience to know which duty is the greater, and to be preferred. Upon this ground, Christ healed on the Sabbath day, and pleaded for his disciples rubbing the ears of grain, and for David's eating the showbread, and tells them that "the Sabbath was made for people, not people for the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27), and that God wants "mercy and not sacrifice" (Matthew 12:7).

Divinity is an intricate, well-composed frame. As it is not enough that you have all the parts of your watch or clock, but you must see that every part is in its proper place, or else it will not run, or answer its end; so it is not enough that you know the various parts of divinity or duty unless you know them in their true order and place. You may be confused before you are aware, and led into many dangerous errors, by mistaking the order of various truths; and you may be misguided into heinous sins, by mistaking the degrees and order of duties; as when duties of piety and charity seem to be competitors; and when you think that the commands of men contradict the commands of God; and when the substance and the circumstances or modes of duty are in question before you as inconsistent; or when the means seems to cease to be a means by crossing the end: and in a great many such cases you cannot easily conceive what a snare it may prove to you to be ignorant of the methods and ranks of duty.

*Objection.* If that is so, what man can help being confused in his religion, when there are so few that observe any method at all, and few that agree in method, and none that has published a scheme or method so exact and clear as to be commonly approved by divines themselves? What then can untaught Christians do?

*Answer.* Divinity is like a tree that has one trunk, [51] and thence a few greater arms or boughs, and thence a thousand smaller branches; or like the veins, or nerves, or arteries in the body, that have first one or few trunks divided into more, and those into a few more, and those into more, until they multiply at last into more than can easily be seen or numbered. Now it is easy for any man to begin at the chief trunk, and to discern the first divisions, and the next, though not to comprehend the number and order of all the outermost and smaller branches. So it is in divinity: it is not very hard to begin at the unity of the eternal Godhead, and see there a Trinity of Persons, and of primary attributes, and of relations; and to rise to the principal attributes and works of God as in these relations, and to the relations of man to God, and to the great duties of these relations, to discern God's covenants and chief laws, and the duty of man in obedience to them, and the judgment of God in the carrying out of his sanctions; though many particular truths are not yet understood. And he that begins, and proceeds as he ought, does know methodically so much as he knows; and he is in the right way to the knowledge of more: and the great mercy of God has laid so great a necessity on us to know these few points that are easily known, and so much less need of knowing the many small particulars, that an ordinary Christian who well understands his catechism, or the creed, Lord's Prayer, and Ten Commandments may live uprightly, and holily, and comfortably; and may find daily work and consolation in the use of these.

A sound and well-composed catechism, well studied and kept in memory, would be a good measure of knowledge for ordinary Christians, and make them solid and orderly in their understanding, and in their going on to the smaller points, and would prevent a great deal of error and failure that many are thrown into by bad teaching, to their own grief and the churches'! Indeed, it is to be wished that some teachers of late had themselves learned so much, and in such order.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows the Latin passages in parentheses; it was prepared for this project and is not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check it before relying on fine points.

[49] Leg. Acost. 1. 4. c. 21 et 22. de fructu catechizandi. Et Li. 5. (Read Acosta, book 4, chapters 21 and 22, on the fruit of catechizing. And book 5.)

[50] Opus est imprimis duplici catechismo: Uno compendario et brevi quem memoriter addiscant; ubi summa sit eorum omnium quæ ad fidem et mores Christiano sunt necessaria: altero uberiore, ubi eadem amplius, dilucidiusque dicantur, et copiosius confirmentur: ut ille prior discipulis potius, hic posterior ipsis præceptoribus usu sit. Acosta, l. 5. c. 14. p. 490. (There is need above all of a double catechism: one short and compendious, which they may learn by heart, where there is a summary of everything that is necessary to a Christian in faith and conduct; the other fuller, where the same things are said more fully and more clearly and confirmed at greater length; so that the first may serve the pupils and the second the teachers themselves. Acosta, book 5, chapter 14, p. 490.)

[51] Stoici dicunt virtutes sibi invicem ita esse connexas, ut qui unam habuerit, omnes habeat. Laertius in Zenone. (The Stoics say that the virtues are so connected with one another that whoever has one has them all. Laertius, *Life of Zeno*.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Mark 2:27; Matthew 12:7:** Baxter gives no references. His words, "the sabbath was made for man, and not man for the sabbath, and that God will have mercy, and not sacrifice," combine Mark 2:27 and Matthew 12:7 (which quotes Hosea 6:6). We use the NET for both: "The Sabbath was made for people, not people for the Sabbath" and "I want mercy and not sacrifice." The references were not in Baxter's text.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): Christ healing on the Sabbath (Matthew 12:9–14; Luke 6:6–11); the disciples rubbing the ears of grain (Mark 2:23–28; Luke 6:1–5); David eating the showbread (1 Samuel 21:1–6).
- **divinity** (throughout): Baxter's word for theology. Kept, as it appears in his title for this Direction. **divines:** theologians.
- **cases of conscience:** left as written; moral cases in which two duties seem to conflict. This is the sense of Baxter's own title, *Cases of Conscience*, for his later chapters.
- **materially good** (paragraph 1): good in the matter of the act itself, as opposed to the motive or circumstances. The point is that when two good acts conflict, the lesser one becomes wrong.
- **intricate:** Baxter's "curious." **respective:** his "several." **various:** his "several." **outermost:** his "extreme."
- **ears of grain:** Baxter's "ears of corn." "Corn" in older English meant grain generally. **showbread:** Baxter's "shew-bread."
- **ordinary Christian:** Baxter's "mean Christian." **untaught:** his "ignorant." **failure:** his "miscarriage."
- **the carrying out of his sanctions:** Baxter's "the execution of his sanctions," meaning the carrying out of God's rewards and penalties.
- **a Trinity of Persons, and of primary attributes, and of relations** (Answer): Baxter outlines the order of divinity: the unity of God, the Trinity of Persons, God's attributes and works, man's relation to God, and the duties of these relations. Left as written.
- **catechism** (heading): a short summary of doctrine, usually in question and answer form. Baxter recommends learning a short one and a fuller one (note 50).
- **Lord's Prayer; Ten Commandments; creed:** the traditional three basics of a catechism.
- **Acosta** (notes 49, 50): see the notes to Direction 5 of Part 1. Here Acosta is cited as an authority on catechizing the native peoples of the Americas.
- **"some teachers of late"** (last paragraph): Baxter's pointed remark that some contemporary teachers lack order in their own understanding.
