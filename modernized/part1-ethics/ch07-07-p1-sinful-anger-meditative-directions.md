# Chapter 7: Title 7, against sinful anger (when anger is sinful; meditative Directions 1-12; Objections 1-4)

**Title 7. Directions against sinful wrath or anger.**

As anger is against the love of our neighbor, I shall speak of it afterward: as it is against the soul itself, I shall speak of it in this place. Anger is the rising up of the heart in emotional displeasure against a perceived evil, which would thwart or deprive us of some desired good. It is given us by God for good, to stir us up to a vigorous resistance of those things which, within us or outside us, oppose his glory or our salvation, or our own or our neighbor's real good.

Anger is good when it is thus used to its appointed end, in a right manner and measure: but it is sinful, 1. When it rises up against God or any good, as if it were evil to us: as wicked men are angry at those that would convert and save them, and that tell them of their sins, and that hinder them from their desires. [339] 2. When it disturbs reason, and hinders our judging of things rightly. 3. When it casts us into any unseemly behavior, or causes or disposes to any sinful words or actions: when it inclines us to wrong another by word or deed, and to do as we would not be done by. 4. When it is mistaken, and without just cause. 5. When it is greater in measure than the cause allows. 6. When it unfits us for our duty to God or man. 7. When it tends to the lessening of love and brotherly kindness, and the hindering of any good which we should do for others: much more when it breeds malice, and revenge, and contentions, and unpeaceableness in societies, oppression of inferiors, or dishonoring of superiors. [340] 8. When it stays too long, and does not cease when its lawful work is done. 9. When it is selfish and carnal, stirred up on account of some carnal interest, and used but as a means to a selfish, carnal, sinful end: as to be angry with men only for crossing your pride, or profit, or amusements, or any other fleshly will. In all these it is sinful.

**Directions meditative against sinful anger.**

**Direction 1. Remember that immoderate anger is an injury to humanity, and a rebel against the government of reason.**

It is without reason and against reason; whereas in man all passions should be obedient to reason. It is the misery of madness, and the crime of drunkenness, to be the suppressing and dethroning of our reason; and sinful anger is a short madness or drunkenness. Remember that you are a man, and scorn to subject yourself to a bestial fury.

**Direction 2. It is also against the government of God: for God governs the rational powers first, and the inferior by them.**

If you destroy the king's officers and judges, you oppose the government of the king. Is a man in passion fit to obey the commands of God, who has silenced his reason?

**Direction 3. Sinful passion is a pain and malady of the mind.**

And will you love or cherish your disease or pain? Do you not feel yourselves in pain and diseased while it is upon you? I do not think you would take all the world to live continually in that state yourselves. If you should be always so, what would you be good for, or what could you enjoy, or what comfort would your lives be to you? Why, if a long pain is so bad, a short one is not lovely. Do not willfully keep so troublesome a malady in your mind.

**Direction 4. Observe also what an enemy it is to the body itself.**

It inflames the blood, and stirs up diseases, and breeds such a bitter displeasure in the mind as tends to consume the strength of nature, and has cast many into acute, and many into chronic sicknesses, which have proved their death. And how comfortless a kind of death is this!

**Direction 5. Observe how unlovely and unpleasant it renders you to beholders; deforming the face, and taking away the lovable sweetness of it, which appears in a calm and loving temper.**

If you should be always so, would anybody love you? Or would they not get out of your way (if not lay hands on you), as they do with anything that is wild or mad? You would scarcely desire to have your picture drawn in your fury, until the frowning wrinkles and inflamed blood have returned to their places, and have left your face to its natural attractiveness. Do not love that which makes you so unlovely to all others.

**Direction 6. You should love it the less because it is a hurting passion, and an enemy to love and to another's good.**

You are never angry but it inclines you to hurt those that angered you, if not all others that stand in your way: it puts hurting thoughts into your mind, and hurting words into your mouths, and inclines you to strike or do some harm: and no men love a hurtful creature. Avoid therefore so harmful a passion.

**Direction 7. Indeed, note the tendency of it, and you will find that if it were not stopped it would tend to the very ruin of your brother, and end in his blood and your own damnation.**

How many thousands has anger murdered or ruined! It has caused wars, and filled the world with blood and cruelty! And should your hearts give such a fury a welcome?

**Direction 8. Consider how much other sin immoderate anger inclines men to. [341]**

It is the great crime of drunkenness that a man, not having the government of himself, is made liable by it to any wickedness: and so it is with immoderate anger. How many oaths and curses does it cause every day! How many rash and sinful actions! What villainy has anger not done! It has slandered, reviled, reproached, falsely accused, and injured many a thousand. It has murdered and ruined families, cities, and states. It has made parents kill their children, and children dishonor their parents. It has made kings oppress and murder their subjects, and subjects rebel and murder kings. What a world of sin is committed by sinful anger throughout all the world! How endless it would be to give you instances! David himself was once drawn by it to intend the murdering of all the family of Nabal. Its effects should make it odious to us.

**Direction 9. And it is much the worse in that it does not allow a man to sin alone, but stirs up others to do the like.**

Wrath kindles wrath, as fire kindles fire. It is two to one that when you are angry you will make others angry, or discontented, or troubled by your words or deeds. And you do not have the power of moderating them in it when you are done. You do not know what sin it may draw them to. It is the devil's bellows to kindle men's corruptions; and sets hearts, and families, and kingdoms in flames.

**Direction 10. Observe how unfit it makes you for any holy duty; for prayer, or meditation, or any communion with God.**

And that should be very unwelcome to a gracious soul which makes it unfit to speak to God, or to be employed in his worship. If you should go to prayer or other worship in your lunatic passion, may not God say, as the king of Gath did of David, "Do I have a shortage of fools ...?" Indeed, it unfits all the family, or church, or society where it comes, for the worship of God. Is the family fit for prayer when wrath has muddied and disturbed their minds? Indeed, it divides Christians and churches, and causes "disorder and every evil practice" (James 3:15–16).

**Direction 11. It is a great dishonor to the grace of God that a servant of his should show the world that grace is of no more force and efficacy, that it cannot rule a raging passion, nor so much as keep a Christian sober: that it possesses the soul with no more patience, nor fear of God, nor government over itself.**

O do not wrong God thus by the dishonoring of his grace and Spirit.

**Direction 12. It is a sin against conscience, always repented of and disowned by almost all when they come to themselves again, and a mere preparation for later sorrow.**

That therefore which we foreknow we must repent of afterward should be prevented and avoided by men that do not choose shame and sorrow.

*Objection 1.* But (you will say) I am of a hasty, choleric nature, and cannot help it.

*Answer.* That may strongly dispose you to anger, but cannot compel you to anything that is sinful: reason and will may yet command and master passion, if they do their duty. And when you know your disease and danger, you must watch the more.

*Objection 2.* But the provocation was so great, it would have angered anyone; who could help it?

*Answer.* It is your weakness that makes you think that anything can be great enough to dismiss a man's reason, and allow him to break the laws of God. That would have been small or nothing to a prepared mind which you call so great. You should rather say, God's majesty and dreadfulness are so great that I dared not offend him for any provocation. Has not God given you greater cause to obey than man can give you to sin?

*Objection 3.* But it is so sudden that I have no time for deliberation to prevent it.

*Answer.* Do you not have reason still about you? And should it not be as ready to rule as passion to rebel? Stop passion at first, and take time for deliberation.

*Objection 4.* But it is only short, and I am sorry for it when I am done.

*Answer.* But if it is evil, the shortest is a sin, and to be avoided: and when you know beforehand that you must be sorry after, why will you breed your own sorrow?

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[339] Duo maxime contraria sunt consilio. Ira et festinatio. Bias in Laert. (Two things are most contrary to good counsel: anger and haste. Bias, in Laertius.)

[340] Read Seneca *de Ira*, and be ashamed to fall short of a heathen.

[341] Proprium est magnitudinis veræ non sentire se esse permissum. Qui non irascitur, inconcussus injuria persistit: qui irascitur motus est. Senec. de Ira, lib. 3. c. 5. (It is the mark of true greatness not to feel that it has been [struck; see the translator's notes on "permissum"]. He who is not angered stands unshaken by the injury; he who is angered has been moved. Seneca, *On Anger*, book 3, chapter 5. Rendered loosely.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Title 7:** "Title" (Baxter's "*Tit.*") is his word for a heading or division. Chapter 7 is divided into ten such Titles, each on one passion; this is the seventh, on sinful anger. This file covers the first part of Title 7; the next file continues with Objection 5 and its answer.
- **"Directions meditative":** Baxter's sub-heading, kept in his word order. "Meditative" Directions are matters to consider and think over, as distinct from practical Directions (things to do); we have not checked whether a "practical" heading follows later in this Title.
- **passion; passions:** kept as Baxter's word for the emotions and affections. "A man in passion" (Direction 2) and "sinful passion" (Direction 3) mean a man in a fit of anger, and anger as a disordered emotion.
- **emotional displeasure:** Baxter's "passionate displacency" ("displacency" = displeasure; "passionate" per the glossary). **perceived evil:** his "apprehended evil." **thwart or deprive us of:** his "cross or hinder us of."
- **behavior:** Baxter's "carriage" (point 3). **lessening:** his "abatement" (point 7). **amusements:** his "sports" (point 9). **comfortless:** his "uncomfortable" (Direction 4), in the older sense of without comfort. **face:** his "countenance" and "visage" (Direction 5). **lovable:** his "amiable." **attractiveness:** his "comeliness." **get out of your way:** his "go out of your way," which today would wrongly suggest making a special effort. **as they do with:** his "as they do by."
- **harm; harmful:** Baxter's "mischief; mischievous" (Direction 6). **love it the less:** his "love it the worse." **Indeed:** his "Nay." **note:** his "mark" ("note the tendency of it") (Direction 7). **ruined:** his "undone." **a welcome:** his "entertainment," in the sense of hospitality.
- **reviled:** Baxter's "railed" (Direction 8). **intend:** his "purpose" (as a verb).
- **when you are done:** Baxter's "when you have done" (Direction 9 and Objection 4). **in flames:** his "in a flame."
- **lunatic passion:** Baxter's "bedlam passion" (Direction 10). "Bedlam" was the London hospital for the insane (Bethlem), and the word meant "mad." His point is the link between anger and madness.
- **always repented of:** Baxter's "still repented of" (Direction 12), "still" in the older sense of "always." Likewise "always so" for his "still so" (Direction 3).
- **choleric** (Objection 1): kept. It is the old humoral term for a temperament inclined to anger (from "choler," yellow bile), parallel to "melancholy"; it means hot-tempered.
- **compel:** Baxter's "necessitate." **do their duty:** his "do their office." **who could help it?:** his "who could choose?" **dismiss a man's reason:** his "discharge a man's reason," that is, release reason from its post. **dared not:** his "durst not." **dreadfulness:** kept, in the sense of awe-inspiring majesty.
- **strength of nature** (Direction 4): kept; it means the body's natural vigor. Baxter's view that anger inflames the blood and breeds acute and chronic diseases reflects the medicine of his day; kept as his claim.
- **1 Samuel 21:15 (flagged):** Baxter quotes Achish king of Gath, "Have I need of mad-men?" (King James, "Have I need of mad men?"), without a reference; the reference is ours and is not in Baxter's text. We quote the NET, which reads "Do I have a shortage of fools so that you have brought me this man to display his insanity in front of me?" The NET's "fools" loses the word "mad-men" on which Baxter builds (his "bedlam passion," anger as madness); the NET's "insanity" later in the verse keeps the idea, but Baxter does not quote that part.
- **James 3:15–16:** Baxter cites verses 15 and 16; the words he quotes are from verse 16 (NET checked). The King James has "confusion and every evil work"; the NET has "disorder and every evil practice." The sense he uses (anger breeds division and evil deeds) survives.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "to do as we would not be done by" (compare Matthew 7:12); David's resolve against "all the family of Nabal" (1 Samuel 25:21–22, 34); "when they come to themselves again" (compare Luke 15:17).
- **Bias** (note 339): one of the Seven Sages of Greece; Baxter cites the saying from Diogenes Laertius. We have not checked the saying against Laertius.
- **Seneca *de Ira*** (notes 340, 341): Seneca's treatise *On Anger*. Baxter's "come short of a heathen" means fall short of a pagan writer's standard.
- **"permissum"** (note 341): printed as in the source. As printed ("not to feel that it has been permitted") the sentence makes little sense; it is probably a misprint for "percussum" ("struck"), which fits the next sentence about standing unshaken by injury. We have not checked the reading against Seneca's text, so the bracketed translation follows the probable reading.
- **Numbered points inline:** in the paragraph on when anger is sinful, Baxter runs points 1-9 inside one paragraph; kept so, to keep his sentence boundaries.
