# Chapter 4: Directions 12 to 18 against greed, and the conclusion of the section

*This part begins in the middle of the Directions against Greed, at Direction 12; no section heading is repeated here.*

**Direction 12. Remember that God has promised to provide for you, and that you will want nothing that is good for you, if you will live above these worldly things, and seek first his kingdom, and the righteousness of it.** And can you not trust his promise? If you truly believe that he is God, and that he is true, and that his particular providence extends to the very numbering of your hairs, [268] you will surely trust him, rather than trust to your own forecast and effort. Do you think his provision is not better for you than your own? All your own care cannot keep you alive an hour, nor can it prosper any of your labors, if you provoke him to blast them. And if you are not content with his provisions, nor submit yourselves to the disposal of his love and wisdom, you disoblige God, and provoke him to leave you to the fruits of your own care and diligence: and then you will find that it would have been your wiser way to have trusted God.

*In the margin: The mischiefs of a worldly mind.*

**Direction 13. Think often on the dreadful importance and effects of the love of riches, or a worldly mind.** [269] 1. It is a most certain sign of a state of death and misery, where it has the upper hand. It is the departing of the heart from God to creatures. See the malignity of it before. Good men have been overtaken with heinous sins; but it is hard to find where Scripture calls any of them greedy. A heart secretly clinging most to this present world and its prosperity, is the very killing sin of every hypocrite, yes, and of all ungodly men. 2. Worldliness makes the word unprofitable; and keeps men from believing and repenting, and coming home to God, and minding seriously the everlasting world. What so much hinders the conversion of sinners, as the love and cares of earthly things? They cannot serve God and mammon: their treasure and hearts cannot chiefly be both in heaven and earth! They will not yield to the terms of Christ that love this world: they will not forsake all for a treasure in heaven. In a word, as you heard, the love of money is the root of all evil, and the love of the Father is not in the lovers of the world. [270] 3. It destroys holy meditation and conversation, and turns the thoughts to worldly things: and it corrupts prayer, and makes it but a means to serve the flesh, and therefore makes it odious to God. 4. It is the great hindrance of men's necessary preparation for death and judgment, and steals away their hearts and time till it is too late. 5. It is the great cause of contentions even among the nearest relations; and the cause of the wars and calamities of nations; and of the woeful divisions and persecutions of the church; when a worldly generation think that their worldly interest engages them, against self-denying and spiritual principles, practices, and persons. 6. It is the great cause of all the injustice, and oppression, and cruelty that rages in the world. They would do as they would be done by, were it not for the love of money. It makes men perfidious and false to all their friends and engagements: no vows to God, nor obligations to men, will hold a lover of the world. [271] The world is his god, and his worldly interest is his rule and law. 7. It is the great destroyer of charity and good works. No more is done for God and the poor, because the love of the world forbids it. 8. It disorders and profanes families; and betrays the souls of children and servants to the devil. It turns out prayer, and reading the Scripture, and good books, and all serious speeches of the life to come, because their hearts are taken up with the world, and they have no relish of anything but the provisions of their flesh. Even the Lord's own day cannot be reserved for holy works, nor a duty performed, but the world is interposing, or diverting the mind. 9. It tempts men to sin against their knowledge, and to forsake the truth, and fit themselves to the rising side, and save their bodies and estates, whatever becomes of their souls. It is the very price that the devil gives for souls! With this he bought the soul of Judas, who went to the Pharisees, with a "What will you give me, and I will deliver him to you." With this he attempted Christ himself, Matthew 4:9, "I will give you all these things if you throw yourself to the ground and worship me." It is the cause of apostasy and unfaithfulness to God. [272] And it is the price that sinners sell their God, their conscience, and their salvation for. 10. It deprives the soul of holy communion with God, and comfort from him, and of all foretaste of the life to come, and finally of heaven itself. [273] For as the love of the world keeps out the love of God and heaven, it must needs keep out the hopes and comforts which should arise from holy love. It would do much to cure the love of money, and of the world, if you knew how pernicious a sin it is. [274]

**Direction 14. Remember how base a sin it is, and how dishonorable and debasing to the mind of man.** If earth is baser than heaven, and money than God, then an earthly mind is baser than a heavenly mind. As the serpent's feeding on the dust is a baser life than that of angels, that are employed in admiring, and obeying, and praising the Most Holy God.

**Direction 15. Call yourselves to a daily reckoning, how you lay out all that God commits to your trust; and try whether it is so as you would hear of it at judgment.** If you did but use to sit in judgment daily upon yourselves, as those that believe the judgment of God, it would make you more careful to use well what you have, than to get more; and it would quench your thirst after plenty and prosperity, when you perceived you must give so strict an account of it. The flesh itself will less desire it, when it finds it may not have the use of it. [275]

**Direction 16. When you find your greed most eager and dangerous, resolve most to cross it, and give more to pious or charitable uses than at another time.** For a man has reason to fly furthest from that sin, which he is most in danger of. And the acts tend to the increase of the habit. Obeying your greed does increase it: and so the contrary acts, and the disobeying and displeasing it, do destroy it. This course will bring your greed into a despair of attaining its desire; and so will make it sit down and give over the pursuit. It is an open protesting against every greedy desire; and an effective kind of repenting; and a wise and honest disarming sin, and turning its motions against itself, to its own destruction. Use it thus often, and greed will think it wisdom to be quiet.

**Direction 17. Above all take care that you do not think of reconciling God and mammon, and mixing heaven and earth to be your happiness, and of dreaming that you may keep heaven for a reserve at last, when the world has been loved as your best, so long as you could keep it.** Nothing so much defends worldliness, as a cheating hope, that you have it but in a subdued, pardoned degree; and that you are not worldlings when you are. And nothing so much supports this hope, as because you confess that heaven only must be your last refuge, and full happiness, and therefore you do something for it on the side. But is not the world more loved, more sought, more delighted in, and more tightly held? Has it not more of your hearts, your delight, desire, and effort? If you cannot let go all for heaven, and forsake all this world for a treasure above, you cannot be Christ's true disciples, Luke 14:26, 27, 30, 33.

**Direction 18. If ever you would overcome the love of the world, your great care must be to mortify the flesh; for the world is desired but as its provision.** A mortified man has no need of that which is a pleasure-seeker's happiness. Quench your hydropical, feverish thirst, and then you will not make such a stir for drink. Cure the disease which enrages your appetite; and that is the safest and cheapest way of satisfying it. Then you will be thankful to God, when you look on other men's wealth and gallantry, that you do not need these things. [276] And you will think what a trouble and burden, and interruption of your better work and comfort it would be to you, to have so much land, and so many servants, and goods, and business, and persons to mind, as rich men have. And how much better you can enjoy God and yourself in a more retired, quiet state of life. But of this more in the next part.

Did men but know how much of an ungodly, damnable state consists in the love of the world; and how much it is the enemy of souls; and how much of our religion consists in the contempt and conquest of it; and what is the meaning of their renouncing the world in their baptismal covenant; and how many millions the love of the world will damn forever; they would not make such a stir for nothing, and spend all their days in providing for their perishing flesh; nor think them happiest that are richest; nor "boast themselves of their heart's desire, and bless the greedy whom the Lord abhors," Psalm 10:3. They would not think that so small a sin which Christians should not so much as "name," but in detestation, Ephesians 5:3; when God has resolved that "the greedy ... will not inherit the kingdom of God," 1 Corinthians 6:10; Ephesians 5:5; and a Christian must not so much as eat with them, 1 Corinthians 5:11. Did Christ say in vain, "Watch out and guard yourself from all types of greed," Luke 12:15. "The one who builds his house by unjust gain is as good as dead. He does this so he can build his nest way up high and escape the clutches of disaster," Habakkuk 2:9. Oh what deserving servants has the world, that will serve it so diligently, so constantly, and at so dear a rate, when they beforehand know, that besides a little transitory, deluding pleasure, it will pay them with nothing but everlasting shame! Oh wonderful deceiving power, of such an empty shadow, or rather wonderful folly of mankind! that when so many ages have been deceived before us, and almost every one at death confesses it did but deceive them, so many still should be deceived, and take no warning by such a world of examples! I conclude with Hebrews 13:5, "Your conduct must be free from the love of money, and you must be content with what you have, for he has said, 'I will never leave you and I will never abandon you.'"

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). Every quoted verse here was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Section position:** this range starts at Direction 12 of the Directions against Greed and ends the section ("But of this more in the next part"; the next part begins at the "PART VII" line after the range). The footnote [277] that follows in the source belongs to the next range.
- **Word swaps:** covetous / covetousness to greedy / greed (the standing decision); hath etc. to modern verbs; "forecast and industry" to "forecast and effort," and "desire, and industry" to "desire, and effort" (his "industry" meant diligent work; the swap to "effort" follows the endeavor-to-effort decision, but "industry" itself is not in the recorded list); "it had been your wiser way" to "it would have been your wiser way"; "so like to devils" is kept; "sensualist's felicity" to "pleasure-seeker's happiness"; "felicity" elsewhere to "happiness"; "holy ... conference" to "holy ... conversation" (his "conference" is talk); "hinderance" to "hindrance"; "faster held" to "more tightly held"; "sure trust" to "surely trust"; "on the bye" to "on the side"; "pretended holy catholic church" to "supposed holy catholic church" (note 269); "rapines" to "plunderings" (note 269); "abhorreth" to "abhors"; "love of money" and "worldly interest" as written.
- **Kept as Baxter's words:** *disoblige* (here "offend"); *forecast* (planning ahead, forethought); *gallantry* (fine show, splendor); *hydropical* (dropsical, with a swollen body and an unquenchable thirst); *mammon*; *worldlings*; *mortify* (to put to death, as in Romans 8:13); *perfidious*; *the flesh*; *estates* (property); *the habit* (Direction 16: the settled disposition built up by repeated acts, in the glossary sense "habit"; his argument is that acts form habits); *catholic* in note 269 (universal), *simony* (buying and selling church office).
- **Direction 13, "the love of money is the root of all evil":** an unmarked allusion to 1 Timothy 6:10. The NET reads "the love of money is the root of all evils." Baxter's "as you heard" refers back to earlier in the section; we have not checked where. "The love of the Father is not in the lovers of the world" alludes to 1 John 2:15; the NET reads "If anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him." "They cannot serve God and mammon" alludes to Matthew 6:24 and Luke 16:13.
- **Direction 12, "seek first his kingdom, and the righteousness of it":** an unquoted allusion to Matthew 6:33. NET: "But above all pursue his kingdom and righteousness, and all these things will be given to you as well." The NET's "above all pursue" differs from the King James "seek ye first"; Baxter's wording is kept since it is not in quotation marks. "The very numbering of your hairs": Matthew 10:30 (NET: "Even all the hairs on your head are numbered") and Luke 12:7, given in his note 268.
- **Matthew 4:9 (flagged):** Baxter writes "All these will I give thee, if thou wilt fall down and worship me." The NET reads "I will give you all these things if you throw yourself to the ground and worship me." The sense is the same; the NET's "throw yourself to the ground" is stronger than "fall down." The Judas sentence, "What will you give me, and I will deliver him to you," is Baxter's quotation of Matthew 26:15 (which he does not cite). It is close to the King James; the NET reads "What will you give me to betray him into your hands?" The NET adds the thirty silver coins. We kept his wording in his quotation marks and give the NET here.
- **Psalm 10:3 (flagged):** Baxter's quoted words, "boast themselves of their heart's desire, and bless the covetous whom the Lord abhorreth," follow the King James. The NET reads "Yes, the wicked man boasts because he gets what he wants; the one who robs others curses and rejects the Lord." The NET has no "bless the covetous," and it reads the last clause as the one who robs others cursing and rejecting the Lord, not the Lord abhorring the greedy man whom the wicked bless. Baxter's argument (the Lord abhors the greedy) leans on the King James reading. His wording is kept, with "covetous" swapped to "greedy," and the sense difference is flagged.
- **Ephesians 5:3 (flagged):** Baxter has Christians not "so much as 'name'" greed "but in detestation." That is the King James "let it not be once named among you." The NET reads "among you there must not be either sexual immorality, impurity of any kind, or greed," with nothing about naming. His "name" is kept in quotation marks as his; the NET has no word to quote for it. Ephesians 5:5 is cited only. The NET calls the greedy person "an idolater."
- **1 Corinthians 6:10:** Baxter quotes "covetous shall not inherit the kingdom of God." The NET: "thieves, the greedy, drunkards, the verbally abusive, and swindlers will not inherit the kingdom of God." We quote "the greedy ... will not inherit the kingdom of God." **1 Corinthians 5:11** (cited only): the NET has "not to associate with anyone who calls himself a Christian who is sexually immoral, or greedy ... Do not even eat with such a person."
- **Luke 12:15:** NET wording. Baxter's "Take heed and beware of covetousness" is "Watch out and guard yourself from all types of greed." The NET goes on, "because one's life does not consist in the abundance of his possessions."
- **Habakkuk 2:9 (flagged):** Baxter quotes the King James, "Woe to him that coveteth an evil covetousness to his house," etc. The NET reads "The one who builds his house by unjust gain is as good as dead. He does this so he can build his nest way up high and escape the clutches of disaster." The NET has no "woe" no "coveteth an evil covetousness," and speaks of escaping disaster, not "the power of evil." Baxter's point, the greedy man's nest set on high, is preserved; the "woe" is lost.
- **Hebrews 13:5:** NET wording. Baxter's "Let your conversation be without covetousness" is the King James; the NET's "Your conduct must be free from the love of money" supports the glossary decision to render "conversation" as "conduct." **Flag:** the King James has "without covetousness" where the NET has "free from the love of money"; Baxter's argument against greed fits either, but the NET names the love of money rather than covetousness as such. "Such things as ye have" is "what you have" in the NET. The words "I will never leave thee nor forsake thee" are "I will never leave you and I will never abandon you."
- **Luke 14:26, 27, 30, 33:** cited only. Direction 17 leans on verse 33 (forsaking all).
- **Note 270:** a list of references. Proverbs 28:9 (NET: the one who turns his ear from the law, even his prayer is an abomination) and Proverbs 18:8 (NET: the words of a gossip) do not obviously speak of greed or riches; they may be misprints for other verses (Proverbs 28:22 speaks of one who "hastens after riches"), but we have not confirmed this and keep Baxter's references. The quotation from Proverbs 28:20 is his King James; the NET reads "the one who hastens to gain riches will not go unpunished," which is close in sense ("innocent" in the King James probably means "unpunished" here).
- **Note 269:** a long note of Baxter's polemic against the Roman Church and the Jesuits, and against worldly church officers generally. It is kept exactly as he wrote it, and is his view, not ours. The historical claims about Rome, the Jesuits, and the monks are Baxter's and are not confirmed here. "Abbas Urspergens." is the Abbot of Ursperg (Burchard of Ursperg is probably meant; we have not confirmed it). "Fortun. Galindas" and "Arcan. Soci. Jesu" are as printed. The reference to John of Salisbury is probably to his *Policraticus*, but we have not confirmed it. The passage printed "quid erupit" is kept as printed. The Latin in this note is rendered loosely.
- **Note 275:** Baxter's "Victor. Ut." is probably Victor of Vita (Victor Uticensis), as in earlier files; we have not confirmed it, nor which Eugenius is meant. "Plutarch. de Tranquil. Anim." is probably Plutarch's work on tranquility of mind; the expansion is ours. **Note 276:** "Laert. in Socr." is probably Diogenes Laertius on Socrates, and the second Latin passage is cited to Petrarch; both are Baxter's citations, with our expansions unconfirmed.
- **Note 274:** a sheep image: Baxter says Christ's mark is plainest on the sheep that are shorn and wears out when the fleece grows long. We read this as a figure for being stripped of wealth, but he does not say so.
- **"attempted Christ" (Direction 13, point 9):** Baxter's "attempted" means tried or tempted; kept as written (the reference he gives is Matthew 4:9, the temptation).
- **"disoblige":** Baxter's sense is "offend" (the opposite of oblige). Left as written.
- **Margin note "The mischiefs of a worldly mind":** "mischiefs" means harms. Kept as written.
- **Baptismal covenant:** Baxter's phrase for the promise made at baptism to renounce the world, the flesh, and the devil.
- **The conclusion** (the paragraph beginning "Did men but know"): it has no Direction number. It is rendered as a closing paragraph.
