# Chapter 8: Part 4, Title 1, Causes of gluttony 7-10, and the greatness of the sin (points 1-2)

*This section begins partway through Part 4, Title 1 (Directions against gluttony), in the middle of section II, Baxter's list of the causes of gluttony, at cause 7; it continues from the previous file.*

7. Another cause of gluttony is that rich men are not acquainted with the true use of riches, nor think of the account which they must make to God of all they have. [400] They think that their riches are their own, and that they may use them as they please; or that they are given them as plentiful provisions for their flesh, and they may use them for themselves, to satisfy their own desires, as long as they drop some crumbs, or scraps, or small matters to the poor. They think they may be saved just in the same way that the rich man in Luke 16 was damned; and he that would have warned his five brothers that they not come to that place of torment is yet himself no warning to his followers. They are clothed in purple and fine linen or silk, and fare sumptuously or luxuriously every day; and have their good things in this life, and perhaps think they merit by giving the scraps to Lazarus (which it is likely that rich man also did). But God will one day make them know that the richest were but his stewards, and should have made a better distribution of his provisions, and a better improvement of his talents; and that they had nothing of all their riches given them for any hurtful or unprofitable pleasing of their appetites, nor had more allowance for luxury than the poor. If they knew the right use of riches, it would reform them.

8. Another cause of gluttony is their unfamiliarity with those rational and spiritual exercises in which the delightful fruits of abstinence most appear. A man that is but a painstaking, serious student, in any noble study whatsoever, finds a great deal of serenity and aptitude come by temperance, and a great deal of cloudy mistiness on his mind and dullness on his invention come by fullness and excess: and a man that is used to holy contemplations, meditation, reading, prayer, self-examination, or any spiritual converse above, or with his heart, easily finds a very great difference; how abstinence helps, and luxury and fullness hinder him. Now these epicures have no acquaintance with any such holy or manly works, nor any mind for them, and are therefore unacquainted with the sweetness and benefit of abstinence; and having no taste or trial of its benefits, they cannot value it. They have nothing to do when they rise from eating, but a little talk about their worldly business, or exchange compliments and talk with company who expect them, or go to their amusements to empty their paunches for another meal, and quicken their appetites lest luxury should decay: as the Israelites worshiped the golden calf (and as the heathens their god Bacchus), "the people sat down to eat and drink, and they rose up to play" (Exodus 32:6). [401] Their diet is fitted to their work; their idle or worldly lives agree with gluttony; but were they accustomed to better work, they would find a necessity of a better diet.

9. Another great cause of gluttony is men's beastly ignorance of what is hurtful or helpful to their very health: [402] they make their appetites their rule for the quantity and quality of their food: and they think that nature teaches them to do so, because it gives them such an appetite, and because it is the measure to a beast: and to prove themselves beasts, they therefore take it for their measure; as if their natures were not rational, but only sensory; or nature had not given them reason to be the superior and governor of sense. As if they did not know that God gives the brutes an appetite more bounded, because they do not have reason to bound it; and does not give them the temptation of your delicate varieties; or gives them a digestion answerable to their appetites; and yet gives man to be the rational governor of those of them that are for his special service and apt to exceed: and if his swine, his horses, and his cattle were all left to their appetites, they would live but a little while. [403] If promiscuous generating is not lawful in mankind, which is lawful in brutes, why should they not confess the same of the appetite. Men have so much love of life and fear of death, that if they did but know how much their gluttony hastens their death, it would do more to restrain it with most, than the fear of death eternal does. But they judge of their digestion by their present feeling: if they do not feel their stomachs sick, or disposed to vomit, or if no present pain corrects them, they think they have eaten no more than does them good. But of this more later in the directions.

10. Another great cause of gluttony is that it has grown the commonest custom, and not being known, is in no disgrace, unless men eat till they spew, or to some extraordinary measure. And so the measure which every man sees another use, he thinks is moderation, and is fit for him: whereas the ignorance of medicine and matters of their own health has made gluttony almost as common as eating, with those that are not restrained by need or sickness. And so every man is an example of evil to another, and encourage one another in the sin. If gluttony were but in as much disgrace as whoredom, indeed, or as drunkenness is, and as easily known, and as commonly taken notice of, it would contribute much to a common reformation.

**III. The Greatness of the Sin of Gluttony.**

To know the greatness of the sin is the chief part of the cure, with those that do but believe that there is a God: I shall therefore next tell you of its nature, effects, and circumstances, which make it great, and therefore should make it odious to all.

1. Luxury and gluttony is a sin exceedingly contrary to the love of God: it is idolatry: it has the heart, which God should have; and therefore gluttons are commonly and well called belly-gods, and god-bellies, because that love, that care, that delight, that service and diligence which God should have, is given by the glutton to his belly and his throat. [404] He loves the pleasing of his appetite better than the pleasing of God; his dishes are more delightful to him than any holy exercise is; his thoughts are more frequent and more sweet of his belly than of God or godliness; his care and labor are more that he may be pleased in foods and drinks, than that he may secure his salvation, and be justified and sanctified. And, indeed, the Scripture gives them this name, "Their end is destruction, their god is the belly, they exult in their shame, and they think about earthly things" (Philippians 3:19), being enemies to the cross of Christ, that is, to bearing the cross for Christ, and to the crucifying of the flesh, and to the mortifying, suffering parts of religion. Indeed, such a devouring idol is the belly, that it swallows up more by intemperance and excess than all other idols in the world do. And remember that the very life of the sin is in the appetite and heart: when a man's heart is set upon his belly, though he fares ever so poorly through necessity, he is a glutton in heart. When you make a great matter of it, what you shall eat and drink as to the delight, and when you take it for a great loss or suffering if you fare poorly, and are troubled at it, and your thoughts and talk are of your belly, and you do not have that indifference whether your fare is coarse or pleasant (so long as it is wholesome), as all temperate persons have, this is the heart of gluttony, and is the heart's forsaking of God, and making the appetite its god.

2. Gluttony is self-murder; though it does not kill suddenly, it kills surely; like the dropsy, which kills as it fills, by degrees. [405] Very many of the wisest physicians believe that of those who outlive their childhood, there is scarcely one of twenty, indeed, or of a hundred that dies, but gluttony or excess in eating or drinking is a principal cause of their death, though not the most immediate cause. It is thought to kill a hundred to one of all that die as adults. And it will not let them die easily and quickly, but torments them first with manifold diseases while they live. You eat more than nature can perfectly digest, and because you do not feel it trouble you or make you sick, you think it does not hurt you; whereas it does by degrees first alter and corrupt the temperament of the blood and humors, making it a crude, undigested, unnatural thing, unfit for the due nutrition of the parts; turning the nourishing mass into a burdensome, excrementitious mixture, abounding with saline or tartareous matter, and consisting more of a pituitous slime, or redundant serosity, than of that sweet, nutrimental milk of nature, quickened with those spirits and well-proportioned heat, which should make it fit to be the oil of life. And our candle either sparkles away with salt, or runs away because there is some thief in it, or goes out because the oil is turned into water, or at once wastes and runs about through the inconsistent softness of its oil: hence it is that one part is tainted with corruption, and another consumes as destitute of fit nutriment; and the vessels secretly obstructed by the grossness or other unfitness of the blood to run its circle and perform its offices, is the cause of a multitude of lamentable diseases. The frigid disorders of the brain, the soporous and comatose effects, the lethargy, carus, and apoplexy, the palsy, convulsion, epilepsy, vertigo, catarrhs, the headache, and often the frenzy and madness, all come from these effects of gluttony and excess, which are made upon the blood and humors. The asthma usually, and the phthisis or consumption, and the pleurisy and peripneumony, and the hemoptoic passion, often come from this. Indeed, the very syncopes or swooning, palpitations of the heart, and faintings, which men think rather come from weakness, usually come either from oppression of nature by these secret excrements or putrilaginous blood, or else from a weakness contracted by the inaptitude of the blood to nourish us, being corrupted by excess. The loathing of food and lack of appetite is ordinarily from the crudities or disorders caused by this excess; indeed, the very canine appetite which would always have more is caused by a corruption in the humors thus contracted. The pains of the stomach, vomitings, the cholera, hiccups, inflammations, thirsts, are usually from this cause. The wind colic, the iliac passion, looseness, and fluxes, the tenesmus and ulcers, the worms and other troubles in those parts, are usually from this. The obstructions of the liver, the jaundice, inflammations, abscesses and ulcers, scirrhus, and dropsy, are commonly from this. Hence also usually are inflammations, pains, obstructions, and scirrhus of the spleen. Hence commonly is the stone, nephritic torments, and stoppages of urine, and ulcers of the kidneys and bladder. Hence commonly is the scurvy and most of the fevers which are found in the world, and bring such multitudes to the grave. Even those that immediately are caused by colds, disorders of the air or infections, are often caused principally by long excess, which corrupts the humors, and prepares them for the disease. Hence also are gouts and hysterical disorders, and diseases of the eyes and other exterior parts. So that we may well say that gluttony enriches landlords, fills the churchyards, and hastens multitudes untimely to their ends. [406] Perhaps you will say that the most temperate have diseases: to which experience teaches me to answer that usually children are permitted to be voracious and gluttonous, either in quantity or in quality, eating raw fruits and things unwholesome; and so when gluttony has bred the disease, or laid in the matter, then all the temperance that can be used is little enough to keep it under all their life after. And a great many that have been brought to the doors of death by excess have been preserved after many years to a reasonable age by abstinence, and many totally freed from their diseases. Read Cornaro's treatise of himself, and Lessius, and Sir William Vaughan, etc. (Though yet I persuade no one without necessity to their exceeding strictness.) Judge now what a murderer gluttony is, and what an enemy to mankind.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[400] Hic est mos nobilium ante alios: artes quæ liberales fuerunt, mechanicæ evasere: ipsique qui bellorum duces, philosophi, rectores urbium, ac patres patriæ esse solent, venatores; atque aucupes facti sunt, utque intelligas nullam esse reliquam spem salutis, nobilitati tribuitur quod est Gulæ. aut proculdubio vanitatis. Petrarch. (English, rendered loosely: This is the custom of the nobles above others: the arts that were liberal have turned into mechanical ones; and those who used to be generals in war, philosophers, rulers of cities, and fathers of their country have become hunters and fowlers; and, that you may understand that no hope of soundness is left, what belongs to gluttony, or no doubt to vanity, is credited to nobility. Petrarch.)

[401] 1 Corinthians 10:7.

[402] Of this see more in my book of *Self-denial*.

[403] See Plutarch's *Precepts of Health*.

[404] Romans 16:17–18. They do not serve the Lord Jesus, but their own bellies.

[405] It is a common saying that Gula plures occidit quam gladius (Gluttony kills more than the sword). Quicquid avium volitat, quicquid piscium natat, quicquid ferarum discurrit, nostris sepelitur ventribus. Quære nunc cur subito moriamur? Quia mortibus vivimus. Senec. (Whatever bird flies, whatever fish swims, whatever wild beast roams, is buried in our bellies. Ask now why we die suddenly? Because we live on deaths. Seneca.) Jerome says that he had read of some that had been sick of the arthritis and podagra, that were cured by being brought to poverty by confiscation of their estates, and so brought to a poor diet.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Where this section sits:** Chapter 8, Part 4 ("Directions for governing the taste and appetite"), Title 1 ("Directions against gluttony"). "Title" (Baxter's "Tit.") is his word for a heading or division within a Part. Title 1 is laid out in four sections: I. what gluttony is; II. its causes; III. its odiousness ("The Greatness of the Sin of Gluttony"); IV. helps against it. This file runs from cause 7 of section II into point 2 of section III; the next file continues it.
- **Exodus 32:6:** Baxter quotes "They sat down to eat and drink, and rose up to play," giving the reference before the quotation; we moved the reference after the quotation, per our convention. The NET verse reads "the people sat down to eat and drink, and they rose up to play"; we quote that part. Footnote [401] points to 1 Corinthians 10:7, where Paul quotes the same words (NET: "The people sat down to eat and drink and rose up to play"), introduced by "So do not be idolaters," which matches Baxter's point about idolatry.
- **Philippians 3:19:** Baxter quotes the King James, "Whose end is destruction, whose god is their belly, who glory in their shame, who mind earthly things." NET: "Their end is destruction, their god is the belly, they exult in their shame, and they think about earthly things." The NET keeps "their god is the belly," the phrase Baxter builds on, so the sense holds. His following words "being enemies to the cross of Christ" paraphrase verse 18 (NET: "they are the enemies of the cross of Christ"); we kept his wording outside the quotation marks, as he has it.
- **Romans 16:18 (footnote [404], flagged):** Baxter's "They serve not the Lord Jesus, but their own bellies" is a loose King James quotation, not in quotation marks in the source; we kept his wording, modernized. The NET reads "do not serve our Lord Christ, but their own appetites." The NET's "appetites" drops the word "bellies," which is the word that ties this note to Baxter's "belly-gods" in the body.
- **Luke 16 (unmarked allusions in cause 7):** Baxter's "warned his five brethren that they come not to that place of torment" alludes to Luke 16:28 (NET: "to warn them so that they don't come into this place of torment"); "clothed in purple and fine linen ... fare sumptuously ... every day" to Luke 16:19 (NET: "dressed in purple and fine linen and who feasted sumptuously every day"); "have their good things in this life" to Luke 16:25 (NET: "in your lifetime you received your good things"). Baxter does not mark these as quotations, so his wording is kept. His "or silk" and "or deliciously" are his own additions to the allusion. "Talents" alludes to the parable of the talents (Matthew 25:14–30); reference not in Baxter's text.
- **Medical content (dated):** Section III, point 2, is 17th-century medicine built on the theory of the four humors (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile) and on "spirits" and "heat" carried in the blood. Baxter's lists of diseases caused by gluttony, and his figures ("scarcely one of twenty, indeed, or of a hundred"; "a hundred to one"), are his and the physicians' he cites, and are not modern medical claims. We kept his medical terms rather than substitute modern diagnoses, which would assert equivalences we cannot vouch for. Brief glosses, for orientation only: *humors* (spelling modernized from "humours"), the body's fluids in that theory; *crude, undigested* (Baxter: "unconcocted"), not properly processed by digestion; *excrementitious*, of the nature of waste matter; *tartareous*, like tartar, a gritty deposit; *pituitous*, phlegmy; *serosity*, watery fluid; *nutrimental*, nourishing; *soporous*, causing heavy sleep; *carus*, a deep stupor; *peripneumony*, inflammation of the lungs; *hemoptoic passion*, spitting of blood; *canine appetite*, a ravenous, insatiable hunger; *cholera*, in the older sense, a disorder of the bowels with vomiting, attributed to bile; *iliac passion*, a severe bowel obstruction with pain; *looseness*, diarrhea; *tenesmus*, painful straining; *scirrhus* (Baxter: "schirrus"), a hard swelling; *the stone*, kidney or bladder stones; *nephritic*, of the kidneys; *phthisis*, consumption, a wasting lung disease; *syncope*, fainting. These glosses are approximate.
- **The candle image:** Baxter compares life to a candle burning on the "oil" of the blood. A "thief" in a candle was a fragment of wick or a fault that makes it gutter and run; we believe this is the sense but have not confirmed it. "Inconsistent softness" means softness without firmness (lacking consistency), not unevenness.
- **"Gluttony enriches landlords":** kept as printed. The sense is not clear to us; we have not guessed at it.
- **Word swaps (Baxter's word, then ours):** concoct / concoction to digest / digestion; unconcocted to undigested; vitiate / vitiated / viciousness (in the humors) to corrupt / corrupted / corruption; distempers to disorders; humours to humors; phrensy to frenzy; schirrus to scirrhus; scorbute to scurvy; reins to kidneys; wind cholic to wind colic; hysterical affects to hysterical disorders; physic to medicine; meats (food in general) to foods; meat (food) to food; want (of appetite) to lack; want (poverty) to need; presently to at once; anon to later; overlive to outlive; scarce to scarcely; yea and nay (intensifiers) to indeed; painful (taking pains) to painstaking; deliciously (luxuriously) to luxuriously; unacquaintedness to unfamiliarity; compliment (verb) to exchange compliments; sensitive to sensory; sports to amusements; fare hardly to fare poorly; indifferency to indifference; accidents (attendant qualities) to circumstances; die at age to die as adults; competent age to reasonable age; abundance (a great many people) to a great many; Hierom to Jerome; Senec. to Seneca (in the translation only; the abbreviation is kept in the Latin as printed).
- **Words kept:** *luxury* means excess or indulgence in eating and drinking, not costly comfort only; *epicures*, those who live for the pleasures of the table; *invention*, the power to think up and devise; *improvement* (of talents), putting to profitable use; *answerable to*, corresponding to; *brutes*, animals; *temperament*, the mixture or balance of the body's qualities in humoral theory; *converse*, communion or dealings (here with God "above" or with one's own heart); *mortifying*, putting sin to death; *religion*, practiced piety.
- **Plain words kept:** "spew," "whoredom," "promiscuous generating," "paunches," as Baxter wrote them.
- **Grammar kept:** "every man is an example of evil to another, and encourage one another" (a shift from singular to plural in the source); "the vessels secretly obstructed ... is the cause" (plural subject, singular verb); "If promiscuous generating is not lawful ... why should they not confess the same of the appetite." ends with a period in the source, not a question mark.
- **"Scarcely one of twenty ... that dies, but gluttony ... is a principal cause":** Baxter's construction means that hardly one adult in twenty (or a hundred) dies without gluttony or excess being a principal cause.
- **Section heading:** "III. The Greatness of the Sin of Gluttony." is a centered heading in the source; we kept Baxter's Roman numeral.
- **Footnote [400] (Petrarch):** The Latin has a full stop after "Gulæ" in the middle of the sentence ("quod est Gulæ. aut proculdubio vanitatis"); kept as printed, read as a comma in the translation. "Spem salutis" could mean hope of safety, health, or salvation; we rendered it "soundness." We have not identified the passage in Petrarch's works.
- **Footnote [405]:** The quoted Latin is attributed by Baxter to Seneca; we have not checked the source. The report Baxter attributes to Jerome is kept as his; we have not located it. *Arthritis* and *podagra* (gout of the feet) kept.
- **Footnote [406]:** "In Hebr. Hom. 29" rendered "On Hebrews, Homily 29" (Chrysostom's homilies on the letter to the Hebrews). Not checked against the source.
- **Footnotes [402], [403]:** Baxter's "book of Self-denial" is his own work, which we italicize as a title as he gives it. Plutarch's "precepts of health" is italicized as a title; it is probably the work usually known in Latin as *De tuenda sanitate praecepta*, but we have not confirmed which edition Baxter used.
- **Authors named in the body:** Cornaro, Lessius, and Sir William Vaughan are named as Baxter gives them. Cornaro is probably Luigi Cornaro, the Venetian who wrote about his own recovery by a sober diet, and Lessius probably Leonard Lessius, the Jesuit who wrote on temperance in diet; we have not confirmed which works Baxter means. "Cornaro's Treatise of himself" is kept as "Cornaro's treatise of himself" (lower case, since it may not be a formal title).
- **Bacchus:** Baxter's parenthesis compares the Israelites' feast to heathen worship of Bacchus, the Roman god of wine; kept as his.
