# Chapter 9: Title 2, Against Profane Swearing (What an Oath Is; Swearing by Creatures; Laying the Hand on the Book)

**Title 2. Special directions against profane swearing, and using God's name irreverently and in vain.**

*In the margin: What an oath is.*

I. To swear is an affirming or denying of a thing, with an appeal to some other thing or person, as a witness of the truth, or avenger of the untruth, who is not producible as witness or judge in human courts. An affirmation or negation is the matter of an oath: the particular appeal is the form. It is not every appeal or attestation that makes an oath. [489] To appeal to such a witness as is credible and may be produced in the court, from a partial, incredible witness, is no oath. To appeal from an incompetent judge, or an inferior court, to a competent judge, or higher court, is no swearing. To say, I take the king for my witness, or I appeal to the king, is not to swear by the king; but to say, I take God to witness, or I appeal to God as the judge of the truth of what I say, is to swear by God. But to appeal to God as a righteous Judge, against the injustice or cruelty of men, without relation to his attesting or judging any affirmation or negation of our own, is no swearing by him, because there lacks the matter of an oath. An oath is an appeal to some supernatural or higher and more fearsome power than that of the court or person we swear to, to make our testimony the more credible, when other evidences of certainty or credibility are lacking. So that a legal testimony or appeal are not swearing.

*In the margin: What is a lawful oath.*

Swearing is either just and lawful, or sinful and abusive. To a just and lawful oath it is necessary, 1. That it be God alone ultimately that we swear by; because no witness and avenging judge above human courts can be appealed to but God: and therefore to swear by any creature properly and in the sense that God is sworn by, is to idolize it, and to ascribe to it the properties of God. [490] (Of which more later.) 2. It is necessary to a just oath that the matter be true as it is affirming or denying; and also if it be promissory, that the matter be, 1. Honest and lawful, 2. and possible. And where any one of these is lacking, it is unlawful. 3. It is needful that there be an honest end; for the end is a principal ingredient in all moral good and evil. 4. It is needful that it be done upon a sufficient call and honest motives, and not unnecessarily or without just reason. 5. And the manner and circumstances must be lawful.

An oath is an equivocal word, taken sometimes for that which is formally so, as before described; and sometimes for that which is but the matter and expressive form without any real intent of swearing. Or, an oath is taken either for the whole human act completely, containing the words signifying and the purpose signified; or else for the outward sign or words alone. (As the word prayer signifies sometimes the bare form of words, and sometimes the words and desire signified by them. And as the word sacrament is sometimes taken for the external signs only, and sometimes for the signs with the mutual covenanting and actions signified.) Here it may be questioned:

*Question.* Whether it is swearing or not, which is frequently used by ignorant, careless people, who use the words or form of an oath, in mere custom, not knowing what an oath is, nor having any thought or purpose of appealing to God, or to the creature by which they swear. The reason of the doubt is, because it seems to be but the matter or external part of an oath; and it is the form that specifies and names it. He that should ignorantly speak the words of an oath in Latin or Greek, while he does not understand the language and intends no such thing, does not swear.

*In the margin: How far the intent of the swearer (as of the baptizer or baptized to baptism) is necessary to the being of an oath.*

*Answer.* 1. In the full and most proper sense of the word, it is before God no oath if there is no intent of confirming your speech by an appeal to God, or to that which you swear by. As a mock washing and using the words of baptism is no true baptism, no more than a corpse is a man. (And thus it is true which the papists say, that the intention of the baptizer is necessary to the being of baptism; that is, it is necessary to the being of sacramental administration to the baptizer himself, before God, that he really intend to baptize; and it is necessary to the being of baptism before God in the person baptized, that he himself if of age, or those that have power to dedicate him to God if he is an infant, do really intend it; and it is necessary to the being of the external ordinance *in foro ecclesiæ*, before the church, that both the baptizer and baptized do profess or seem to intend it.) 2. But if you use such words as are the ordinary form of an oath in a language which you understand, so that the hearers may justly suppose you to understand it, it is an oath, *coram hominibus*, before men, and in the latter narrower sense of the word. And it shall be binding and able to be pleaded against you in any court of justice by those you swear to; indeed, and God himself does take you thereby to be obliged thus to men: and if it is a profane, causeless swearing, men must call it an oath; for they do not see the heart; even as they must take him to be baptized that professes to intend it; and *in foro humano*, it is so indeed: and God himself will account you a sinner, even one that uses the external form of an oath, and that which before men is an oath, to the wrong of his name and honor, and to the scandal of others. And it will not excuse you that you did not know that it was an oath, or that you did not know the nature of an oath, or that you rashly used it, not considering that it was an oath; for you were bound to have known and to have considered; you should have done it, and might have done it if you would. But if they were words which you could not know to have been the form or expressions of an oath, but the hearers might perceive that you meant no such thing, but something else, then you are excusable, if you had just cause to use them.

*In the margin: How far swearing by creatures is a sin.*

II. As to the case of swearing by creatures, how far it is sinful; it is just like the case of worshipping images, or by images. He that worships an image or any creature as God, and ultimately terminates his worship in it, does commit direct and full idolatry; [491] which is so much the greater sin, by how much the baser the thing is which he idolizes. But if he makes the image or creature but his medium of that worship which should be immediately offered to God, in whom it is ultimately terminated, then it is not gross idolatry, but it is false and forbidden worship of the true God. But if the creature is made but the medium of that worship which God would have offered him by a medium, then it is lawful so to use or worship it (as to honor and admire God as appearing in his works; to give that worship or honor to our parents and rulers as his officers, which is ultimately terminated in God). Just so is it in the case of swearing; for swearing is a part of the worship of God. He that swears by any creature as a god, or as the avenger of those that by falsehood elude the judgment of man, does commit idolatry in it; [492] as Julian did when he swore by the sun (which he praised by his orations and worshipped as God). But he that only swears so by a creature, as to intend God ultimately as the witness and avenger, but yet so as that the creature only is named, or so named as has an appearance of idolatry, or tends to entice the mind from God, or scandalously to obscure his honor, or in any other forbidden way, does swear by the true God intentionally, but in a sinful manner. But he that directly swears by God (upon a just call), and by the creature (or names the creature rather) but in a just, and clear, and inoffensive subordination to God, is excusable. So we are accustomed to lay our hands on the Bible and thus to swear, So help me God, and the contents of this book. Thus on great occasions many good men in their writings to clear themselves from some slander have said, I call God, and angels, and men to witness. Many in naming creatures intend rather a curse than a swearing by the creature: as, If it is not so, let God destroy me by this fire, or this water, etc.

*Question.* Is it lawful to lay hands on the book and kiss it in swearing, as is done in England?

*Answer.* To take an oath as imposed in England with laying the hand on the Bible and kissing it is not unlawful.

*Proved.* 1. That which is not forbidden by God is lawful (before God). But so to take an oath is not forbidden by God. Therefore, etc. The minor will be sufficiently proved by disproving all the claims of a prohibition. The major needs no proof.

2. If it is forbidden it is either, 1. As an act in worship not commanded, and so will-worship. 2. Or as a significant ceremony in worship not commanded. 3. Or as an uncommanded significant ceremony which has in itself some forbidden matter or manner. But it is not forbidden in any of these respects; therefore not at all.

I. Not as an act not commanded in worship; for *a quatenus ad omne valet consequentia*, then all acts in worship not commanded would be unlawful, which is false: for, 1. The acts used in swearing (Genesis 24:2; 14:22; Revelation 10:5) were not commanded and yet lawful; of which more later. 2. God has not commanded what tune to sing a psalm in, what division to make of the Bible into chapters and verses, whether to use a written or a printed Bible, what words, what method, what particular text to choose, what translation to use, with many such like.

II. Not as a significant ceremony not commanded; for then all such should be forbidden, which is not true. For, 1. Abraham's swearing by lifting up the hand (and so the angels, Revelation 10), and Abraham's servant by putting his hand under the thigh, were significant ceremonies. And he that will say they were commanded must prove it. The contrary may well be supposed by us, 1. Because no such law is made known in Scripture, and here *non apparere* and *non esse* are equal, because of the perfection of God's laws. 2. Because it is mentioned, as Paræus and other commentators note, as some accustomed rite, and so does not depend on any particular precept to Abraham alone as a prophet. 3. Because it is not one but several sorts of swearing rites that are mentioned, lifting up the hand, and putting it under the thigh.

2. Almost all Christians take some uncommanded significant ceremony in swearing to be lawful. The ceremony mentioned by Paræus, in the same place, as used in the Palatinate, is such, of lifting up three fingers: *Hodie nos juramus, digitis tribus dextræ sublatis, invocantes vindicem S. Trinitatem*. The English annotations tell you that the customs of countries are very various in this point, yet most agree in adding some outward attestation of action or gesture to words in taking of an oath, to make it better remembered and more regarded than bare words of affirmation, promise, or imprecation. And Josephus (cited by Grotius) tells us it was then the custom among the Jews to swear by this ceremony of putting the hand under the thigh (whether in token of subjection, or because it was the place of the sword, the instrument of revenge, as Grotius and others say, or in expectation of the promised seed, as the fathers thought). And the case of Joseph's adjuration shows it. See Pererius on Genesis 14 and 24.

3. An action of another part of the body is no more forbidden to express the mind by than of the tongue. God never said, you shall in no way express your minds in things sacred or civil but by the tongue. A change of the countenance may express it; a frown, or a pleasant look. (*Index animi vultus.*) Paul did lift up the hand to the Jews when he would speak for himself; Christ made as if he would have gone further (Luke 24). Words are not natural signs, but invented and arbitrary in particulars, though the power of speaking words so invented and learned is natural. If it is lawful to use significant words not commanded in worship, it is lawful to use significant actions (under due regulation). Therefore all the ancient churches, without one contradictor that ever I read of, did use many such. Though Augustine, in his Epistle to Januarius, sadly complains that then they were grown to an oppressive number; yet he never speaks against the thing itself. To stand up at the creed is a significant expression of consent, which not only all the churches else, but the old nonconformists never scrupled at, nor do the present as far as I can learn: whether to sit, stand, or kneel at singing psalms is left at liberty. To take off the hat is a significant ceremony or act in worship, not commanded in itself, nor used of old for the same signification as now. And where the covering of the head does signify reverence, it is better than to be bare. In one country custom makes standing up, in another sitting and hanging down the head, in another kneeling, in another prostration, to be the sign of reverence, which accordingly may be used in God's service. When covenants between God and the people are renewed, consent may lawfully be expressed either by standing up or by holding up the hand (by which votes in things sacred used to be given), or by subscribing, or by voice. For God has commanded us the expressing of consent, reverence, etc., but left the word, gesture, or expressing sign to liberty. He that affirms that God has left no other signification of our minds in sacred things to our liberty, but tied us to words alone, must prove what he says (which he must do against Scripture, against nature, and against all the judgment and custom of all Christ's churches and of the world).

III. If laying the hand on the book and kissing it is unlawful for any special matter or manner forbidden more than other significant acts, it is for some of the reasons named by you: which now I will answer.

I. *Objection.* It smacks of the Romish superstition. *Answer.* 1. Not at all; prove that if you can. 2. Superstition is the feigning of things to be pleasing or displeasing to God which are not, and using or disusing them accordingly; whatever is the etymology of the word, *Superstitum cultus*, or *supra statutum*, etc., it is certain that the common use of it among heathens (as Plutarch at large) and Christians was for an erroneous, undue fear of God, thinking this or that was displeasing or pleasing to him, to be done or to be avoided, which was not so, but was the notion of a frightened, mistaken mind. Therefore to say that God is displeased with this signification of the mind, when it is not so, nor can be proved, is superstition. And this is not the solitary instance of Satan's introducing superstition under pretense of avoiding superstition. 3. The sense of the law is to be judged by the law, and by the well-known doctrine and profession of the lawmakers and of the land; which here renounces the superstitious use of it. But I confess I was more afraid that the papists had taken too much away from the Scripture than given too much to it. And they profess that they do not swear by a creature. See Pererius, in the place cited above, on Genesis 24:2.

## Translator's notes

- **Latin:** the draft English renderings below were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition; have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.
- **Title:** "Tit." is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," his word for a heading or division of a chapter. Chapter 9 ("Directions for the Government of the Tongue") has six such Titles; this file begins Title 2. The preceding Direction 15 of Title 1 ends just before this range.
- **irreverently:** Baxter's "unreverently."
- **Scripture:** no verse is quoted in this range, so no NET wording appears in the body. References are converted to modern form. Baxter's "Apoc." (the Apocalypse) is Revelation.
- **matter / form** (paragraph I and the Question): scholastic terms. The "matter" of a thing is its content or material; the "form" is what makes it the kind of thing it is. Kept. Likewise "formally so" (that which truly has the form of an oath), "specifies" (determines its kind), and "names" (Baxter's "denominateth").
- **the particular appeal is the form:** Baxter's "the peculiar appellation." "Appellation" here is the act of appealing (Latin *appellatio*), not a name; "peculiar" means distinctive or particular.
- **fearsome:** Baxter's "terrible" (inspiring terror or awe). **lacks / lacking:** his "wanteth" / "wanting."
- **"So that a legal testimony or appeal are not swearing":** Baxter's grammar (plural verb after "or") kept.
- **affirming or denying:** Baxter's "assertory or negative." **promissory** (a promise sworn to) kept.
- **mock washing:** Baxter's "ludicrous washing," meaning done in play or jest, not "absurd."
- **papists:** Baxter's word for Roman Catholics; kept as his term (and his polemic). His point is that he agrees with them, in a qualified sense, that the baptizer's intention is necessary to a true baptism.
- ***in foro ecclesiæ*:** "in the court of the church"; Baxter glosses it "before the church." ***coram hominibus*:** "before men" (Baxter's own gloss follows). ***in foro humano*:** "in the human court," that is, before human judgment.
- **binding and able to be pleaded against you:** Baxter's "obligatory and pleadable against you." **indeed:** his "yea."
- **scandal / scandalously:** in Baxter's sense, giving others occasion to stumble or sin, not only causing outrage. Kept.
- **terminates / medium** (section II): Baxter's terms. Worship "terminated" in something has that thing as its final object; a "medium" is a means or channel through which worship passes to God.
- **Julian:** probably the Roman emperor Julian, called the Apostate; the oration Baxter mentions is probably his oration in praise of the sun (Helios), but we have not confirmed it. Baxter's example kept as he gives it.
- **we are accustomed to:** Baxter's "we use to." **slander:** his "calumny." **etc.:** his "&c."
- **"So help me God, and the contents of this book":** the form of the English oath on the Bible as Baxter reports it.
- ***Answer.* (the Bible-kissing question):** Baxter's label here is "_Resp._" (Latin *responsio*, "answer"), not his usual "Answ."; rendered *Answer.* ***Proved.*** is his label "_Proved_" for the proof that follows.
- **minor / major:** the minor and major premises of the syllogism just stated ("That which is not forbidden... is lawful" is the major; "so to take an oath is not forbidden" is the minor). **claims:** Baxter's "pretences."
- **will-worship:** a King James term (Colossians 2:23) for worship people devise for themselves without God's command. Kept.
- **significant ceremony / significant actions:** "significant" means signifying, carrying a meaning, not "important." Kept throughout.
- **Numbering in the proof:** Baxter's numbering here is layered and somewhat irregular (1, 2; then I, II, III for the three ways it might be forbidden; with 1, 2, 3 under II, and a nested 1, 2, 3 inside II's first point). Kept as printed.
- ***a quatenus ad omne valet consequentia*:** a logical maxim: "the inference from 'as such' to 'every' is valid" (what holds of a thing as such holds of every instance). Baxter's point: if the act were forbidden merely as an uncommanded act in worship, then every uncommanded act in worship would be forbidden. Rendered loosely.
- **Genesis 24:2; 14:22; Revelation 10:5:** Abraham's servant swearing with his hand under Abraham's thigh; Abram raising his hand to the Lord (NET: "I raise my hand to the Lord," Genesis 14:22); the angel lifting his hand to heaven. Baxter's "Gen. xxiv. 2; xiv. 22" is converted as printed.
- **and so the angels, Revelation 10:** kept as printed ("and so the angels, Apoc. x."). Revelation 10:5 describes one angel lifting his hand; Baxter's "angels" may be a plural used loosely or a possessive ("the angel's"). Not changed.
- ***non apparere* and *non esse*:** "not appearing" and "not being." Baxter applies the legal maxim that what does not appear is treated as what does not exist: since God's laws are complete, a law not found in Scripture is taken not to exist.
- **made known:** Baxter's "notified" (made known, published).
- **Paræus; Pererius; Grotius:** commentators on Genesis that Baxter cites (Paræus probably David Pareus, the Reformed commentator at Heidelberg; Pererius probably Benedict Pereira, the Jesuit commentator on Genesis; Grotius is Hugo Grotius). We have not checked the passages cited. Baxter's citations "Paræus, ibid.," "Vid. Perer. in Gen. xiv. and xxiv.," and "Vid. Perer. ubi sup. in Gen. xxiv. 2" are rendered "Paræus, in the same place," "See Pererius on Genesis 14 and 24," and "See Pererius, in the place cited above, on Genesis 24:2."
- **the Palatinate:** a German territory (with Heidelberg its university town), probably named because Pareus taught there; we have not confirmed this.
- ***Hodie nos juramus, digitis tribus dextræ sublatis, invocantes vindicem S. Trinitatem*:** (English: Today we swear with three fingers of the right hand raised, calling on the Holy Trinity as avenger.)
- **The English annotations:** probably the *Annotations upon all the Books of the Old and New Testament* produced by a group of English divines in the 1640s and 1650s (often called the Westminster or English Annotations); not confirmed.
- **Josephus (cited by Grotius):** Baxter's report of what Josephus says, via Grotius; we have not checked it. **as Grotius and others say:** "say" added to complete the clause (Baxter: "as Grotius and others").
- **the promised seed:** the promised descendant of Abraham, read by the church fathers as Christ.
- **Joseph's adjuration:** probably Genesis 47:29, where Jacob has Joseph put his hand under his thigh and swear (NET: "put your hand under my thigh and show me kindness and faithfulness"). Not cited by Baxter.
- ***Index animi vultus*:** (English: The face is the index of the mind.) A proverbial saying.
- **Paul did lift up the hand:** probably Acts 21:40 (Paul "gestured to the people with his hand" before speaking to the Jews in Jerusalem) or Acts 26:1 ("Paul held out his hand and began his defense"). Not cited by Baxter.
- **Christ made as if he would have gone further (Luke 24):** Baxter's King James phrase, kept. Luke 24:28 in the NET: "He acted as though he wanted to go farther."
- **Augustine, Epistle to Januarius:** Baxter's "Ep. ad Januar." Augustine wrote letters to a Januarius on church observances; we have not confirmed which passage Baxter means.
- **nonconformists:** the English Puritans who did not conform to the ceremonies of the Church of England; Baxter's "old non-conformists" are those of the earlier generation. **scrupled at:** Baxter's "scrupled" (had scruples of conscience about).
- **take off:** Baxter's "put off" (the hat). **votes:** his "suffrages." **subscribing:** signing one's name.
- **Romish superstition:** Baxter's polemical word for the Roman Church, kept. **smacks of:** his "savoureth of."
- ***Superstitum cultus*, or *supra statutum*:** two proposed origins of the word "superstition": "worship of the survivors" (or of the dead who live on; rendered loosely) and "beyond what is established." Baxter sets the etymology aside.
- **Plutarch at large:** probably Plutarch's essay *On Superstition*; Baxter gives no title.
- **notion:** Baxter's "conceit." **mistaken:** his "mistaking." **pretense:** his "pretence." **well-known:** his "notorious," which then meant widely known, not infamous.
- **taken too much away from:** Baxter's "too much derogated from."
- **"the reasons named by you":** Baxter addresses an objector directly, as if answering a correspondent's listed reasons; the objections continue in the next file.
