# Chapter 1, Part 1: Direction 1

**Direction 1. If you are truly willing to be sanctified and a child of God, do not remain in a state of ignorance; but do your best to come into the light, and understand the word of God in the matters of salvation.**

If knowledge is unnecessary, why do we have understanding? [16] and in what does a man excel a beast? If any knowledge at all is necessary, certainly it must be the knowledge of the greatest and most necessary things: and nothing is so great and necessary as to obey your Maker, and to save your soul. Knowledge is to be valued according to its usefulness. If it is a matter of as great concern to know how to do your worldly business, and to trade and gather worldly wealth, and to understand the laws, and to maintain your honor, as it is to know how to be reconciled to God, to be pardoned and justified, to please your Creator, to prepare in time for death and judgment, and an endless life, then let worldly wisdom have the preeminence. But if all earthly things are dreams and shadows, and valuable only as they serve us in the way to heaven, then surely the heavenly wisdom is the best. Alas, how far is that man from being wise who is acquainted with all the fine points of the law, who is excellent in the knowledge of all the languages, sciences, and arts, and yet does not know how to live to God, to mortify the flesh, to conquer sin, to deny himself, nor to answer in judgment for his fleshly life, nor to escape damnation! As far is such a learned man from being wise as he is from being happy.

Two sorts among us live quietly in damning ignorance. First, a great many poor people, who think they may continue in it because they were bred in it; and that because they are not book-learned, therefore they need not learn how to be saved; and because their parents neglected to teach them when they were young, therefore they may neglect themselves ever after, and need not learn the things they were made for. Alas, sirs, what do you have your lives, your time, and reason for? Do you think it is only to know how to do your worldly business? Or is it to prepare for a better world? It is better that you did not know how to eat, or drink, or speak, or walk, or dress yourselves than that you do not know the will of God, and the way to your salvation. Hear what the Holy Spirit says (2 Corinthians 4:3–4): "But even if our gospel is veiled, it is veiled only to those who are perishing, among whom the god of this age has blinded the minds of those who do not believe so they would not see the light of the glorious gospel of Christ, who is the image of God." Darkness is unsafe and full of fears; the light is safe and comforting. A man in ignorance is never likely to find his way: nor can he know whether he is in the way or out of it; nor what enemy or danger he is near. It is the devil that is the prince of darkness, and his kingdom is a kingdom of darkness, and his works are works of darkness. See Ephesians 6:12; Colossians 1:13; 1 John 2:11; Luke 11:34–35. Grace turns men from darkness to light (Acts 26:18), and causes them to cast off the works of darkness (Romans 13:12); because we are the children of light and of the day, and not of darkness or of night (1 Thessalonians 5:5). They that were once darkness are light in the Lord when they are converted, and must walk as the children of the light (Ephesians 5:8). In the dark the devil and wicked men may cheat you, and do almost whatever they want with you. You will not buy your goods in the dark, nor travel, nor do your work in the dark: and will you judge the state of your souls in the dark? and do the work of your salvation in the dark? I tell you the devil could never entice so many souls to hell if he did not first put out the light, or put out their eyes. They would never so follow him by crowds to everlasting torments by daylight, and with open eyes. If men did but know well what they do when they are sinning, and where they are going in a carnal life, they would quickly stop, and go no further. All the devils in hell could never draw so many there, if men's ignorance were not the advantage of temptations.

Another sort among us that are ignorant of the things of God are pleasure-loving gentlemen, and scholars, that have so much education as to understand the words, and speak somewhat better than the ruder sort, but indeed never knew the nature, truth, and goodness of the things they speak of: [17] they are many of them as ignorant of the nature of faith, and sanctification, and the working of the Holy Spirit in planting the image of God upon the soul, and of the saints' communion with God, and the nature of a holy life, as if they had never heard or believed that there is such a thing as any of these in existence. Nicodemus is a vivid example in this case: a ruler in Israel, and a Pharisee, and yet he did not know what it was to be born again. And the pride of these fashionable gentlemen makes their ignorance much harder to be cured than other men's, because it hinders them from knowing and confessing it. If anyone would convince them of it, they say with scorn, as the Pharisees to Christ, "We are not blind too, are we?" (John 9:40). Indeed, they are ready to gloat over the children of the light, who are wise to salvation, because they differ from the loose or hypocritical opinions of these gentlemen in some matters of God's worship; of which their honors are as competent judges as the Pharisees of the doctrine of Christ, or as Nicodemus of regeneration, or as Simon Magus, or Julian, or Porphyry, of the gifts of the Holy Spirit. These honorable, miserable men will bear no contradiction or reproof: who dares be so unmannerly, disobedient, or bold as to tell them that they are out of the way to heaven, and strangers to it (not to say enemies); and to presume to stop them in the way to hell, or to hinder them from damning themselves, and as many others as they can? They think this talk of Christ, and grace, and life eternal, if it is but serious (and not like their own, in form, or levity, or scorn), is but the troublesome strictness of hypocritical, moody, crack-brained fellows: and say of the godly, as the Pharisees did, "You haven't been deceived too, have you? None of the members of the ruling council or the Pharisees have believed in him, have they? But this rabble who do not know the law are accursed!" (John 7:47–49).

Well, gentlemen or poor men, whoever you are that have no taste for the things of the Spirit (Romans 8:5–7, 13), but live in ignorance of the mysteries of salvation, let it be known to you that heavenly truth and holiness are works of light, and never prosper in the dark; and that your best understanding should be used for God and your salvation, if for anything at all. It is the devil and his deceits that fear the light. Do but understand well what you do, and then be wicked if you can; and then think lightly of Christ and holiness if you dare! O only come out of darkness into the light, and you will see that which will make you tremble to live ungodly and unconverted another day: and you will see that which will make you lament with penitent remorse your so long neglect of heaven, and wonder that you could live so far and so long beside your wits as to choose a course of emptiness and brutishness in the chains of Satan rather than the joyful liberty of the saints: and, though we must not be so uncivil as to tell you where you are, and what you are doing, you will then more uncivilly call yourselves "furiously enraged," "foolish, disobedient, misled, enslaved to various passions and desires," as one did that thought himself before as wise and good as any of you (Acts 26:11; Titus 3:3). Do not live in a sleepy state of ignorance, if ever you would have saving grace.

[16] Qui seipsum cognoverit, cognoscet in se omnia: Deum, ad cujus imaginem factus est: mundum, cujus simulachrum gerit; creaturas omnes cum quibus symbolum habet. Paul. Scaliger Thes. p. 722. (Whoever has come to know himself will know all things in himself: God, in whose image he was made; the world, whose likeness he bears; all creatures, with which he shares a common mark. Scaliger, *Thesaurus*, p. 722.)

[17] Cum quem pœnitet peccasse pene innocens est: maxima purgationum pars est voluntaria pœnitentia delictorum. Scal. Thes. p. 742. (One who repents of having sinned is almost innocent: the greatest part of purifications is voluntary repentance for offenses. Scaliger, *Thesaurus*, p. 742.) Facilius iis ignoscitur qui non perseverare sed ab errato se revocare, moliuntur; est enim humanum peccare, sed belluinum in errore perseverare. Cic. in Vat. (Those are more easily forgiven who strive not to persist but to call themselves back from error; for to sin is human, but to persist in error is beastly. Cicero, in the work against Vatinius [as Baxter cites it].) Even Aristotle could say that he that believed as he ought of the gods should think as well of himself as Alexander that commands so many men. Plutarch, de Tranquil. Anim. p. 155. Nullus suavior animo cibus est, quam cognitio veritatis. Lactant. Instit. 1. 1. c. 1. (No food is sweeter to the soul than the knowledge of truth. Lactantius, *Divine Institutes*, book 1, chapter 1.) It is a marvelous and sorrowful thing to think how ignorant some people live, even to old age, under constant and excellent teaching. Some learn neither words nor sense, but hear as if they did not hear: some learn words, and know the sense no more than if they had learned only an unknown tongue; and will repeat their creed and catechism when they do not know what it is that they say. A worthy minister of Switzerland told me that their people are very constant at their sermons, and yet most of them grossly ignorant of the things which they most frequently hear. It is almost incredible what ignorance some ministers report that they have found in some of the oldest of their hearers. Nay, when I have examined some that have professed strictness in religion above the common sort of people, I have found some ignorant of some of the fundamentals of the Christian faith. And I remember what an ancient bishop about twelve hundred years ago says, Maximus of Turin in his homilies: that when he had long preached to his people, even on an evening after one of his sermons, he heard a cry or noise among the people, and listening to what it was, they were by their outcry helping to deliver the moon, that was in labor and needed help. His words are, Quis non moleste ferat sic vos esse vestræ salutes immemores, ut etiam cœlo teste peccetis? Nam cum ante dies plerosque cum cupiditate pulsaverim, ipsa die circiter vesperam tanta vociferatio populi extitit, ut irreligiositas ejus penetraret ad cœlum. Quod cum requirerem quid sibi clamor his velit? dixerunt mihi quod laboranti lunæ vestra vociferatio subveniret; et defectum ejus suis clamoribus adjuvaret: Risi equidem et miratus sum vanitatem, quod quasi devoti Christiani Deo ferebatis auxilium. Clamabatis enim ne tacentibus vobis perderet elementum. tanquam infirmus enim et imbecillis, nisi vestris adjuvaretur vocibus, non posset luminaria defendere quæ creavit. (Who would not be vexed that you are so forgetful of your own salvation that you sin even with the heavens as witness? For when I had been pressing [this point] earnestly for many days beforehand, on that very day, around evening, such an uproar of the people arose that its irreligion reached up to heaven. When I asked what the clamor meant, they told me that your shouting was coming to the aid of the laboring moon and helping its eclipse with your cries. I laughed, to be sure, and marveled at the folly, that like devout Christians you were bringing help to God. For you were crying out lest the heavenly body be lost if you kept silent. As though he were weak and feeble, and unless he were helped by your voices, he could not defend the lights which he created. The Latin of the opening is hard to construe in places; rendered loosely.) It is cited also by Papirius Massonus in vita Hilarii Papæ, fol. 67. (in the life of Pope Hilarius, folio 67.) Therefore popery is suitable to the children of darkness, and unsuitable to the children of light, because it greatly befriends ignorance, hindering the people from the Holy Scriptures, and quieting them with the opiate of an easy implicit faith, in believing as the Roman church believes, though they do not know what it believes, or mistake, and think it believes that which it does not. Ockam. lib. de Sacram. Altar. cap. 1. cites Innocent. Extra de Sum. Trin. to prove the great benefit and efficacy of implicit faith, that it would prove an error to be no sin: "In tantum, inquit, valet fides implicita, ut dicunt aliqui, ut si aliquis eam habet, quod scilicet credit quicquid Ecclesia credit, si false opiniatur, ratione naturali motus, quia pater est vel prior filio, vel quod tres personæ sint tres res ab invicem distantes, non est hæreticus, nec peccat; dummodo hunc errorem non defendat, et hoc ipsum credit, quia credit ecclesiam sic credere, et suam opinionem fidei ecclesiæ supponit. Quia licet sic male opinetur, non tamen est illa fides sua, immo fides sua est fides Ecclesiæ." ("Implicit faith," he says, "is so powerful, as some say, that if anyone has it, that is, if he believes whatever the Church believes, and he holds a false opinion, moved by natural reason (such as that the Father is prior to the Son, or that the three persons are three things distinct from one another), he is not a heretic and does not sin, provided he does not defend this error, and he believes this very thing because he believes that the Church believes it so, and places his own opinion beneath the faith of the Church. For although he thinks wrongly in this way, that is still not his faith; rather his faith is the faith of the Church.") This implicit faith, being nothing but to believe that the church does not err, is not an implicit faith in God (to believe that all that God reveals is true), which all men have that believe in God, as rational an excuse for ignorance and error as a belief in the church of Rome? This is too short and easy a faith to be effectual to the true ends of faith. Si igitur tantæ sit efficaciæ fides implicita, ut excuset ignoranter errantem circa illa quæ in Scriptura canonica sunt expressa, multo magis excusabit ignoranter opinantem aliquid quod nec in Scriptura canonica reperitur expressum. Ockam. ibid. (If, then, implicit faith is of such efficacy that it excuses one who errs in ignorance about things that are expressly stated in canonical Scripture, much more will it excuse one who holds in ignorance some opinion that is not even found expressly stated in canonical Scripture. Ockham, same place.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). Baxter quotes the King James wording. Where the NET reads differently the sense is the same except as noted below.
- **John 9:40; John 7:47–49:** Baxter's "Are we blind also?" and "Are ye also deceived?" are rendered with the NET's question forms, which expect the answer "no."
- **Acts 26:11; Titus 3:3** (last paragraph): Baxter joins fragments of two verses into one quotation. Paul says of his old self that he was "exceedingly mad" against Christians (King James; "mad" here means furious, not insane), and the NET reads "so furiously enraged" in Acts 26:11. We kept the two fragments in Baxter's order. His phrase "deceived, serving divers lusts and pleasures" appears in the NET as "misled, enslaved to various passions and desires."
- **mortify the flesh** (paragraph 1): kept. It means to put to death the sinful desires, as in Romans 8:13.
- **fine points of the law** (paragraph 1): Baxter's "punctilios," meaning technical details.
- **pleasure-loving** (paragraph 3): Baxter's "sensual," meaning given to the pleasures of the senses. **fashionable gentlemen:** Baxter's "gallants."
- **their honors** (paragraph 3): Baxter's "their worships," an ironic title of respect for the gentlemen. We avoid "worships," which now reads as acts of religious worship.
- **gloat over:** Baxter's "insult over."
- **strictness** and **moody** (paragraph 3): Baxter's "preciseness" and "humorous." The ungodly mock the Puritans as over-strict ("precise") and as eccentric or unstable in temperament ("humorous," from the old theory of bodily humors).
- **emptiness and brutishness** (last paragraph): Baxter's "vanity and bestiality." "Bestiality" here means living like a beast, not the modern sexual sense.
- **have no taste for** (last paragraph): Baxter's "savour not."
- **Switzerland** (note 17): Baxter's "Helvetia." **popery:** kept as Baxter's term for Roman Catholicism, which he writes about in polemical language here. **Note 17's long sentence on implicit faith** is garbled in the source (it begins as a statement and ends with a question mark). Baxter's point: isn't a general faith in God an equally good excuse for ignorance as faith in the Roman church? We left the syntax as printed.
