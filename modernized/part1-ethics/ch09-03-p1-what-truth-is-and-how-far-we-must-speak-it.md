# Chapter 9: Title 3, what truth is, and how far we are bound to speak it

**Title 3. Special directions against lying and dissembling.**

*In the margin: What truth is.*

That you may know what lying is, we must first know what truth is, and what is the use of speech. Truth is to be considered, 1. As it is in the things known and spoken of. 2. As it is in the conception or knowledge of the mind. 3. As it is in the expressions of the tongue. 1. Truth in the things known is nothing but their reality; that indeed they are that which their names import, or the mind apprehends them to be: this is that which is called both physical and metaphysical truth. [497] 2. Truth in the conception or knowledge of the mind is nothing else but the agreement or conformity of the knowledge to the thing known; to conceive of it truly is to conceive of it as it is; mistake or error is contrary to this truth. 3. Truth as it is in the expressions is indeed a twofold relation. (1.) The primary relation is of our words or writings to the matter expressed. And so truth of speech is nothing but the agreeableness of our words to the things expressed; when we speak of them as they are. (2.) The secondary relation of our words is to the mind of the speaker; for the natural use of the tongue is to express the mind as well as the matter: and thus truth of speech is nothing but the agreeableness of our words to our thoughts or judgments. Truth as it is the agreement of thoughts or words to the matter may be called logical truth. And this is but the common matter of moral or ethical truth, which may be found partly in a clock, or watch, or weathercock, or a seaman's chart. The agreement of our words to our minds is the more proper or special matter of moral truth; the form of it as a moral virtue is its agreement to the law of the God of truth. And as the *terminus* enters the definition of relations, so our words have respect to the mind of the hearer or reader, as their proper *terminus*; their use being to acquaint him, 1. With the matter expressed; 2. With our minds concerning it. Therefore it is necessary to the logical truth of speech that it have an aptitude rightly to inform the hearer; and to the ethical truth, that it be intended by the speaker really to inform him, and not to deceive him. (Supposing that it is another that we speak to.)

You see then that to a moral truth all these things are necessary: 1. That it be an agreement of the words with the matter expressed (as far as we are obliged to know the matter). 2. That it be an agreement of the words with the speaker's mind or judgment. 3. That the expressions have an aptitude to inform the hearer of both the former truths. 4. That we really intend them to inform him of the truth, so far as we speak it. 5. That it be agreeable to the law of God; which is the rule of duty, and discoverer of sin.

In some speeches the truth of our words as agreeing to the matter and to the mind is all one, that is, when our own conception or judgment of a thing is all that we assert. As when we say, I think, or I believe, or I judge that such a thing is so. Here it is not at all necessary to the truth of my words that the thing be so as I think it to be, (for I do not affirm it to be so,) but that indeed I think as I say I think. But that our words and minds agree is always and inseparably necessary to all moral truth.

*In the margin: How far we are bound to speak the truth.*

We are not bound to make known all that is true, (for then no man must keep a secret,) much less to every man that asks us. Therefore we are not bound to attempt the cure of every man's error or ignorance in every matter; for we are not bound to talk at all to every man. And if I am not bound to make known the truth at all, or my mind at all, I am not bound to make known all the truth, or all that is in my mind; no, not to all those to whom I am bound to make known part of both. If I find a man in an ignorance or error which I am not bound to cure, (indeed, possibly it would be my sin to cure it; as to open the secrets of the king's counsels or armies to his enemies, etc.) I may and must so fit my speech to that man, even about those matters, as not to make him know what he should not know either of the matter or of my mind; I may either be silent, or speak obscurely, or speak words which he does not understand, (through his own imperfection,) or which I know his weakness will misunderstand; but I must speak no falsehood to him. Also there is a great difference between speaking so as not to cure the ignorance or error of the hearer, which I found him in, and so speaking as to lead him into some new error; I may do the former in many cases, in which I may not do the latter. And there is great difference between speaking such words as in the common use of men are apt to inform the hearers of the truth, though I may know that through some weakness of their own they will misunderstand them, and be deceived by them; and the speaking of words which in common use of men have another meaning than that which I use them to. By the former way, the hearer sometimes is the deceiver of himself, and not the speaker, when the speaker is not bound to reveal any more to him; but by the latter way the speaker is the deceiver. Also there is great difference to be made between my speaking to one to whom it is my duty to reveal the truth, and my speaking to a man to whom I am not bound to reveal it; indeed, from whom my duty to God, and my king or country, bind me to conceal it. By these grounds and distinctions you may know what a lie is, and may resolve the ordinary doubts that are usually raised about our speaking truth or falsehood. As,

*Question 1.* Am I bound to speak the truth to every one that asks me? *Answer.* You are not bound to speak at all in every case to every one that asks you; and he that is silent does not speak the truth.

*Question 2.* Am I bound to speak the truth to every one that I answer? *Answer.* Your answer may sometimes be such as signifies but a refusal to answer, or to reveal what is demanded of you.

*Question 3.* Am I bound to speak all the truth, whenever I speak part of it? *Answer.* No: it is God's word that must tell you when, and how much you must reveal to others [498] and if you go as far as God allows you, it does not follow that therefore you must go further. A soldier taken by the enemy may tell the truth when he is asked in things that will do no harm to his king and country; but he must conceal the rest, which would advantage the enemy against them.

*Question 4.* Is it always a sin to speak a logical falsehood; that is, to speak disagreeably to the thing which I speak of? *Answer.* Not always: for you may sometimes believe an untruth without sin. For you are to believe things according to their evidence and appearance. Therefore if the deceit is unavoidably caused by a false appearance or evidence, without any fault of yours, it is not then your fault to be mistaken. But then your expressions must signify no more certainty than you have, nor any more confidence than the evidence will warrant. When you say, such a thing is so; the meaning must be but, I am persuaded it is so; for if you say, I am certain it is so, when you are not certain, you offend.

*Question 5.* Is it always a sin to speak falsely or disagreeably to the matter, when I know it to be false? that is, Is it always a sin to speak contrary to my judgment or mind? *Answer.* Yes: for God has forbidden it, and that upon great and weighty reasons, as you shall hear shortly.

*Question 6.* Is it a sin when I do not speak a known untruth, nor contrary to my opinion, nor with a purpose to deceive? *Answer.* Yes: it is often a sin when there is none of this. For if it is your duty to know what you say, and to deliberate before you speak, and your duty to be acquainted with the truth or falsehood which you are ignorant of, and your duty to take care that you do not deceive another negligently, and yet you neglect all these duties, and by a culpable ignorance and negligence deceive both yourselves and others, then this is a sin, as well as if you knowingly deceived them.

*Question 7.* But though it is a sin, it remains doubtful whether it is a lie. *Answer.* This is but *lis de nomine*, a controversy about the name and not the thing. As long as we are agreed that it is a sin against God, and to be avoided, whether you call it a lie, or by another name, is no great matter. But I think it is to be called a lie: though I know that most definers follow Cicero, and say that a lie is a falsehood spoken with a purpose to deceive; yet I think that where the will is culpably negligent of not deceiving, an untruth so negligently uttered deserves the name of a lie.

*Question 8.* Must my words, to free them from falsehood, be always true in the proper, literal sense? *Answer.* No. Augustine's determination in this case is clear truth, *Quod figurate dicitur non est mendacium, (i. e. eo nomine)*. To speak ironically, metonymically, metaphorically, etc. is not therefore to lie. For the truth of words lying in that aptitude to express the thing and mind which is suited to the intellect of the hearers, they are true words that thus express them, whether properly or figuratively; but if the words are used figuratively, contrary to the hearers, and the common sense of them, with a purpose to deceive, then they are a lie, notwithstanding you claim a figure to verify them.

*Question 9.* Must my words be used by me in the common sense, or in the hearer's sense? *Answer.* No doubt but so far as you intend to inform the hearer, you are to speak to him in his own sense. If he has a peculiar sense of some word, differing from the common sense, and this is known to you, you must speak in his peculiar sense. But if it is in a case that you are bound to conceal from him, the question is much harder. Some think it an untruth and sinful to speak to him in words which you know he will use to his own deceit. Others think that you are not bound to fit yourselves to his infirmity, and speak in his dialect contrary to common sense; and that it is not your fault that he misunderstands you, though you foresee it, where it will not profit him to understand you, nor are you yourselves obliged to make him understand you, but the contrary: the next will explain this.

## Translator's notes

- **Title:** "Tit." is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," a heading or division. Chapter 9 ("Directions for the Government of the Tongue") is divided into six Titles; this file opens Title 3. Baxter's "Quest." and "Answ." are rendered *Question* and *Answer*; his Roman numerals (I.-IX.) are given as Arabic numbers.
- **No quoted Scripture in this range.** Note 498 is a list of references only (passages where Christ keeps silence before his judges, and Jeremiah 38:26-27, where Jeremiah tells the officials only part of his conversation with the king), converted to modern form. Baxter offers them as examples of revealing only part of the truth.
- **Truth is to be considered:** Baxter's "Truth is considerable," meaning "may be considered" or "is to be considered" (in three respects).
- ***terminus*** (kept in italics as Baxter printed it): a term of the logic of his day, the "end-term" to which a relation refers. His point: as a relation is defined partly by what it points to, so words are defined partly by the hearer they are meant to inform.
- **logical truth / moral (ethical) truth; matter / form:** Baxter's scholastic terms, kept. "Matter" and "form" are used in the Aristotelian sense (the stuff of a thing and what makes it the kind of thing it is).
- **apprehends:** kept in its older sense, "grasps" or "understands."
- **agreeableness; disagreeably:** kept, in the sense "agreement," "not in agreement with."
- **that is** (paragraph 3): Baxter's "viz." **not at all necessary:** his "no whit necessary." **I do not affirm it to be so:** his "I affirm it not to be so."
- **attempt the cure:** Baxter's "endeavour the cure."
- **indeed:** Baxter's "nay" (in the parenthesis about the king's counsels) and "yea" (before "from whom my duty to God"). **speak obscurely:** his "speak darkly."
- **meaning:** Baxter's "signification" ("have another meaning than that which I use them to"). The phrase "use them to" is left as written.
- **are usually raised:** Baxter's "are used to be raised."
- **a refusal to answer:** Baxter's "a denying to answer." **every one that I answer:** his "every one that I answer to."
- **shortly:** Baxter's "anon." **take care:** his "take heed." **explain:** his "open" ("the next will open this").
- **negligent of not deceiving:** Baxter's "neglective of not deceiving," meaning careless about avoiding deception.
- **claim a figure:** Baxter's "pretend a figure" (claim a figure of speech to make the words true).
- **No doubt but so far as:** left as written ("there is no doubt that, so far as...").
- **nor are you yourselves obliged** (Question 9): Baxter's "nor yourselves are obliged"; only the word order needed for the modern verb was changed.
- **The missing comma before "and if you go as far"** (Question 3, at note 498): the source prints the footnote marker with no punctuation before "and"; left as printed.
- **lis de nomine** (Question 7): Latin, "a dispute about the name," which Baxter himself translates.
- **Quod figurate dicitur non est mendacium, (i. e. eo nomine)** (Question 8): "What is said figuratively is not a lie (that is, by that name)." Baxter attributes the ruling to Augustine and gives no reference; we have not confirmed the source. The parenthesis "i. e. eo nomine" appears to be Baxter's own qualification ("not a lie, as such").
- **Cicero** (Question 7): Baxter's statement that most definers follow Cicero in defining a lie as a falsehood spoken with intent to deceive is kept as his; we have not checked the attribution.
- **the king's counsels** (paragraph 4) and **the soldier taken by the enemy** (Question 3): Baxter's examples, kept as written.
