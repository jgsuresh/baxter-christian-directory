# Chapter 9: Title 3, Questions 10–11, what a lie is, and Direction 1 (reasons 1–5)

*This file continues Title 3 (special directions against lying and dissembling) at Question 10; Questions 1–9 are in the previous file. It ends partway through Direction 1, after its fifth reason; the next file continues it.*

*Question 10.* Is it lawful by speech to deceive another, yes, and to intend it, supposing it is by truth? *Answer.* It is not a sin in all cases to contribute toward another man's error or mistake. [499] For, 1. There are many cases in which it is no sin in him to mistake, nor any hurt to him: therefore to contribute to that which is neither sin nor hurt is of itself no sin: yes, there are some cases in which an error (though not as such) may be a duty; as, to think charitably and well of a hypocrite, as long as he seems to be sincere. Here if by charitable reports I contribute to his mistake, it seems to be but my duty. For as he is bound to believe, so I am bound to report the best while it is probable. 2. There are many cases in which a man's ignorance or mistake may be his very great benefit; his life or property may depend on it; and I may know that if he understood such or such a thing, he would make use of it to his ruin. 3. There are many cases in which a man's innocent error is necessary to the safety of others, or of the commonwealth. 4. It is lawful in such cases to deceive such men by actions; as an enemy by military stratagems, or a traitor by signs which he will mistake. And words of truth which we foreknow he will mistake, not by our fault, but by his own, do seem to be less questionable than actions which have a proper tendency to deceive. 5. God himself has written and spoken those words which he foreknew that wicked men would mistake and deceive themselves by; and he has done those works, and gives those mercies, which he knows they will turn to a snare against themselves. And his dominion or prerogative cannot here be pleaded to excuse it, if it were unholy. And in this sense (as to permitting and occasioning) it is said, "As for the prophet, if he is made a fool by being deceived into speaking a prophetic word—I, the Lord, have made a fool of that prophet" (Ezekiel 14:9). Yet we must not think with Plato that it is lawful to lie to an enemy to deceive him. For, 1. All deceit that is against charity or justice is sinful. 2. And all deceit that is performed by a lie. As Augustine says, There are some lies which are spoken for another's safety or advantage, not in malice, but in kindness, as the midwives to Pharaoh.—These lies are not commended in themselves, but in the deceit (or charity) of them. They that thus lie will deserve (that is, be in the way) to be at last delivered from all lying. There is also a lying in jest, which does not deceive; because he that is spoken to knows it to be spoken in jest. And these two sorts are not faultless; but the fault is not great. A perfect man must not lie to save his life.—But it is lawful to silence the truth, though not to speak falsely. On the Psalms and in the Enchiridion he says, *Mihi non absurdum*, etc. It seems not absurd to me that every lie is a sin; but it is a great matter or difference, with what mind and in what matters a man lies. Some think a physician may lie to entice his patient to take a medicine to save his life: he may lawfully deceive him by hiding a medicine, and by true speeches and dark ones, which he thinks will be misunderstood; but not by falsehood.

*Question 11.* Where does the proper vice of lying lie? Is it in deceiving? or in speaking falsely? or in speaking contrary to the thoughts? *Answer.* It is the aggravation of a lie that it is an injurious deceit. But the malignity of the sin does not consist in the mere deceit of another man's intellect: for, as is said, it may be a great benefit to many men to be deceived: a patient's life may be saved by it, when his physician finds it necessary to his taking a medicine, which without deceit he will not take. And so children and weak-headed people must be dealt with. Now such a charitable deceit, as such, can be no sin. Therefore the common nature of a lie consists not, only, in the purpose of deceiving, but in the speaking falsely, contrary to the mind: else it would follow either that all deceit is sin, or that all lying or false speaking is lawful where the deceit of another is charitable or lawful: which are neither of them to be granted. Yet it is not every untruth that is a lie. Some schoolmen distinguish between *mentiri* (as being *contra mentem ire*) and *mendacium dicere*; as if to tell a lie were not always to lie, because not contrary to the mind. But then by *mendacium* they mean no more than *falsum*.

*In the margin: What a lie is.*

*In the margin: How sin is voluntary.*

I conclude then that a lie is the voluntary asserting of a falsehood. And the more it tends to the injury of another, the more it is aggravated; but it is one thing to be injurious, and another thing to be a lie. When I name a falsehood, I mean that which is apt to deceive the hearer. So that it is necessary to the being of a lie that it is deceitful, though the purpose of deceiving is found only in the more explicit sort of lies; for *falsum dicitur a fallendo*, it would not be false if it were not deceitful, or apt to deceive. For an unapt or figurative expression which has a right sense as used by the speaker and hearer is no falsehood. In one language a double negative affirms; and in another a double negative is a more vehement kind of denial; and yet neither is to be called by the others an untruth. By asserting, I mean any expression that makes the falsehood our own, as distinct from a historical narration; for it is not lying to repeat a lie, as only telling what another said. By voluntary, I mean not only that which is done knowingly, upon actual will and deliberate choice, or consent; but also that which is done *ex culpa voluntatis*, by the fault of the will, and is to be imputed to the will. [500] For it is of great necessity to observe this about every sin, that whereas we truly say that all sin is voluntary, and no further sin than voluntary; yet by voluntary, here, is not meant only that which is actually willed; but all that the will is guilty of. For it is true that Augustine says, *Ream linguam non facit nisi rea mens*, The tongue is not made guilty but by a guilty mind. But then it must be known that the mind or will is guilty of forbidden omissions as well as actions: and so it is a lie or voluntary untruth when the mind and will do not restrain the tongue from it when they ought. As, 1. When a man errs or is ignorant through willful sloth or negligence, and so speaks falsely when he thinks it true; this is a culpable falsehood, and so a lie; because he might have avoided it and did not: and this is the case of most false teachers and heretics. So, also, if a man will through passion, custom, or carelessness let his tongue run before his wits, and speak falsely for lack of considering or heeding what he says, this is a culpable untruth, and a lie, and it is voluntary; because the will should have prevented it and did not; though yet there was no purpose to deceive.

You see then that there are two degrees of lying. 1. The grossest is the speaking of a known falsehood, with a purpose to deceive. 2. The other is the speaking falsely through culpable ignorance, error, or inconsiderateness.

**Direction 1. Be well informed of the evil of the sin of lying;**

for the common cause of it is that men think that there is no great harm in it, unless someone is greatly wronged by it: but it is not forbidden by God only because it wrongs others, but it has all this evil in it.

1. Lying is the perverting of man's noble faculties, and turning them clean contrary to their natural use. God gave man a tongue to express his mind, and reveal the truth; and lying does monstrously turn it to the hindering of the mind and truth, yes, to the voicing of the contrary to both. And as it is the evil of drunkenness to be a voluntary madness or corruption of so noble a faculty as reason, so it is the fault of lying to be corrupting, perverting, and deforming both of the mind and tongue; and by confusion, a destroying of God's work and creature as to its proper use. [501]

2. Lying is the enemy and destroyer of truth: and truth is a thing divine, of unspeakable excellence and use. It is God's instrument by which he makes men wise, and good, and happy. Therefore if he should not make strict laws for the preservation of so excellent a thing as truth, he would not secure the happiness of the world. As to the securing of men's lives it is not enough to make a law that you shall not kill men without just cause (though that is all that the law intends to attain); for then every man being left to judge would think there were just cause whenever his passion or interest told him so; but the law is, You shall not kill at all without the judgment of the magistrate: so, if the law against lying did intend no more than the securing men from the injuries of error and deceit, yet would it not have been a sufficient means to have said only, You shall not injure men by lying; for then men would have judged of the injury by their own interests and passions; but much more is it needful to have a stricter law when truth itself is the thing that God intends to secure, as well as the interest of men. In the eyes of Christians, and heathens, and all mankind that have not unmanned themselves, there appears a singular beauty and excellence in truth. Aristotle could say that the nature of man is made for truth. Cicero could say that *Quod verum, simplex, sincerumque est, id naturæ hominis accommodatissimum est*: Verity and virtue were ever taken as the inseparable perfections of man. Pythagoras could say that to love truth and do good were the two things that made man likest to God, and therefore were his two most excellent gifts. Plato could say that truth was the best rhetoric and the sweetest oration. Epictetus could say that truth is a thing immortal, eternal, of all things most precious; better than friendship, as being less liable to blind affections. Iamblichus could say that as light naturally and constantly accompanies the sun, so truth accompanies God and all that follow him. Epaminondas is praised because he would not lie, no, not in jest. Pomponius Atticus was so great a hater of a lie that all his friends were desirous to trust him with their business, and use him as their counselor. He does not know what use man's understanding or his tongue were made for, that does not know the excellence of truth. [502] Let a Pilate only ask as a stranger, "What is truth?" (John 18:38), as Pharaoh asked, "Who is the Lord ...?" "For this end Christ himself came into the world, to bear witness to the truth, and everyone that is of the truth will hear him" (John 18:37). "He is the truth" (John 14:6), and "full of grace and truth" (John 1:14). "Grace and truth came about through" him (John 1:17). His Spirit is given to "guide his servants into the truth" (John 16:13), and to "sanctify them by the truth" (John 17:19), that "knowing the truth, it might make them free" (John 8:32). "The fruit of the Spirit is in all truth" (Ephesians 5:9). His ministers "cannot do anything against the truth, but only for the sake of the truth" (2 Corinthians 13:8). "Truth" is the "belt" that must be fastened "around your waist" (Ephesians 6:14). The "church" is the "support and bulwark of the truth" (1 Timothy 3:15). The faithful are those "who believe and know the truth" (1 Timothy 4:3). "Speaking the truth in love" is the way of the churches' growth and edification (Ephesians 4:15). "Repentance" is given men, "and then knowledge of the truth, ... and they will ... escape the devil's trap" (2 Timothy 2:25–26). The dullards are those that are "never able to arrive at a knowledge of the truth" (2 Timothy 3:7). "These people—who have warped minds ...—also oppose the truth" (2 Timothy 3:8). "They found no place in their hearts for the truth so as to be saved" (2 Thessalonians 2:10). All "who have not believed the truth ... will be condemned" (2 Thessalonians 2:12–13). You see what truth is in the judgment of God and all the sober world. Therefore a lie, that is contrary to truth as darkness to light, must be equally odious as truth is lovable: no wonder therefore if it is absolutely forbidden by God.

3. You may the more easily perceive this by considering that other faults of the tongue, as idle talk, swearing, and suchlike, are forbidden, not only because they are a hurt to others, but for the intrinsic evil in the thing itself: great reason therefore that it should be so in this.

4. Lying is a vice which makes us most unlike to God. For he is called the "God of truth" (Psalm 31:5; Deuteronomy 32:4). All his "ways" are "mercy and truth" (Psalm 25:10). His "judgment is in accordance with truth" (Romans 2:2). "It is impossible for God to lie" (Hebrews 6:18; Titus 1:2). His word is the "message of truth" (Psalm 119:43; Colossians 1:5; 2 Timothy 2:15; James 1:18; 2 Corinthians 6:7). And who shall "dwell in his tabernacle," but those that "speak the truth in their hearts" (Psalm 15:2). [503] The lack of conformity of the soul to God, then, being its greatest deformity, in things wherein it is made to be conformed to him, it may hence appear that lying is an odious sin. And this may the more easily appear if you consider what a case the world would be in if God could lie, and were not of undoubted truth: we should then be sure of nothing; and therefore could have no sure information by his word, no sure direction and guidance by his precepts, and no sure consolation in any of his promises. Therefore that which makes us so unlike to the true God must be odious.

5. Lying is the image or work of the devil, and liars are his children in a special sort: for Christ tells us that he "does not uphold the truth because there is no truth in him. Whenever he lies, he speaks according to his own nature because he is a liar and the father of lies" (John 8:44). The proud, the malicious, and the liars are in a special sort the children of the devil; for these three are in Scripture in a special manner made the devil's sins. [504] Therefore surely there is an intrinsic evil and odiousness in a lie. It was Satan that filled the hearts of Ananias and Sapphira to lie to the Holy Spirit (Acts 5:3). To exchange "the truth of God for a lie," and to "make" God "a liar," are therefore the most odious sins (Romans 1:25; 1 John 5:10); because it is a feigning him to be like the devil: and should we make ourselves like him then by the same vice? If you do not love the devil's sin and image, do not love a lie.

[499] Acts 23:6–9. Licitum est aliquando salva veritate, illa verba proferre, ex quibus probabiliter novimus auditores aliquid conclusores falsi. (It is sometimes lawful, with the truth kept safe, to utter those words from which we know the hearers will probably conclude something false. [500] Tolle voluntatem, nec erit discrimen in actu. (Take away the will, and there will be no distinction in the act.)

Verbis ergo uti ad fallaciam, non ad quod sunt instituta, peccatum est. Aug. Enchirid. (Words were instituted for this purpose: not that men might deceive one another by them, but that by them each might bring his thoughts to the knowledge of another. [504] 1 Kings 22:22–23, "I will ... be a lying spirit in the mouths of all his prophets." 2 Chronicles 18:21–22.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). All NET wording below was fetched from labs.bible.org for this file.
- **Title:** "Tit." is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," a heading or division. Chapter 9 ("Directions for the Government of the Tongue") is divided into six Titles; this file is in Title 3, "Special directions against lying and dissembling." Baxter's "Quest." and "Answ." are rendered *Question* and *Answer*; his Roman numerals (X., XI., I.) are given as Arabic numbers.
- **Ezekiel 14:9** (Question 10): Baxter (King James) reads "if the prophet be deceived, I the Lord have deceived that prophet." The NET reads "if he is made a fool by being deceived ... I, the Lord, have made a fool of that prophet." Baxter leans on God's being said to "deceive"; the NET keeps "deceived" for the prophet but renders God's act as "made a fool of," which softens the verb his argument uses. Baxter himself limits the sense to "permitting and occasioning." The NET's opening words "As for the prophet" replace Baxter's "And."
- **Plato** (Question 10): Baxter's statement that Plato held it lawful to lie to an enemy is kept as his; we have not checked the passage he has in mind.
- **Augustine** (Question 10): Baxter's summary of Augustine is given without quotation marks in the source and is kept as his paraphrase, modernized. The "--" marks in the source (shown here as dashes) appear to mark skipped material. "the midwives to Pharaoh" alludes to Exodus 1:15–21 (reference not in Baxter's text). "In Psal." is rendered "On the Psalms" (presumably Augustine's *Enarrations on the Psalms*; we have not confirmed which Psalm). **Mihi non absurdum, etc.** (Latin, "To me [it does] not [seem] absurd"): Baxter translates it himself in the next sentence.
- **in the deceit (or charity) of them** (Question 10): kept as printed. The sense seems to be that such lies are commended for the kind intention behind them, not for the lying itself; "deceit" may be a misprint, but we have not checked another edition.
- **deserve (that is, be in the way)** (Question 10): Baxter's own gloss, kept.
- **true speeches and dark ones** (Question 10): Baxter's "true speeches and dark"; "dark" means obscure or ambiguous. "ones" added for modern grammar.
- **benignity** became **kindness**; **commodity** (benefit) became **advantage**; **estate** became **property**; **lie upon** became **depend on**; **used** (children "must be used") became **dealt with**; **for want of** became **for lack of**; **venting** became **voicing**; **obnoxious** (exposed, liable) became **liable**; **amiable** became **lovable**; **intrinsical** became **intrinsic**; **easilier / the easier** became **more easily**; **must needs** became **must**; **disconformity** became **lack of conformity**; **for that** became **because**; **Austin** became **Augustine**; **Holy Ghost** became **Holy Spirit**; **Jamblichus** is given in the modern spelling **Iamblichus**.

- **schoolmen** (Question 11): the medieval scholastic theologians. Kept. **mentiri ... contra mentem ire ... mendacium dicere ... mendacium ... falsum:** Latin, "to lie" (*mentiri*), "to go against the mind" (*contra mentem ire*, the supposed origin of *mentiri*), "to say a lie/falsehood" (*mendacium dicere*), "a lie," and "a falsehood." The point is that some distinguish lying (speaking against one's mind) from merely saying something false.
- **consists not, only, in** (Question 11): Baxter's odd commas are kept, since removing them could commit us to one reading.

- **double negative:** Baxter's example of an idiom that is not a falsehood. He names no languages.
- **heretics; false teachers:** Baxter's judgment that most of them lie by culpable negligence is kept as his.
