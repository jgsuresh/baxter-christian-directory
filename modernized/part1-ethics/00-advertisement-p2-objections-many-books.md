# Advertisement to the Readers (part 2 of 3)

*Continues part 1, which ends with Baxter saying he expects to be assaulted with these objections.*

*Objection 1.* You have written too many books already. Who do you think has so little to do as to read them all? Is it not pride and self-conceit to think that your scribblings are worthy to be read? and that the world has need of so much of your instructions, as if there were no wise men but you? You have given offense already by your writings; you should *write less*, and *preach more*.

*Answer.* 1. I have seldom, if ever, in all my ministry, omitted one sermon for all my writings. I was not able to live in London, nor ride abroad; but through God's mercy I seldom omitted any opportunities at home.

2. And if I preach the same doctrine that I write, why should men not be as angry with me for preaching it, as for writing it? But if it is good and true, why is it not as good to preach by the press, to many thousands, and for many years after I am dead, as to preach to a parlor full for a few hours? Or why are not both as good as one?

3. I will not take the reverend objector to be ignorant that writing, and publishing the word of God by it, is preaching it, and the most public preaching; and has the example of the apostles and evangelists, as well as speaking. And one is no more restricted to them than the other: though the extraordinary features of both are peculiar to them. And do you not perceive what self-condemning contradiction it is, at the same time to cry out against those that dissuade you from preaching, or hinder you, and tell you it is needless, and you are proud to think that the world needs your preaching, and yet you yourselves to say the very same against your brethren's preaching by the press? I know an ignorant, illiterate sectarian might say, Writing is no preaching; and you are called to preach, and not to write. But I must reverence you more than to suppose you so absurd. Other men forbid you only *less public* preaching, and you reproach me for *more public* preaching: that is the difference. How hard it is to know what spirit we are of! Did you think that you had been patrons of idleness, and silencers of ministers, while you declaim so much against it? Your claim that you would have me preach more is feigned. Are you sure that you preach more often than I do? When I persuaded ministers in the past to catechize and instruct all their parishes personally, family by family, you said it was more toil than was our duty. And now you are against much writing too; and yet would be thought hard-working ministers.

And as to the number and length of my writings, it is my own labor that makes them so, and my own great trouble, that the world cannot be sufficiently instructed and edified in fewer words. But, 1. Would not all your sermons set together be as long? And why is not much and long preaching blameworthy, if long writings are? 2. Are not the works of Augustine, and Chrysostom, much longer? Who yet has reproached Aquinas or Suarez, Calvin or Zanchy, etc. for the number and greatness of the volumes they have written? Why do you contradict yourselves by coveting great libraries? 3. When did I ever persuade any one of you to buy or read any book of mine? What harm will they do those that let them alone? Or what harm can it do you for other men to read them? Let them be to you as if they had never been written; and it will be nothing to you how many they are. And if all others do not take you for their tutors, to choose for them the books that they must read, that is not my doing, but their own. If they err in taking themselves to be fitter judges than you of what tends most to their own edification, why do you not teach them better? 4. Either it is God's truth, or error, which I write. If error, why does no one of you show so much charity as by word or writing to instruct me better, nor prove it to my face, but do all to others by backbiting? If truth, what harm will it do? If men had not leisure to read our writings, the booksellers would silence us, and save you the labor; for none would print them. 5. But who can please all men? While a few of you cry out about too much, what if twenty or a hundred for one are yet for more? How shall I know whether you or they are the wiser and the better men?

Readers, you see on what terms we must do the work of God. Our slothful flesh is reluctant, and weary of so much labor: malignant enemies of piety are against it all. Some slothful brethren think it necessary to cloak their fleshly ease by vilifying the diligence of others. Many sects whom we oppose think it the interest of their cause (which they call God's cause) to make all that is said against them seem empty, contemptible, and odious; which because they cannot do by refutation, they will do by backbiting and confident chat. And one or two reverend brethren have, by the wisdom described exactly (James 3:15–16), arrived at the liberty of backbiting and magisterially sentencing the works of others (which they confess they never read), that their reputation of being most learned, orthodox, worthy divines may keep the chair at easier rates than the wasting of their flesh in unwearied labors to know the truth, and communicate it to the world. And some are angry, who are eager to write, that the booksellers and readers do not silence others as well as them.

*Objection 2.* Your writings, differing from the common judgment, have already caused offense to the godly.

*Answer.* 1. To the godly that were of a contrary opinion only. Sores that will not be healed are usually aggravated by the medicine. 2. It was none but healing, peacemaking writings that have caused that offense. 3. Have not those dissenters' writings more offended the godly that were against them? They have but one trick, to honor their denial, which more dishonors it, even by unsanctifying those that are not of their minds. 4. If God blesses me with opportunity and help, I will offend such men much more, by attempting, further than ever I have done, the quenching of that fire which they are still blowing up; and exposing the folly and mischief of those logomachies by which they militate against love and concord, and inflame and tear the church of God. And let them know that I am about it. But some pastors, as well as people, have the weakness to think that all our preaching and writings must be brought under their dominion, and to their bar, by the bare saying that we offend the godly, that is, those of their opinion, which they falsely call by the name of scandal. 5. But I think they will find little controversy to offend them in this book.

*Objection 3.* You should take more leisure, and take other men's judgment of your writings before you thrust them out so hastily.

*Answer.* 1. I have but a little while to live, and therefore must work while it is day. Time will not stay. 2. I do show them to those that I take to be most judicious, and never refused any man's criticism; but it is not many that have leisure to do me so great a kindness. But that I do not commit them to the perusal of every objector is a fault incurable by one that never had a secretary, and has but one copy, usually. 3. And if I could do it, how should I be sure that they would not differ as much among themselves as they do from me? And my writings would be like the picture which the great painter exposed to the criticism of every passer-by, and made it ridiculous to all, when he altered all that every one advised him to alter. And, to tell you the truth, I was never yet blamed by one side as not sufficiently pleasing them; but I was blamed also by the contrary side for coming so near them: and I had not wit enough to know which party of the accusers was the wiser. And therefore I am resolved to study to please God and conscience, and to take man-pleasing, when inconsistent, for an impossible and unprofitable work; and to cease from man whose breath is in his nostrils, whose thoughts all perish as he passes off the tribunal of his stage to the tribunal of God.

## Translator's notes

- **Range:** this file covers Objections 1, 2, and 3 with their answers.
- **Unmarked allusions in the Answer to Objection 3** (kept in Baxter's wording): "cease from man whose breath is in his nostrils" is the King James of Isaiah 2:22 (NET: "Stop trusting in human beings, whose life's breath is in their nostrils"); "whose thoughts all perish" recalls Psalm 146:4. **tribunal:** Baxter's "judicature" (a court or seat of judgment); the "stage" is the stage of this life.
- **Scripture translation:** quoted Scripture in this project is from the NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). No verse is quoted in this section. There is no Latin or Greek in this range.
- **James 3:15–16** (cited, not quoted): Baxter's "the wisdom described exactly, James iii. 15, 16" points to the "wisdom" that is not from above. The NET reads: "Such wisdom does not come from above but is earthly, natural, demonic. For where there is jealousy and selfishness, there is disorder and every evil practice." We put Baxter's reference in parentheses, which drops nothing but the comma.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording):
  - "How hard it is to know what spirit we are of!" echoes the King James of Luke 9:55, "Ye know not what manner of spirit ye are of." The NET does not have these words in the verse (it reads only "But Jesus turned and rebuked them,"), because it follows manuscripts without them. Baxter's allusion therefore has no counterpart in the NET text.
  - "must work while it is day" echoes John 9:4; the NET reads "We must perform the deeds of the one who sent me as long as it is daytime. Night is coming when no one can work."
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): self-conceitedness → self-conceit; offence → offense; parlour → parlor; "appropriate to them" → "restricted to them" (in 1673 "appropriate" meant belonging exclusively to someone); "the extraordinaries of both be proper to them" → "the extraordinary features of both are peculiar to them" (Baxter means the miraculous or inspired side of apostolic preaching and writing belonged only to the apostles); sectary → sectarian (a member of a sect; his polemic word, kept in force); "forbid you but" → "forbid you only"; pretence → claim; ofter → more often; heretofore → in the past; catechise → catechize; laborious → hard-working (he means diligent); blamable → blameworthy; affecting (great libraries) → coveting (setting one's heart on; "desiring" would also do); evince → prove; "cry out of" → "cry out about" (complain of); backward → reluctant; vain → empty; confutation → refutation; forward (to write) → eager; "use to be exasperated" → "are usually aggravated"; pacificatory → peacemaking; endeavouring → attempting; detecting → exposing; censure → criticism (in 1673 it meant judgment or assessment, not necessarily blame); uncurable → incurable; amanuensis → secretary (a person who writes from dictation or makes copies); passenger → passer-by; "You shall take" → "You should take."
- **Added words** (needed for modern grammar): "to" in "as good to preach by the press" (the source reads "as good preach," probably a dropped word); "of" in "fitter judges than you of what tends"; "I" in "therefore I am resolved" (the source reads "And therefore am resolved"). We also turned "magisterial sentencing the works" into "magisterially sentencing the works."
- **Kept words:** *brethren* (his fellow ministers); *divines* (theologians, clergy learned in divinity); *sects* (his polemic against the separating groups of his day, kept as he wrote it); *logomachies* (quarrels about words, verbal disputes); *militate against* (fight against); *unsanctifying* (declaring people unholy, denying they are true saints); *scandal* (in Baxter's technical sense: something that causes others to stumble or sin, not public disgrace); *keep the chair* (keep their seat of authority, as a teacher's chair); *at easier rates* (at lower cost); *ride abroad* (travel about, outside his home); *bar* (a court, a place of judgment); *reverence* (as a verb, to respect).
- **"live in London, nor ride abroad":** Baxter does not say why here. We have not checked his circumstances at the time and add no explanation.
- **"silencers of ministers":** probably an allusion to the ejection and silencing of nonconforming ministers in the 1660s, which included Baxter, but he does not say so here; we leave it unexplained in the text.
- **"the reverend objector" / "one or two reverend brethren":** Baxter does not name them, and we have not tried to identify them.
- **Names:** Augustine, Chrysostom, Aquinas, Suarez (probably the Jesuit Francisco Suárez), Calvin, and Zanchy (probably the Reformed theologian Girolamo Zanchi, whose name was often anglicized this way) are printed as Baxter has them. "&c." is rendered "etc."
- **when inconsistent** (Answer to Objection 3): left as written; Baxter seems to mean pleasing men when their demands contradict one another.
- **"the great painter":** Baxter alludes to a story of a painter who altered his picture at every passer-by's advice until it became ridiculous. We have not identified the painter or source he has in mind.
- **Punctuation:** Baxter's "? and that the world..." (lowercase continuation after a question mark) is kept. Commas were dropped only before clauses that complete the sense ("ignorant that writing," "show so much charity as," "is feigned," "differ as much among themselves as," "a fault incurable by one"). "How hard is it to know" was printed with an exclamation mark and is rendered "How hard it is to know."
