# Chapter 3: Grand Direction XI (part 4 of 8)

*This file begins at Direction 5 of Grand Direction 11 and runs through Direction 7; the Grand Direction's heading and Directions 1 to 4 are in the earlier files.*

**Direction 5. Regard your lack of love for God (with the turning of it to the creature) as the heart of the old man; your most comprehensive, hateful sin: and observe this as the life of all your particular sins, and hate it above all the rest.**

This is the very death and greatest deformity of the soul; the absence of God's image, and Spirit, and objectively of himself. I never loathe my heart so much as when I observe how little it loves the Lord. It seems to me that all the sins I ever committed are not so loathsome to me as this lack of love for God. And it is this that is the venom and malignity of every particular sin. I never so much hate myself as when I observe how little of God is within me, and how far my heart is estranged from him. I never do so fully approve of the justice of God, if it should condemn me, and thrust me forever from his presence, as when I observe how far I have thrust him from my heart. If there were any sin that did not proceed from a lack of love for God, I could more easily pardon it to myself, knowing that God would more easily pardon it. But not to love the God of love, the fountain of love, the happiness of souls, is a sin unfit to be pardoned to anyone until it is repented of, and partly cured; Christ will forgive it to none who keep it; and when it is incurable, it is the special sin of hell, the badge of devils and damned souls. If God will not give me a heart to love him, I wish I had never had a heart. If he will give me this, he gives me all. Happy are the poor, the despised, and the persecuted, who can but live in the love of God! O miserable emperors, kings, and lords, who are strangers to this heavenly love, and love their lusts above their Maker! Might I but live in the fervent love of God, what matter is it in what country, or what cottage, or what prison I live? If I do not live in the love of God, my country would be worse than banishment, a palace would be a prison; a crown would be a miserable comfort to one who has cast away his comfort, and is going to everlasting shame and woe. If we were but duly aware of the worth of love, and the hatefulness and malignity that is in the lack of it, it would keep us from being at ease in the daily neglect of it, and would stir us to seek it, and to rouse it up.

**Direction 6. Make use of the principle of self-love, to promote the love of God, by considering what he has done for you, and what he is, and would be to you.**

I do not mean carnal, excessive self-love, which is the chief enemy of the love of God; but I mean that rational love of happiness, and self-preservation, which God put into innocent Adam, and has planted in man's nature as necessary to his government. This natural, innocent self-love is that remaining principle in the heart of man which God himself still presupposes in all his laws and exhortations; and which he takes advantage of in his works and word, for the conversion of the wicked, and the persuading of his servants themselves to their obedience. This is the common principle in which we are agreed with all the wicked of the world, that all men should desire and seek to be happy, and choose and do what is best for themselves; or else it would be in vain for ministers to preach to them, if we were agreed in nothing, and we did not have this ground in them to cast our seed into, and to work upon. And if self-love is only informed and guided by understanding, it will compel you to love God, and tell you that nothing should be so much loved. Everyone who is a man must love himself; we will not beg him to, nor be indebted to him for this: and everyone who loves himself will love what he judges best for himself: and every wise man must know that he never had, nor can have, any good at all but what he had from God. Why do men love lust, or wealth, or honor, but because they think that these are good for them? And would they not love God, if they practically knew that he is the best of all for them, and instead of all? Unnatural, ungrateful heart! can you love yourself, and not love him who gave you yourself, and gives you all things? Nature teaches all men to love their most intimate and necessary friends: do we deserve a reward by loving those who love us, when tax collectors will do the same? (Matthew 5:46.) Are you not bound to love those who hate you, and curse, and persecute you? (verses 44-45.) What reward then is due to your unnatural ingratitude, that cannot love your chief Friend? All the friends who were ever kind to you, and did you good, were but his messengers to deliver what he sent you. And can you love the bearer, and not the Giver? He made you a man, and not a beast. He cast your lot in his visible church, and not among deluded unbelievers, or miserable heathens, who never heard, except in scorn, of the Redeemer's name. He brought you forth in a land of light, in a reformed church, where knowledge and holiness have as great an advantage as anywhere in all the world; and not among deluded, ignorant papists, where ambition must have been your governor, and pride and tyranny have given you laws, and a formal, ceremonious image of piety must have been your religion. He gave you parents who raised you in his fear, and not such as were profane and ignorant, and would have restrained and persecuted you from a holy life. He spoke to your conscience early in your childhood, and held you back from the gross abominations that you would otherwise have committed. He bore with the folly and frailties of your youth. He gave you at the right time those books, and teachers, and company, and helps, which were fittest for you; and blessed them to your further awakening and instruction, when he passed by others, and left them in their sins. He taught you to pray, and heard your prayer. He turned all your fears and groans to your spiritual good. He pardoned all your grievous sins: and since then, how much has he endured and forgiven! He gave you timely and necessary strokes, and brought you up in the school of affliction; so moderating them that they might not disable or discourage you, but only correct you, and keep you from careless confidence, self-indulgence, dullness of heart, and contempt of holy things, and might spoil all temptations to ambition, worldliness, indulgence in pleasure, and fleshly lust. By the threats of great calamities and death, he has frequently awakened you to cry to Heaven; and by as frequent and wonderful deliverances, he has answered your prayers, and encouraged you still to wait upon him. He has given you the heartfelt prayers of many hundreds of his faithful servants, and heard them for you in many a distress. He has remarkably preserved you in manifold dangers. He has not made you of the lowest of the people, whose poverty might tempt them to discontent; nor set you upon the pinnacle of worldly honor, where giddiness might have been your ruin, and where temptations to pride, and lust, and luxury, and enmity to a holy life are so violent that few escape them. He has not set you out upon a sea of cares and vexations, worldly business and burdens; but fed you with food suited to you, and given you leisure to walk with God. He has not chained you to an unprofitable profession, nor used you as those who live like their beasts, to eat, and drink, and sleep, and play, or live to live; but he has called you to the noblest and sweetest work; when that has been your business which others were glad to taste of as a recreation and a meal. He has allowed you to converse with books, and with the best and wisest men, and to spend your days in drinking in delightful knowledge: and this is not only for your pleasure, but your use; and not only for yourself, but for many others. O how many sweet and precious truths has he allowed you to feed on all the day, when others are distracted, and commonly look at them only sometimes from afar off! O how many precious hours has he granted me, in his holy assemblies, and in his honorable and most pleasant work! How often have his day, and his holy uncorrupted ordinances, and the communion of his saints, and the mention of his name and kingdom, and the pleading of his cause with sinners, and the celebrating of his praise, been my delight! O how many hundreds whom he has sent have lacked the abundant encouragement which I have had! When he has seen the disease of my despondent mind, he has not tried me by denying me success, nor allowed me, with Jonah, according to my inclination to overrun his work; but has enticed me on by continued encouragements, and strewn all the way with mercies: but his mercies to me in the souls of others have been so great that I shall secretly acknowledge them, rather than record them here, where I must have regard to those usual mercies of believers which lie in the common road to heaven. And how endless it would be to mention all! All the good that friends and enemies have done me! All the wise and gracious arrangements of his providence; in every condition, and change of life, and change of times, and in every place wherever he brought me! His every day's renewed mercies! His support under all my languishing and weakness; his plentiful supplies; his gracious helps; his daily pardons; and the glorious hopes of a blessed immortality which his Son has purchased, and his covenant and Spirit sealed to me! O the mercies that are in one Christ, one Holy Spirit, one holy Scripture, and in the blessed God himself! These I have mentioned, ungrateful heart, to shame you for your lack of love for God. And these I will leave on record, to be a witness for God against your ingratitude, and to confound you with shame, if you deny your love to such a God. Every one of all these mercies, and multitudes more, will rise up against you, and shame you, before God and all the world, as a monster of unkindness, if you do not love him who has used you thus.

Here also consider what God is for your future good, as well as what he has been up to now; how all-sufficient, how powerful, merciful, and good. But of this more soon.

**Direction 7. Make use of the emptiness and vexation of the creature, and all your disappointments, and injuries, and afflictions, to promote your love for God.**

And this by a double advantage: First, by observing that there is nothing fit to divert your love, or rob God of it; unless you will love your trouble and distress! Secondly, that your love for God is the comfort by which you must be supported under the injuries and troubles which you meet with in the world; and therefore to neglect it is but to give yourself up to misery. Is it for nothing, O my soul, that God has turned the world loose against you? that devils rage against you; and wicked men do reproach and slander you, and seek your ruin; and friends prove insufficient, and like broken reeds? It would have been as easy for God to have prospered you in the world, and suited all things to your own desires, and strewn your way with the flowers of worldly comforts and delights; but he knew your proneness to undo yourself by carnal loves, and how easily your heart is enticed from your God; and therefore he has wisely and mercifully ordered it that your temptations shall not be too strong, and no creature shall appear to you in an overly lovable, tempting dress. Therefore he has allowed them to become your enemies: and will you love an enemy better than your God? what! an envious and malicious world; a world of cares, and griefs, and pains; a weary, restless, empty world? How deep and piercing are its injuries! How superficial and deceitful is its friendship! How serious are its sorrows! What trifling shows and dreams are its delights! How constant are its cares and labors! How seldom and short are its flattering smiles! Its comforts are disgraced by the certain expectation of sorrows to follow: its sorrows are heightened by the expectation of more: in the midst of its flatteries, I hear something within me saying, You must die: this is but the way to rottenness and dust: I see a winding-sheet and a grave still before me: I foresee how I must lie in pains and groans, and then become a loathsome corpse. And is this a world to be more delighted in than God? What have I left for my support and comfort, in the midst of all this emptiness and vexation, but to look to him who is the all-sufficient, sure, never-failing good? I must love him, or I have nothing to love but enmity or deceit. And is this the worst of God's design, in allowing and causing my pains and disappointments here? It is but to drive my foolish heart to himself, that I may have the solid delights and happiness of his love. O then let his blessed will be done! Come home, my soul, my wandering, tired, grieved soul! Love where your love shall not be lost: love Him who will not reject you, nor deceive you; nor repay you as the world does, with injuries and abuse: do not despair of a welcome, though the world denies it to you. The peaceable region is above. In the world you must have trouble, that in Christ you may have peace. Retire to the harbor, if you would be free from storms. God will receive you, when the world casts you off, if you heartily cast off the world for him. Oh what a comfort it is to the soul, to be driven clearly from the world to God, and there to be exercised in that sacred love, which will accompany us to the world of love!

## Translator's notes

- **Start and end of range:** the range opens at the heading of Direction 5 (source: "Direct. V.") of Grand Direction 11, and ends at the end of Direction 7. Baxter's Directions here are labelled with Roman numerals; we render them 5, 6, 7. In the source, each Direction's heading sentence is joined to its body with "--"; as in the finished files, the heading is set in bold and the body begins on its own line. No margin notes occur in this range.
- **Matthew 5:46 (Direction 6):** Baxter's "do we deserve a reward by loving those that love us, when publicans will do the like?" is his paraphrase, not a quotation, so his wording is kept (modernized). The NET reads "For if you love those who love you, what reward do you have? Even the tax collectors do the same, don't they?" The one swap is "publicans" (KJV) to "tax collectors" (NET's word); the sense is unchanged.
- **Matthew 5:44-45 (Direction 6):** Baxter's "love them that hate thee, and curse, and persecute thee" is his paraphrase (probably blending the KJV of this passage), and is kept in his wording, modernized. The NET of 5:44-45 reads "love your enemy and pray for those who persecute you, so that you may be like your Father in heaven, since he causes the sun to rise on the evil and the good, and sends rain on the righteous and the unrighteous." The NET text does not contain "hate" or "curse" in these verses, so his list of three is fuller than the NET's. Flagged. His argument (that we are bound to love even those who treat us badly) rests on "persecute," which the NET keeps. Baxter writes "ver. 44, 45" and we render it "verses 44-45."
- **John 16:33 (end of Direction 7):** "In the world thou must have trouble, that in Christ thou mayst have peace" is an unmarked allusion; Baxter's wording is kept, modernized, and no reference is put in the text. The NET of John 16:33 reads "so that in me you may have peace. In the world you have trouble and suffering, but take courage--I have conquered the world." Flagged: the NET says "you have" (a plain statement), where Baxter has "must have" (necessity), and the NET has "in me" where Baxter says "in Christ"; his lean on necessity of trouble is his own adaptation.
- **Other unmarked allusions (kept in Baxter's wording, no references added in the text):** "broken reeds" (Direction 7), probably an echo of the Old Testament image of a reed as an unreliable support (we have not confirmed which verse he has in mind); "with Jonah ... overrun his work" (Direction 6), an allusion to Jonah's flight from his commission, applied by Baxter to his own temperament. "In the common road to heaven" and "the peaceable region is above" are his own phrases.
- **Baxter's own life (Direction 6):** the passage from "O how many precious hours has he granted me" onward is Baxter speaking of himself as a minister and writer ("his honorable and most pleasant work"); we have not checked it against any biographical source and add no claims.
- **Polemic:** "deluded, ignorant papists" (and "formal, ceremonious image of piety") is Baxter's own polemic against the Roman Church, kept exactly as he wrote it. "Papist" is his word for a Roman Catholic. His "deluded unbelievers, or miserable heathens" is likewise his own description of the unevangelized.
- **Word choices (Baxter's word, then ours):** esteem to regard (heading of Direction 5); Methinks to "It seems to me"; felicity (of souls) to happiness; easilier to "more easily"; "meet" (fit to divert) to "fit"; quicken to "stir"; "stir it up" to "rouse it up"; entreat to "beg"; "beholden" to "indebted"; "amiable" (in "over amiable, tempting dress") to "overly lovable"; "improve" (make use of) to "make use of"; "vanity and vexation of the creature" to "emptiness and vexation of the creature"; "toyish" to "trifling"; "prevented" (the gross abominations) to "held back"; "seasonably" to "at the right time" / "timely"; "stripes" to "strokes"; "stupidity" to "dullness of heart"; "wantonness" to "self-indulgence"; "voluptuousness" to "indulgence in pleasure"; "strangely" (preserved) to "remarkably"; "repast" to "meal"; "sucking in" to "drinking in"; "diverted" to "distracted"; "disposals" (of providence) to "arrangements"; "entertainment" (despair not of) to "welcome"; "requite" to "repay"; "solace" to "comfort"; "harbour" to "harbor"; "honour" to "honor"; "infidels" to "unbelievers"; "more anon" to "more soon."
- **More word choices (Baxter's word, then ours):** odious to "hateful" and odiousness to "hatefulness"; sensible ("duly sensible of the worth of love") to "aware"; quiet ("being quiet in the daily neglect") to "at ease"; chiefest to "chief"; inordinate to "excessive"; unthankful to "ungrateful"; "most entire and necessary friends" to "most intimate and necessary friends"; educated (brought up) to "raised"; threatenings to "threats"; "food convenient" to "food suited to you"; wanted (lacked) to "lacked"; "suffered" (allowed) to "allowed"; "enticed", "overrun" and "strewed" kept; "Him" is capitalized as Baxter has it in Direction 7.
- **Technical terms kept:** "carnal" (carnal self-love, carnal loves); "the old man" (the unregenerate nature, from the New Testament, used here as Baxter's own term); "security" appears in Direction 6 in his special sense of careless confidence, which we render "careless confidence" in that passage ("keep thee from security" becomes "keep you from careless confidence").
- **"Objectively of himself" (Direction 5):** Baxter's scholastic phrasing: the soul lacks God's image and Spirit within it, and lacks God himself as the object it loves and enjoys. Kept as written.
- **"Government" (Direction 6):** "necessary to his government" means necessary to the way God governs human beings (by appealing to their desire for happiness). Kept.
- **Grammar:** in Direction 5 and Direction 7, Baxter moves between speaking of himself ("I"), addressing his own soul ("my soul"), and addressing the reader ("you"); we keep each as he has it. In Direction 6, the "O how many hundreds that he hath sent" sentence is Baxter speaking about other ministers (we take it so, but note it as our reading).
- **Cross-reference:** "But of this more anon" (Direction 6) points forward in the Directory to a later discussion of God's goodness; we have not checked where.
