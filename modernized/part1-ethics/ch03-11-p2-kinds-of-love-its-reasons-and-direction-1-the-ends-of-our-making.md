# Chapter 3: Grand Direction XI (part 2 of 8)

*This section begins partway through Grand Direction 11 ("Let it be most deeply engraved on your heart, that ..."), at Baxter's numbered point II of his preliminary remarks on the love of God; it carries on through points III to VI and the first Direction (Direction 1), and ends partway through that Direction's third numbered consideration. No new Grand Direction heading is given here.*

II. Though love in its own nature is always the same, and is nothing but the rational appetite for good; or the will's volition of good grasped by the understanding; the first motion of the will to good, arising from that natural inclination to good, which is the nature of the will, and the *pondus animæ*, the weight of the soul; or from healing grace which repairs the breach that was made in nature; yet love, in regard to the state of the lover and the way of its commanded acting, is differentiated like this. 1. Either the lover is in the hopeful pursuit of the thing loved, and then it is desiring, seeking love. 2. Or he is, or seems to be, denied, left without, and deprived of what he loves (in whole or in part); and then it is a mourning, lamenting love. 3. Or he enjoys what he loves, and then it is enjoying, delighting love. 1. The ordinary love which grace produces on earth is a predominance of seeking, desiring love, encouraged by some little foretastes of enjoying, delighting love, and, in a great measure, accompanied by mourning, lamenting love. 2. The state of deserted, dark, declining, relapsing, and melancholy, tempted Christians is a predominance of mourning, lamenting love, assisted with some help of seeking, desiring love; but without enjoying, delighting love. 3. The state of the glorified is the perfection of enjoying, delighting love alone. And all the rest are meant to bring us to this. [114]

III. The reasons why love to God is so great, and high, and necessary a thing, and so much esteemed above other graces, are: 1. It is the motion of the soul that tends to the end; and the end is more excellent than all the means as such. 2. The love, or will, or heart is the man; where the heart or love is, there the man is: it is the fullest surrender of the whole man to God, to love him as God, or offer him the heart. God never has his own fully until we love him. Love is the great, significant, vital motion of the soul; such as the heart, or will, or love is, such you may boldly call the man. 3. The love of God is the perfection and highest improvement of all the faculties of the soul, and the end of all other graces, to which they tend, and to which they grow up, and in which they end their operations. 4. The love of God is that spirit or life of moral excellence in all other graces in which (though not their form, yet) their acceptability consists, without which they are to God as a lifeless carcass is to us. And to prove any action sincere and acceptable to God, is to prove that it comes from a willing, loving mind, without which you can never prove it. 5. Love is the commander of the soul, and therefore God knows that if he has our hearts, he has all, for all the rest are at its command; for it is, as it were, the nature of the will, which is the commanding faculty; and its object is the ultimate end, which is the commanding object. Love sets the mind on thinking, the tongue on speaking, the hands on working, the feet on going, and every faculty obeys its command. 6. The obedience which love commands shares in its nature, and is a ready, cheerful, sweet obedience, acceptable to God, and pleasant to ourselves. 7. Love is a pure, chaste, and cleansing grace; and most powerfully casts out all creature pollution from the soul: [115] the love of God quenches all carnal, sinful love; and most effectively carries the soul up to such high delights as cause it to despise and forget the trifles which it admired before. 8. The love of God is the true acknowledging and honoring of him as good. That blessed attribute, his goodness, is denied, or despised, by those who do not love him. The light of the sun would not be valued, honored, or used by the world, if there were no eyes in the world to see it. And the goodness of God is to those who do not love him as the light is to those who have no eyes. If God had wanted his goodness to be thus unknown or neglected, he would never have made intellectual creatures. Only those who truly love him give him the glory of his goodness. 9. Love (in its attainment) is the enjoying and delighting grace: it is the very contentment and happiness of the soul: both as it makes us able to receive the most delightful communications of God's love to us; and as it is the soul's delightful union with its most lovable, happiness-giving object. 10. Love is the everlasting grace, and the work which we must be doing in heaven forever. These are the reasons for love's pre-eminence.

IV. The love of creatures has its opposites on both extremes, in the excess and in the defect; but the love of God has no opposite in excess: for Infinite Goodness cannot possibly be loved too much (unless as the passion may possibly be raised to a degree that distracts or disturbs the brain). The hateful vices opposed to the love of God are, 1. Privative; not loving him. 2. Positive; hating him. 3. Opposite; loving his creatures in his place: all these meet in every unsanctified soul. That they are all without the true love of God, and taken up with creature love, is beyond all doubt; but whether they are all haters of God, may seem more questionable. But it is as certain as the other; only the hatred of God in most does not break out into that open opposition, persecution, or blasphemy, as it does with some who are given up to desperate wickedness; nor do they think that they hate him. But the aversion of the will is the hatred of God; and if men did not have a great aversion to him, they would not forsake him, and refuse to be converted to him, despite all the arguments of love that can be used to draw them. Displeasure, nolition, and aversion are hatred.

If you think it impossible that men can hate God, whom they admit to be infinitely good, consider, for the true understanding of this hatred, 1. That it is not as good that they hate him; 2. and it is not God simply in himself considered; 3. and therefore it is not all in God; 4. and it is not the name of God; 5. but it is, 1. God as he seems unsuitable to them, and unfit for their delight and love: which seeming is caused by their carnal inclination to things of another nature, and the sinful perverting of their appetites, and the blindness and error of their minds. 2. And it is God as he is an enemy to their carnal concupiscence; whose holy nature is against their unholiness, and hates their sin, and his laws forbid them the things which they most love and take delight in: and so they hate God, as a madman hates his keeper and physician, and takes them for his enemies; and as a hungry dog hates the one who keeps him from the meat which he loves, or would take it out of his mouth. 3. And they hate God, as one who by his holiness, justice, and truth is bound to condemn them for their sin, and so (as a consequence of their sin) is their enemy who will destroy them (unless they forsake it): when their wills are enslaved to their sins, and they cannot endure to be forbidden them, and yet see that God will damn them in hellfire if they do not cast them away: this fills them with displeasure against God, as holy and just. 4. And then, as a result, they hate him in the rest of his attributes: as his omniscience, that he always sees them; his omnipresence, that he is always with them; his omnipotence, that he is irresistible and able to punish them: his very mercy as shown to others, when they must have no part in it; yes, his very immutability, eternity, and being, as he is to continue an avenger of their iniquity: so that the wicked in despair wish that there were no God; and in prosperity, they wish he were not their Governor and Judge, or were unholy and unjust, allowing them to do what they want without account or punishment. Thus God is hated by the wicked according to the measure of their wickedness, and carnal interest, and concupiscence which he is against. Where you may note, 1. that the hatred of God begins at the physical love of temporal things which he forbids; 2. that the wicked great ones of the world, and those who have the strongest concupiscence, are usually the greatest haters of God, as having the greatest opposing interest, and being most in love with the things which he prohibits and will condemn.

V. The counterfeit of love to God is something that seems like it, and yet is consistent with a powerful hatred, or the absence of true love, and makes self-deceiving hypocrites. 1. One is when so much of God is loved as men think has no opposition to their lusts and carnal interest (as his mercy and readiness to forgive); and then they think that they truly love God, though they hate his holiness and other attributes. 2. Another counterfeit is, to love God on the basis of mistakes, imagining that he is of the sinner's mind, and will put up with him and not condemn him, though he continues indulging in physical pleasure and ungodly: this is not indeed to love God, but something contrary to God. If men's imaginations will take God to be like the devil, a friend to sin, and no friend to holiness, and false in his threatenings, etc. and thus will love him; this is so far from being indeed the love of God, that it is a hateful blaspheming of him. 3. Another counterfeit is, to love God only for his temporal mercies, as because he preserves and maintains them, when yet he is resisted when he would give them spiritual things. 4. Another is, when the approval of the mind that is mere opinion, and honoring God with the lips and knee, are mistaken for true love. In a word, whatever love of God does not regard him as God indeed, and is not supreme, but is subordinate to creature love, is but a counterfeit.

VI. The directions for the exercise of the love of God are these:

**Direction 1. Consider well, that the love of our Creator, Redeemer, and Regenerator, is the very end for which we are created, redeemed, and regenerated; and how just it is that God should have the end of such excellent works, and that by neglecting or opposing the love of God, which is the end, we neglect or oppose the works of creation, redemption, and regeneration themselves.**--Let us plead these works of God with our hearts, and say,--1. O sluggish soul! do you forget the use for which you were created, and for which you were endowed with rational faculties? Do you repent that you are a man, and refuse the employment of a man? What is the means or instrument good for, but its proper end, and use, and action? God made the sun to shine, and it shines; he made the earth to support us and bear fruit, and it does so: and he made you to love him, and will you refuse and disobey? How noble and excellent is your employment in comparison with theirs! Is the fruit of the earth, or the labor of your beast, or the service of any lower creature, so sweet and honorable a work as yours, to know and love your bountiful, glorious Creator? How happy is your lot! how blessed is your portion in comparison with theirs! And do you forsake your place, and descend to more ignoble objects, as if you would rather have been some poor, foolish, sordid animal? If you would not rather be a beast than a man, why do you choose the love and pleasures of a beast, and refuse the love and pleasures of a man? Is creation, and the image of God in a rational, free soul, a thing to be thus despised for nothing? What is the sun good for, if it should yield no light or heat? And what are you good for more than the beasts that perish, if you do not know and love your Creator? If God should offer to unman you, and turn you into a horse or dog, you would think he had thrust you into misery; and yet you can voluntarily and willfully unman yourself, and take it as your ease and pleasure. If death came this night to dissolve your nature, it would not please you; and yet you can daily destroy your nature, as to its use and end, and not lament it! It would be better that I had never been a man, nor ever had a heart or love within me, if I do not use it in the holy love of my Creator. It is true, I have a body that is made to eat, and drink, and sleep; but all this is only to serve my soul in the love of him who gives me all. Life is not for food, or drink, or play; but these are for life, and life for the higher ends of life.

2. Look to your Redeemer, drowsy soul! and consider for what end he redeemed you: Was it to wander a few years about the earth, and to sleep, and amuse yourself awhile in flesh? Or was it to crucify you to the world, and raise you up to the love of God? He came down to earth from love itself, being full of love, to show the loveliness of God, and reconcile you to him, and take away the enmity, and by love to teach you the art of love. His love compelled him to offer himself as a sacrifice for sin, to make you a priest yourself to God, to offer up the sacrifice of a heart inflamed in love and praise; and will you disappoint your Redeemer, and disappoint yourself of the benefits of his love? The means is for the end; you may as well say, I would not be redeemed, as say, I would not love the Lord.

3. And think, O drowsy soul, for what you were regenerated and sanctified by the Spirit? Was it not that you might know and love the Lord? What is the Spirit of adoption that is given to believers, but a Spirit of supreme love to God? (Galatians 4:6). You could have loved emptiness, and doted on your fleshly friends and pleasures, without the Spirit of God: it was not for these, but to destroy these, and kindle a more noble, heavenly fire in your breast, that the Spirit renewed you. Examine, search, and test yourself, whether the Spirit has sanctified you or not. Do you not know that "if anyone does not have the Spirit of Christ, this person does not belong to him"? (2 Corinthians 13:5; Romans 8:9). And if Christ and his Spirit are in you, your love is dead to earthly emptiness, and quickened and raised to the most holy God. Live then in the Spirit, if you have the Spirit: to walk in the Spirit is to walk in love. Has the regenerating Spirit given you on purpose a new principle of love, and done so much to excite it, and been blowing at the coals so often, and shall your carnality or sluggishness yet extinguish it? As you would not renounce or despise your creation, your redemption, and regeneration, do not despise and neglect the love of your Creator, Redeemer, and Regenerator, which is the end of all.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[114] Nobilius et præstantius est charitatem exercere in Deo, quam virtutes propter Deum. Charitas compendiosissima ad Deum via est per quam celerrime in Deum pervenitur; nec sine charitate aliqua virtus supernaturaliter homini sapit: charitas enim forma omnium virtutum est. Per hoc charitatis exercitium, homo ad tantam sui abominationem venit, ut non solum seipsum contemnat, verum etiam se ab aliis contemni æquo animo ferat; imo etiam ab aliis contemptus gaudeat.--Thaulerus, flor. c. 7. p. 114. (English: It is nobler and more excellent to exercise love in God than to exercise the virtues for God's sake. Love is the shortest way to God, by which one most quickly arrives in God; nor does any virtue taste supernaturally to a man without some love: for love is the form of all the virtues. Through this exercise of love, a man comes to such abhorrence of himself that he not only despises himself, but even bears it with a calm mind to be despised by others; indeed, he even rejoices when despised by others. Tauler, *flor.* chap. 7, p. 114.)

[115] Austin, (Tract. 9. in John,) having showed that among men, it makes no one beautiful to love one that is beautiful, says, Anima nostra fœda est per iniquitatem: amando Deum pulchra efficitur: qualis amor qui reddat pulchrum amantem? Deus semper pater est: amavit nos fœdos, ut ex fœdis faceret pulchros: pulchri erimus amando eum qui pulcher est. Quantum in te crescit amor, tantum crescit pulchritudo; quia ipsa charitas animæ pulchritudo est. (English: Our soul is ugly through iniquity: by loving God it is made beautiful. What kind of love is it that makes the lover beautiful? God is always a father: he loved us when we were ugly, so that from ugly he might make us beautiful: we shall be beautiful by loving him who is beautiful. As love grows in you, so beauty grows; because love itself is the beauty of the soul.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®, fetched from labs.bible.org; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Where this begins:** the range opens at Baxter's point II (after a point I in the previous file), under the Grand Direction named in the hint. Points II to V are Baxter's own preliminary remarks, numbered with Roman numerals; VI introduces the Directions proper. The first Direction (Direction 1) is cut off partway, after its third consideration; the next file continues it. Baxter's "1." "2." "3." sub-points are kept as he numbered them, and points II to V keep their Roman numerals.
- **2 Corinthians 13:5; Romans 8:9 (flag):** Baxter's quoted words ("if any man have not the Spirit of Christ, the same is none of his") are the King James of Romans 8:9. They are not in 2 Corinthians 13:5, which asks the reader to examine themselves and says "Jesus Christ is in you" (NET: "Put yourselves to the test to see if you are in the faith; examine yourselves! Or do you not recognize regarding yourselves that Jesus Christ is in you--unless, indeed, you fail the test!"). We kept Baxter's two references in his order and quoted the NET of Romans 8:9 ("Now if anyone does not have the Spirit of Christ, this person does not belong to him"). The sense is the same; the "examine yourselves" thought in Baxter's sentence before the quotation matches 2 Corinthians 13:5.
- **Galatians 4:6 (flag):** Baxter cites this verse for "the Spirit of adoption." The NET of Galatians 4:6 reads "God sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, who calls 'Abba! Father!'", and has no "Spirit of adoption." That exact phrase is in Romans 8:15 (NET: "you received the Spirit of adoption, by whom we cry, 'Abba, Father'"). We kept Baxter's reference and his phrase, with no quotation marks, and note that his reference does not contain the wording. His point, that the Spirit given to believers produces a childlike love, is in both verses.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording; the verse suggestions are ours and may need checking): "the beasts that perish" (compare Psalm 49:12, 20); "life is not for meat, or drink" (compare Matthew 6:25); "to crucify thee to the world" (compare Galatians 6:14); "to make thee a priest ... to God" (compare Revelation 1:6); "to walk in the Spirit" (compare Galatians 5:16, 25).
- **Latin and technical terms kept:** *pondus animæ* (English: the weight of the soul; the idea that the will is drawn toward good as a weight is drawn downward; often traced to Augustine, but we have not checked where Baxter takes it from). "Imperate acting": his phrase for acts that the will commands (the old Latin *actus imperatus*), as opposed to the will's own first motion. "Nolition": the will's refusing or being unwilling, the opposite of volition (see the earlier file on the will for this term). "Concupiscence": strong desire for forbidden or worldly things. "Privative / Positive / Opposite" (point IV): his three kinds of vice, meaning a lack of love, active hatred, and love turned to another object. "Regenerator": his title for the Holy Spirit, as the one who regenerates.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then mine): hath, has; doth, does; saith, says; honour, honor; labour, labor; pre-eminence kept; "infinite" and "attributes" left as written; "differenced," differentiated; "obeys its command" unchanged; "partaketh of its nature," shares in its nature; "acceptableness," acceptability; "carrion," carcass; "toys," trifles; "contemn," despise; "closure," union; "felicitating," happiness-giving; "felicity" (in point III item 9, "content and felicity"), contentment and happiness; "amiable," lovable; "displacency," displeasure; "aversation," aversion; "sensual love" (point IV, note 1), physical love; "sensual and ungodly" (point V, item 2), indulging in physical pleasure and ungodly; "fantasies," imaginations; "opinionative approbation," the approval of the mind that is mere opinion; "prevalent hatred," a powerful hatred; "silly, sordid animal," poor, foolish, sordid animal; "vanity" (empty), emptiness; "sports" (in "sleep, and sport awhile"), amuse yourself; "list" (what they list), want; "bethink thyself," think; "wilfully," willfully; "&c.," etc.; "constrained," compelled; "disobey" unchanged; "nature of the will" unchanged.
- **"carnal":** kept (see the glossary). **"unman":** kept as Baxter's word for stripping a person of human nature.
- **"hate" (point IV):** Baxter's argument that the unconverted hate God, not as he is in himself, but as he seems unsuitable to them and opposed to their desires. We kept his wording, including his blunt comparisons (a madman and his keeper, a hungry dog).
- **"great ones of the world"** (point IV): left as Baxter's phrase, meaning the rich and powerful.
- **Thaulerus** (note 114): Tauler. We have not identified the work "flor." and make no claim about it; the Latin is kept as printed. "Austin" (note 115) is Augustine; the reference "Tract. 9. in John" is Baxter's, and we have not checked it.
- **Source oddities:** in note 115, "fœda" and "fœdos" are as printed. The sentence in note 115 beginning "Anima nostra ..." is Baxter's quotation, joined to his English by a comma.
- **Cross-reference:** the marker [116] appears in the source just after this range (in the next file's material) and is not included here.
