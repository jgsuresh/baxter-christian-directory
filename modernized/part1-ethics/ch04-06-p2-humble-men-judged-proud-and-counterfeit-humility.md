# Chapter 4: Why the humble are called proud (points 6-20), and counterfeits of humility (III)

*This range begins partway through Direction 1 of the Directions against Pride, and for Humility, in the middle of Baxter's numbered list of ways in which the humble are misjudged to be proud (it picks up at point 6, and the list continues past the end of the previous file). No new Direction heading is given here. The range ends at the close of Baxter's third point under this Direction, on the counterfeits of humility.*

6. If a man understands the truth in any point of divinity better than most others, and holds any truth which is there in credit, or commonly received, he shall be accounted proud, for presuming to be so singular, and seeming wiser than those that think they are wiser than he. But humility teaches us not to err for company, nor to grow no wiser when once we arrive at the common stature; nor to forsake the truth which others do not understand, nor to forbear to teach it because it is not known already. If some of the pastors in Abassia, Syria, Armenia, Russia, Greece, or Italy, or Spain, were as wise as the ministers in England are, it would be no evidence of their pride.

7. If a man that understands anything contrary to the judgment of another, cannot forsake it, and think or say as another would have him; especially if you contradict him in disputation; he will take it to be your pride, and overvaluing your own understanding, and being too tenacious of your own notions. [199] Erroneous men that in their pride are overeager to have others of their mind, will call you proud because you do not yield to their pride. They think that the evidence is so clear on their side, that if you were not proud you could not help thinking as they do.

8. Some humble men are naturally of a warm and earnest manner of talk; and their natural heat and eagerness of speech is frequently misjudged to come from pride, until fuller acquaintance with their humble lives does rectify the mistake. [200] It is written of Bishop Hooper the martyr, that "those that visited him once, condemned him of over-austerity; they that went to him twice, only suspected him of the same; those that associated with him constantly, not only acquitted him of all sullenness, but commended him for sweetness of manners: so that his ill nature consisted in other men's little acquaintance with him." Thomas Fuller's *Church History*, book 7, p. 402, and Godwin on the bishops of Gloucester. The same is true of very many worthy men. [201]

9. If we zealously contend for the faith or the peace of the church against heretical or dividing persons, and their dangerous ways, they will call us proud, though God commands it to us (Jude 3), especially if we "avoid them, and bid them not God speed" (Titus 3:10; 2 John 10).

10. When a man of understanding opens the ignorance of another, and speaks words of pity concerning him, though it is no more than truth and charity command, they will be taken to be the words of supercilious pride.

11. That plain dealing in reproof which God commands, especially to his ministers, toward high and low, great and small, and which the prophets and servants of God have used, will be misjudged as arrogance and pride (Amos 7:12-13; 2 Chronicles 25:16; Acts 23:4). As if it were pride to be true to God, and to pity souls, and seek to save them, and tell them in time of that which conscience will more closely and terribly tell them of, when it is too late.

12. Self-idolizing papists accuse their inferiors of pride, if they do but modestly exercise a judgment of discretion about the matters that their salvation is concerned in, and do not implicitly believe as they believe, and forbear to prove or try their sayings, and do not swallow all without any chewing, and offer to object the commands of God against any unlawful commands of men. [202] As if God were contented to suspend his laws, whenever man's commands contradict them; or humility required us to please and obey men at the price of the loss of our salvation. [203] They think that we should not busy ourselves to inquire into such matters, but trust them with our souls, and that the Scriptures are not for the laity to read, but they must wholly rely upon the clergy: and if a layman inquires into their doctrines or commands, they say as David's brother to him, "To whom did you entrust those few sheep in the wilderness? I am familiar with your pride and deceit!" (1 Samuel 17:28).

13. If a zealous, humble preacher of the gospel, that preaches not himself but Christ, is highly esteemed and honored for his work's sake (1 Thessalonians 5:12-13), and crowded after, and greatly followed by those that are edified by him, it is ordinary for the envious, and the enemies of godliness, to say that he is proud, and preaches to draw disciples after him, and to be admired by men; for they judge of the hearts of others by their own: as if they did not know that Christ and his most excellent servants have been crowded after without being thereby lifted up, or chargeable with pride. As the sun is not accusable for being beheld and admired by all the world; nor fire and water, earth and air, food and rest, for being valued by all. Little do they know how deep a sense of their own unworthiness is renewed in the hearts of the most applauded preachers, by the occasion of men's estimation and applause, and how much they desire that none may overvalue them, and turn their eye from the doctrine upon the person: and how often they cry out with the laborious apostle, "who is adequate for these things?" And how often they are tempted to cast off all through fear and sense of their unfitness, when the envious dullards fearlessly utter a dry talk, and think that they are wronged because they are not commended and followed as much as others. They think the common sense of all the faithful, and the love of truth, and care of their salvation, must be called pride, because it carries men to prefer the means which is fitted best to their edification and salvation.

14. If a humble Christian has, after much temptation and a holy life, attained to well-grounded persuasions of his salvation, and is thankful to God for sanctifying him, and numbering him with his little flock, when the world lies in wickedness, he will be taken for proud by ungodly men, that cannot endure to hear beforehand of the difference which the judgment of God will declare between the righteous and the wicked: as if it were pride to be happy or to be thankful.

15. If a man that is falsely accused or slandered, modestly denies the charge, and uses that lawful means which he owes to his own vindication, he will be accused of pride because he contradicts proud accusers, and does not consent to belie himself; yes, though the dishonor of religion, and the hindrance of men's salvation, is the consequence of his dishonor.

16. Many of the poor mistake their superiors to be proud, if their apparel is not in fashion and value almost like their own, though it is sober and agreeable to their rank.

17. Some are of a more rustic or careless disposition, unfit for compliment; and some are taken up with serious studies and employments, so contrary to compliment, that they have neither time nor mind for the observance of the whims of ceremonious persons, who, because they expect it, and think they are neglected, usually accuse such men of pride. [204]

18. Some are of a silent temper, and are accused of pride, because they do not speak to others as often as they expect it.

19. Some are naturally unapt to be familiar until they have much acquaintance, and are so far from impudent that they are not bold enough to speak much to strangers and take acquaintance with them, no, though it is with their inferiors; and therefore are ordinarily misjudged to be proud.

20. Some have contracted some unbecoming customs in their speech or gestures, which, to rash censurers, seem to come from pride, though it is not so. By all these seemings the humble are judged by many to be proud. [205]

III. There are also many counterfeits of humility, by which the proud are taken to be humble: as, 1. An accusing of themselves and bewailing their vileness, through mere terror of conscience, as Judas, or the constraint of affliction, as Pharaoh, or of the face of death. 2. A customary confessing of such sins in prayer, or in speech with others, which the best are used to confess, and the confessing of them is taken rather to be an honor than a disgrace. 3. A religious observance of those commandments and doctrines of men, which the apostle speaks of (Colossians 2:18-23), which have "the appearance of wisdom with their self-imposed worship and humility achieved by an unsparing treatment of the body ... they in reality result in fleshly indulgence." 4. A holding of those tenets, which doctrinally are most to man's abasement; but yet never humbled themselves at the heart. 5. A discreet restraint of boasting, and such a disparaging of themselves, as tends to procure them the reputation of modesty and humility. 6. An affected condescension and familiarity with others, even of the lower sort, which may seem humility, when the poorest have their smiles and courtesy; and yet may be but the humility of Absalom (2 Samuel 15:3-6), the fruit of pride, designed to procure the commendations of the world. 7. A choosing to converse with their inferiors, because they would bear sway, and be always the greatest themselves in the company: like Dionysius the tyrant, that when he was dethroned, turned schoolmaster, that he might domineer among the boys. 8. A constrained meanness of apparel, provisions, and deportment; when poverty forces men to speak and live as if they were humble; whereas if they had but wealth and honors, they would live as high as the proudest of them all. How quiet is the bear when he is chained up! and how little serves a dog or a fox when he can get no more! 9. An affected meanness and plainness in apparel, while pride runs out some other way. He that is odiously proud of his supposed wisdom, or learning, or holiness, or birth, or great reputation, may in his very pride be above the womanish and childish way of pride, in apparel, and such other little trifles. 10. A loathing and speaking against the pride of others, while he overlooks his own, perhaps because the pride of others clouds him; as the greedy hate others that are greedy, because they are the greatest hinderers of their gain; as dogs fight for the bone which both would have. Many more counterfeits of humility may be gathered, from what is said before of the seemings of pride, to which it is contrary.

## Baxter's notes

The Latin is reproduced exactly as printed. A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; these translations were prepared for this project and need a Latinist's check. English in the notes is modernized like the body.

[199] Siquid agere instituis, lente progredere: in eo autem quod elegeris, firmiter persiste. Bass in Laert. (English: If you set out to do anything, proceed slowly; but in what you have chosen, stand firm. Bass [as printed], in Laertius.)

[200] Pertinacior tamen erat (Chrysanthius) nec de sententia facili discedebat: inquit Eunapius humilitatem ejus laudans. (English: But he [Chrysanthius] was rather stubborn and did not easily depart from his opinion, says Eunapius, praising his humility.)

[201] Bullingero ob eruditionem non contemnendam, morumque tam sanctitatem quam suavitatem, percharus fuit. p. 591. (English: He was very dear to Bullinger on account of his learning, not to be despised, and of his character, both its holiness and its sweetness. p. 591.)

[202] Genesis 19:8-10.

[203] Cum humilitatis causa mentiris, si non eras peccator antequam mentiris, mentiendo efficies quod evitaras. Augustin. de Verb. A post. (English: When you lie for the sake of humility, if you were not a sinner before you lied, by lying you will bring about what you had avoided. Augustine, probably *On the Words of the Apostle* [our expansion of the printed "de Verb. Apost."; unconfirmed].) The printed "antequam mentiris" (indicative) is kept as is; classical usage might expect the subjunctive "mentiaris" after "antequam" (we have not checked); the translation reads it as "before you lied."

[204] Attila incessu adeo gestuosus et compositus, ut vel exinde superbissimi animi contraxerit infamiam. Callimach. Exper. de Attil. p. 341. (English: Attila was so given to gesture and so composed in his gait that from this alone he incurred the reputation of a most proud spirit. Callimachus Experiens, on Attila, p. 341.)

[205] Quod à magnatum ac procerum congressu abstinuerit (Chrysanthius) alieniorque fuerit, non arrogantiæ aut fastui tribuendum est, quin potius rusticitas quædam aut simplicitas existimari debet in eo qui quid esset potestas ignorabat; ita vulgariter, et minime dissimulanter cum illis verba factitabat. Eunapius in Chrysost. (English: That he [Chrysanthius] kept away from meetings with the great and the nobles and was rather a stranger to them is not to be attributed to arrogance or haughtiness, but should rather be thought a certain rusticity or simplicity in one who did not know what power was; so commonly, and with no disguise at all, did he exchange words with them. Eunapius, "in Chrysost." [as printed].)

## Translator's notes

- **Mid-list start:** the range begins at Baxter's point 6 of a list that began in the previous file. Point numbers 6-20 are his. The final block is his third main point ("III."), with its own numbered list of ten counterfeits.
- **Colossians 2:18-23 (important):** Baxter quotes the King James: "show of wisdom in will-worship and humility, and neglecting of the body, not in any honour to the satisfying of the flesh." The NET reads "the appearance of wisdom with their self-imposed worship and humility achieved by an unsparing treatment of the body" and then, after a dash ("a wisdom with no true value"), "they in reality result in fleshly indulgence." The NET's last clause says these practices end in fleshly indulgence; the King James clause ("not in any honour to the satisfying of the flesh") is hard to construe, and the NET gives a different sense. Baxter's use (they look like humility but are not) is served by the first half, which the NET keeps; the closing clause differs in wording and sense, so we quote it from the NET with "..." for the skipped dash phrase. Flagged.
- **1 Samuel 17:28:** Baxter's "With whom hast thou left the sheep ...? I know thy pride, and the naughtiness of thy heart." The NET reads "To whom did you entrust those few sheep in the wilderness? I am familiar with your pride and deceit!" "Naughtiness of thy heart" becomes "deceit"; the NET has no "of your heart." Baxter's point (a family member imputing pride) is untouched.
- **Jude 3; Titus 3:10; 2 John 10 (point 9):** "avoid them, and bid them not God speed" is Baxter's own blend, not a single verse. The NET of Titus 3:10 reads "Reject a divisive person after one or two warnings"; the NET of 2 John 10 reads "do not receive him into your house and do not give him any greeting." We kept his wording in his quotation marks and gave his references in modern form. Jude 3 is cited as a reference only, not quoted.
- **"Who is sufficient for these things?" (point 13):** Baxter gives no reference. It matches 2 Corinthians 2:16 (not in Baxter's text; our identification). The NET reads "who is adequate for these things?"; "sufficient" became "adequate."
- **Other references (not quoted):** Amos 7:12-13; 2 Chronicles 25:16; Acts 23:4; 1 Thessalonians 5:12-13; 2 Samuel 15:3-6 (Absalom). Footnote [202] gives Genesis 19:8-10 (Lot and the men of Sodom). Allusions without references: Judas and Pharaoh (point III.1), Dionysius the tyrant turned schoolmaster (III.7, Baxter's historical claim, kept as his and not checked).
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): conceits to notions; discourse to talk; honour(ed) to honor(ed); dishonour to dishonor; hinderance to hindrance; covetous to greedy; humours to whims (of "complimental persons"); complimental to ceremonious; toys to trifles; unhandsome to unbecoming; discommending to disparaging; morosity to sullenness (inside Fuller's quotation); repaired to to went to; conversed with to associated with (in the quotation); over eager to overeager; yea to yes; "choose but think" to "help thinking"; christian to Christian. "How little doth serve a dog" became "how little serves a dog." Verb endings modernized (openeth to opens, etc.); "if ... understand" subjunctives made indicative; "it were no evidence" to "it would be no evidence." "Do rectify" became "does rectify." "Command it us" became "commands it to us."
- **Kept as is:** *compliment* here means formal, ceremonial courtesy (points 17, III); *in credit* (point 6) means in good repute; *divinity* (theology) and *papists* (Baxter's polemical word for Roman Catholics; the polemic in point 12 is his, kept exactly); *censurers* means severe critics; *opens the ignorance* (point 10) means exposes it.
- **Place names:** "Abassia" (point 6) is kept as printed; it is probably Abyssinia (Ethiopia), but we have not confirmed Baxter's meaning.
- **Fuller and Godwin (point 8):** Baxter's "Tho. Fuller's Church Hist. lib. 7, p. 402, and Godwin in Glocest. Bishops" is modernized as "Thomas Fuller's *Church History*, book 7 ... Godwin on the bishops of Gloucester." The work of Godwin intended is not confirmed here. The marker [201] follows "very many worthy men," and the note's subject ("he was very dear to Bullinger") is not named in the printed note; it may refer to Hooper, but we have not confirmed this.
- **Footnote sources:** [199] "Bass" is as printed; it may be a misprint for Bias (the sage quoted by Diogenes Laertius), but this is a guess. [205] "Chrysost." is as printed; the sense (and [200]) points to Chrysanthius in Eunapius's *Lives of the Philosophers*, but we have not checked this. [203] "antequam mentiris" is as printed (see the note there).
- **Not checked:** all Latin translations are drafts; Baxter's historical and ecclesial claims are his own.
