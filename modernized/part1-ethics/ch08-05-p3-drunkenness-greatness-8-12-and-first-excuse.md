# Chapter 8: Part 4, Title 2, the greatness of the sin of drunkenness (8–12) and the drunkard's first excuse

*This file begins partway through Baxter's numbered list under section III of Part 4, Title 2 (the greatness of the sin of drunkenness), at item 8; items 1–7 are in the previous file, and the drunkard's excuses under section IV continue in the next file.*

8. You are the shame of human nature: [438] you represent man in the likeness of a beast, and worse; as if he were made but instead of a barrel or a drain: look on a drunkard fouling and spewing, and reeling and bawling, and see if he is not uglier than a brute! You are a shame to your own reason, when you show the world that it cannot so much as shut your mouth, nor prevail with you in so small a thing. Do not wrong reason so much as to call yourself rational; and do not wrong mankind so much as to call yourself a man: *Non homo sed amphora*, said one of Bonosus the drunken emperor when he was hanged: It is a barrel and not a man.

9. You destroy that reason which is the glory of your nature, and the natural part of the image of God upon your mind. If you should deface the king's arms or image in any public place, and set in its place the image of a dog, would it not be a traitorous contempt? how much worse is it to do so to God! If you mangled and deformed your body, it would be less in this respect; for it is not your body, but your soul, that is made after the image of God: has God given you reason for such high and excellent ends and uses, and will you dull it and drown it in obedience to your throat? Your reason is of higher value than your house, or land, or money, and yet you will not cast them away so easily! Had God made you an idiot, or mad and lunatic, your case would have been to be pitied: but to make yourself mad, and despise your manhood, deserves punishment. It is the saying of Basil: Involuntary madness deserves compassion, but voluntary madness, the sharpest whips. "Penalties have been prepared for scorners, and floggings for the backs of fools" (Proverbs 19:29); especially for the voluntary fool: he that will make himself a beast or a madman should be used by others like a beast or a madman, whether he will or not.

10. You make yourself unfit for anything that is good. Oh how unfit are you to read, or hear, or meditate on the word of God! how unfit to pray! how unfit to receive the holy sacrament! what a dreadful thing is it to think of a drunken man speaking to God in prayer! [439] Your best posture till you are sober is to be asleep: for then you do least hurt, and you are made incapable of doing good; yes, and of receiving any good from others; you are not so much as capable of reproof or counsel: he that should cast pearls before such a swine, and offer to speak to you for the good of your soul, would but dishonor the name and word of God. As it is said of a drunkard, that when one rebuked him, saying, Are you not ashamed to be thus drunken, replied, Are you not ashamed to talk to a man that is drunken? it is a shame to the man that would cure you by reason, when you have thrown away your reason. And if you have but a merry cup, and think yourself the fitter for your duty, yes, if you do it well, as to the outward appearance, as the principle is false and base, so you deserve blame for casting your work upon so great a hazard. As Sophocles said of an orator that wrote well when he was half drunk, Though he did it well, he did it ignorantly and in uncertainty; for your levity weakens your judgment, and you do the good you do but by chance; as an emotional man may speak well, but it is unlikely and uncertain; and therefore no thanks to him that it did not turn out to be worse.

You dispose yourself to almost every sin. [440] Drunkenness breaks every one of the commandments, by disposing men to break them all. It disables them for the duties of the first commandment above all, namely, to know God, and believe, and trust, and love him: it utterly unfits men for the holy worship required in the second commandment, as I have shown: he that hates the guilt of former sin in his worshippers hates present wickedness much more. "The wicked person's sacrifice is an abomination; how much more when he brings it with evil intent!" (Proverbs 21:27). Idolatry, and wantonness, and excess in eating and drinking usually dispose to one another. See 1 Corinthians 10:7. Sacrifices of mirth and joviality, and gluttony and drunkenness, are fit for idols and devils, but unfit for God! And therefore commonly we find that it is the drunkards and dissolute people in every town that are the great enemies to the preaching of the gospel, and to all holy exercises, and to all that fear God, and will not be as mad as they: when there is a sacrifice to be offered to Bacchus, and any merry meeting where potting and feasting, and dancing and roaring, is to be the game, there it is that the ministers and servants of Christ are slandered, and scorned, and railed at. [441] There it is that hellish reproach of godliness, like the devil's cannons, are let fly without control (though through God's mercy they have more powder than bullet, and do little execution). There it is that the devil sits as president in his council, plotting what to do against the people and ways of Christ. And though it is drunken, besotted counsel, it is the fitter for his business; for it is a brutish thunderbolt that he has to cast; a senseless, furious work that he has to do; and no other instruments will serve his turn. He has a plot to blow up the reputation and honor of serious godliness; but he that sets fire to his train must at the same time blow up himself: and none is so fit for this work as a drunkard or a pleasure-loving sot: few others will venture to cast their own souls into the fire of hell, that they may procure a little stinking breath to be blown into the faces of the godly; few others would set their own houses on fire, that they may trouble God's servants by the smoke. Their very work is to do as those in Daniel 3: to cast the servants of Christ into those flames which must devour those that cast them in, and must scarcely touch a thread of the garments, or a hair of the head, of those for whom it was prepared: [442] and who would do this, that knew what he did, and were well in his wits? must he not first be made drunk that does it? Also drunkenness disposes you to swearing, and blaspheming, and perjury, and speaking contemptuously and irreverently of God, and to speak profanely and jestingly of the Scripture: and thus "fools make a mock of sin" (Proverbs 14:9). You are good for none of the holy exercises of the Lord's day: that is the day that you must defile with your filthy sin; the day in which God sends abroad his gracious invitations, and the devil his wicked incitements; in which God gives most of his grace, and the devil infects most with sin; in which God is best served by his sincere ones, and the devil is most served by his impious ones. [443] And you dispose yourselves to sin against your governors: you have no hold of tongue or action when you are drunken. How many in their drunkenness have reproached and abused father and mother; and spoken treason against their king, or reviled magistrates and superiors; and perhaps attempted and done mischief as well as spoken it! If you are superiors, how unfit are you to judge or govern! Is it not lawful for any to appeal from you, as the woman did from Philip drunk to Philip sober? You will be apter to abuse your inferiors than to govern them well. Also drunkenness destroys civility, justice, and charity. It inflames the mind with anger and rage; it teaches the tongue to curse, and rail, and slander; it makes you unfaithful, and incapable of keeping any secret, and ready to betray your chief friend, as being master neither of your mind, or tongue, or actions. Drunkenness has made men commit many thousand murders; it has caused many to murder themselves, and their nearest relations; many have been drowned by falling into the water, or broken their necks with falling from their horses, or died suddenly by the suffocation of nature. It draws men to idleness, and takes them off their lawful calling: it makes a multitude of thieves, by breeding necessity, and emboldening to villainy. It is a principal cause of lust and filthiness, and the great maintainer of whoredoms; and takes away all shame, and fear, and wit, which should restrain men from this or any sin: what sin is it that a drunken man may not commit? no thanks to him that he refrains from the greatest wickedness! Cities and kingdoms have been betrayed by drunkenness; many a drunken garrison has let in the enemy. There is no confidence to be put in a drunken man; nor any mischief that he is secure from.

12. Lastly, You do not sin alone, but tempt others with you to perdition. It is the great crime of Jeroboam that he made Israel sin. The judgment of God determines those men to death that not only do wickedness, "but also approve of those who practice them" (Romans 1:32). And is not this your case? Are you not Satan's instrument to tempt others with you to waste their time, and neglect their souls, and abuse God and his creatures? Yes, some of you glory in your shame, that you have drunk down your companions, and carried it away (the honor of a sponge or a tub, which can drink up or hold liquor as well as you). And what is that man worthy of, that would thus transform himself and others into such monsters of iniquity?

IV. Next let us hear the drunkard's excuses (for even drunkenness will lay claim to reason, and men will not make themselves mad without an argument to justify it). I. Says the tippler, I take no more than does me good: you allow a man to eat as much as does him good, and why not to drink as much? No man is fitter to judge this than I, for I am sure I feel it do me good.

*Answer.* What good do you mean, man? Does it fit you for holy thoughts, or words, or deeds? Does it help you to live well, or fit you to die well? Are you sure that it tends to the health of your body? You cannot say so without the imputation of folly or self-conceitedness, when all the wise physicians in the world hold the contrary. No, it does as gluttony does; it pleases you in the drinking, but it fills your body with crudities and phlegm, and prepares for many mortal sicknesses: it makes your body like grounds after a flood, that are covered with stinking slime; or like marshy lands that are drowned in water, and bear no fruit; or like grounds that have too much rain, that are dissolved to dirt, but are unfit for use. It makes you like a leaking ship, that must be pumped and emptied, or it will sink; if you do not have vomits or purges to empty you, you will quickly drown or suffocate your life. As Basil says, a drunkard is like a ship in a tempest, when all the goods are cast overboard to unburden it lest it sink. Physicians must pump you, or unburden you, or you will be drowned; and all will not serve if you keep on filling it up again; for intemperance makes most diseases incurable. A historian speaks of two physicians that differed in their prognoses about a patient; one abandoned him as incurable; the other undertook him as certainly curable; but when he came to his remedies, he prescribed him so strict abstinence as he would not undergo: and so they agreed in the outcome; when one judged him incurable because intemperate, and the other curable if he would be temperate. You that feel the drink do you good little think how the devil has a design in it, not only to have your soul, but to have it quickly; that the mud-walls of your body being washed down may not hold it long. And I must tell you that you have cause to value a good physician for greater reasons than your life, and are more beholden to him than many others; even that he may help to keep your soul out of hell a little longer, to see if "God will grant [you] repentance," that you may "escape the devil's trap where [you are] held captive to do his will" (2 Timothy 2:25–26). As Aelian writes of king Antigonus, that having great respect for Zeno the philosopher, he once met him when he was in drink, and embracing him, urged him to ask of him what he would, and bound himself with many oaths to give it to him. Zeno thanked him, and the request he made to him was that he would go home and vomit. To tell him that he more needed to be unburdened of his drink than he himself needed his gifts. The truth is, the good that you feel the drink do you is but the present pleasing of your appetite, and tickling your imagination by the exhilarating vapors: and so the glutton, and the whoremonger, and every pleasure-loving wretch will say that he feels it do him good; but God bless all sober men from such a good. So the gambler feels the amusement do him good; but perhaps he is quickly made a beggar by it. It is reason and faith, and not your appetite or present feeling, that must tell you what and how much does you good.

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows the Latin in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[439] Of drunken priests I am loath to speak: but pray such to read Isaiah 52:12; 28:7; Micah 2:11; 1 Timothy 3:3, 8; Isaiah 56:11–12; Leviticus 10:9; Jeremiah 35; Ezekiel 44:21; Matthew 24:49; 1 Thessalonians 5:7; Galatians 5:21.

[441] Est certa et constans plurimorum sententia, frustra Indos christianam religionem doceri, quamdiu pestifera isthæc consuetudo inerti nostrorum dissimulatione retinetur (It is the sure and settled opinion of very many that the Indians are taught the Christian religion in vain as long as this pestilent custom is kept up by the sluggish connivance of our own people), says Acosta speaking of drunkenness, l. 3. c. 22. p. 336. (Book 3, chapter 22, p. 336.)

[442] Leg. Jos. Acostam de procur. Indor. salut. l. 3. c. 21, 22. (Read Joseph Acosta, *De procuranda Indorum salute* [*On Procuring the Salvation of the Indians*], book 3, chapters 21, 22.)

[443] Gluttons, and drunkards, and lustful pleasure-seekers are prepared for atheism, unbelief, and any impious notion. For their wits are buried in the dunghill of their guts, and drowned in the excremental humidity of their brains: (ubi oculus siccus clarus intellectus:) (where the eye is dry, the understanding is clear) and the vapors and fumes of their boiling lusts do so intoxicate and cloud their brains that they have little use of their reason except to contrive the service of their guts and lusts. Lege Basilii Homil. in Ebriet. et Lux. (Read Basil's homily on drunkenness and luxury.) Vide ipse ex taberna duos semi captos vino egressos, vix oboli causa, se mutuo uno eodemque gladio confecisse; et quidem extracto his e percusso corpore, præ alterum feriendi furore: itaque momento temporis ambo exanimes corruerunt. (I myself saw two men come out of a tavern half overcome with wine who, for the sake of scarcely an obol [a coin of trifling value], killed each other with one and the same sword; that is, the sword was drawn out of the body that had been struck, in the fury of striking the other; and so in a moment of time both fell down lifeless. Rendered loosely: "his" and "præ" are hard to construe as printed.) Jos. Acosta de proc. Ind. salut. l. 3. c. 21. p. 332. (Joseph Acosta, *De procuranda Indorum salute*, book 3, chapter 21, p. 332.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). All NET wording was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Labels:** this file sits in Part 4 of Chapter 8, Title 2 (against drunkenness). "Tit." in the source is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," a heading or division within a Part. The roman "IV." (the drunkard's excuses) is Baxter's section number within Title 2; the excuses themselves are numbered with roman numerals ("I." here; the following excuses are headed "Object. II." and so on in the source), and the items under section III keep his arabic numbers.
- **Missing "11."** The source numbers items 8, 9, 10, and then 12; the paragraph beginning "You dispose yourself to almost every sin" has no number, though it stands where item 11 would be. We keep it unnumbered as printed.
- **Proverbs 19:29:** NET wording. Baxter's King James has "Judgments are prepared for scorners, and stripes for the fool's back." The NET's "penalties" and "floggings" keep the sense he uses ("the sharpest whips"). Baxter's reference stood before the quotation in the source; it is moved after it, following the project's practice.
- **Proverbs 21:27:** NET wording. The King James "with a wicked mind" is "with evil intent" in the NET; the sense Baxter uses is unchanged.
- **Proverbs 14:9:** kept in Baxter's wording, because the words he quotes are not in the NET. The King James "Fools make a mock at sin" is the basis of his point that drunkards jest at sin and Scripture. The NET reads "Fools mock at reparation, but among the upright there is favor," apparently taking the Hebrew word in the sense of making amends rather than "sin," so the verse no longer says directly that fools make light of sin.
- **Romans 1:32:** NET wording for the phrase Baxter quotes. The King James "have pleasure in them that do them" becomes "approve of those who practice them" in the NET. Baxter's point (the guilt of those who encourage others' sin) still holds, but the NET's "approve" is weaker than the King James "have pleasure in," which suited his picture of drinkers who glory in drinking their companions down. The NET's "them" refers to the vices listed in the preceding verses; Baxter's "wickedness" stands in for that list.
- **2 Timothy 2:25–26:** NET wording, with "[you]" and "[you are]" put in brackets where the NET has "them" and "they are," because Baxter turns Paul's words into the second person ("if God will give thee repentance," "who taketh thee captive at his will"). The King James "the snare of the devil" is "the devil's trap" in the NET; the sense he leans on is unchanged. In the NET, verse 25 has "Perhaps God will grant them repentance"; Baxter's "if" is kept outside the quotation marks.
- **1 Corinthians 10:7; Daniel 3; Proverbs 23:29–33 (note 440):** cited, not quoted. 1 Corinthians 10:7 quotes "The people sat down to eat and drink and rose up to play" (NET), the link Baxter draws between idolatry and feasting. Daniel 3 is the fiery furnace, whose flames killed those who threw Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego in, while not a hair of their heads was singed.
- **Note 439, "Isaiah 52:12":** printed "Isa. lii. 12." That verse (NET: "Yet do not depart quickly or leave in a panic") has nothing about drink, so the reference is probably a misprint, perhaps for Isaiah 5:11–12 ("those who get up early to drink beer ... wine at their parties," NET). We have not checked another edition, so Baxter's reference is kept as printed.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "cast pearls before such a swine" (Matthew 7:6, NET "throw your pearls before pigs"); Jeroboam "made Israel sin" (1 Kings 14:16 and often in Kings); "glory in your shame" (Philippians 3:19); "the image of God" (Genesis 1:27); the first and second commandments (Exodus 20:3–6).
- **"hellish reproach ... are let fly":** the source has the singular "reproach" with the plural verb "are"; kept as printed rather than corrected.
- **Bonosus; *Non homo sed amphora*:** the Latin means "Not a man but a wine jar," which Baxter then renders "a barrel and not a man." Bonosus was a third-century Roman usurper remembered as a heavy drinker, as we understand it; Baxter calls him "the drunken emperor." We have not traced the source of the saying.
- **Anecdotes not traced:** the sayings of Basil (on voluntary madness; the ship in a tempest), Sophocles on the half-drunk orator, the woman who appealed "from Philip drunk to Philip sober" (traditionally told of Philip of Macedon), the two physicians, and Aelian's story of Antigonus and Zeno are Baxter's; we have not checked his sources.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): sink → drain; filthing → fouling (making filth); in the stead of it → in its place; thus by God → so to God; passionate → emotional (see GLOSSARY.md); at a venture → by chance; fell not out → did not turn out; viz. → namely; riotous → dissolute (here the sense is revelry and loose living); incitations → incitements; sottish → besotted; withal → at the same time; sensual → pleasure-loving; unreverently → irreverently; chiefest → chief; imboldening → emboldening; forbeareth → refrains from; pretend to reason → lay claim to reason; fenny → marshy; disburden → unburden; hold on to fill it up → keep on filling it up; prognostics → prognoses; forsook him → abandoned him; issue → outcome; fantasy → imagination; gamester → gambler; sport → amusement; infidelity → unbelief, sensualists → pleasure-seekers, conceit → notion, excrementitious → excremental (all note 443); loth → loath (note 439).
- **Words kept:** idiot (in Baxter's day a person born without the use of reason, as distinct from one who is "mad" or "lunatic"); manhood (here human nature, being a man rather than a beast); levity (light-headedness, unsteadiness of mind, not joking); potting (drinking from pots, tippling); train (a trail of gunpowder laid to fire a charge); execution (damage done by shot); sot (a habitual drunkard, a fool); wit (good sense); crudities (undigested matter in the stomach, in the medicine of the time); vomits and purges (medicines to make one vomit or empty the bowels); "the suffocation of nature" (being stifled or choked, by the physiology of the time); mud-walls (walls of dried mud, easily washed away); beholden (indebted); "in drink" (drunk); "carried it away" (carried off the prize); "God bless ... from" (God keep ... from); "nor any mischief that he is secure from" (there is no mischief he can be counted on not to do); tippler (one who drinks habitually in small amounts).
- **Bacchus:** the Roman god of wine; Baxter's "a sacrifice to be offered to Bacchus" is his figure for a drinking party.
- **Note 438:** Cicero addressed *De Officiis* to his son Marcus; the story that the son became a drunkard is Baxter's ("as it is said").
- **Notes 441–443:** Acosta is the Jesuit José de Acosta, whom Baxter cites elsewhere on the native peoples of the Americas; *De procuranda Indorum salute* is his work on missions to them. **the Indians:** as in Acosta. "Lege Basilii Homil. in Ebriet. et Lux." refers to a homily of Basil the Great against drunkenness; we have not confirmed the exact title Baxter used. *Ubi oculus siccus clarus intellectus* reads like a proverb; we have not traced it. In note 443 "Vide ipse" (literally "see for yourself") is probably a misprint for *Vidi ipse* ("I myself saw"), which the draft translation follows; the Latin is kept as printed.
