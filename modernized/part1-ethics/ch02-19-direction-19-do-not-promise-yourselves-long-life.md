# Chapter 2: Direction 19

**Direction 19. Do not promise yourselves long life, or prosperity and great matters in the world, lest it entangle your hearts with transitory things, and engage you in ambitious or greedy plans, and steal away your hearts from God, and destroy all your serious awareness of eternity.**

Our own experience, and the changes that the approach of death makes upon most people, clearly prove that the expectation of a speedy change, and reckoning on a short life, greatly helps us in all our preparation, and in all the work of holiness through our lives. Come to a man who lies on his deathbed, or a prisoner who is to die tomorrow, and try him with talk of riches, or honors, or temptations to lust, or drunkenness, or excess; and he will think you are mad, or very much out of place, to tell him of such things. If he is but a man of common reason, you will see that he will more easily despise such temptations than any religious persons will do, in their prosperity and health. Oh how serious are we in repenting and reviewing our former lives, and adding up our accounts, and asking, What we shall do to be saved, when we see that death is indeed at hand, and time is at an end, and we must go! Every sentence of Scripture then has some life and power in it; every word of exhortation is savory to us; every reproof of our negligence and sin is then well taken; every thought of sin, or Christ, or grace, or eternity, goes then to the quick. Then time seems precious; and if you ask a man whether it is better spent in cards and dice, and plays and feastings, and needless recreations and idleness, or in prayer, and holy conversation, and reading and meditating on the word of God and the life to come, and the holy use of our lawful labors; how easily will he be satisfied of the truth, and refute the quibbles of pleasure-loving time-wasters! Then his judgment will more easily be in the right than learning or arguments before could make it. [75] In a word, the expectation of the speedy approach of the soul into the presence of the eternal God, and of our entering into an unchangeable, endless life of joy or torment, has so much in it to awaken all the powers of the soul, that if ever we will be serious, it will make us serious, in every thought, and speech, and duty. And therefore, as it is a great mercy of God that this life, which is so short, should be as uncertain, and that frequent dangers and sicknesses call to us to look about us, and be ready for our change; so usually the sickly, who look for death, are most thoughtful: and it is a great part of the duty of those who are in youth and health, to consider their frailty, and the shortness and uncertainty of their lives, and always live as those who wait for the coming of their Lord. And we have great reason for it, when we are certain it will be before long; and when we have so many perils and weaknesses to warn us, and when we are never sure to see another hour; and when time is so swift, so quickly gone, so unrecoverable, and nothing when it is past. Common reason requires such to live in a constant readiness to die.

But if youth or health once make you reckon on living long, [76] and make you put away the day of your departure, as if it were far off; this will do much to deceive and dull the best, and take away the power of every truth, and the life of every good thought and duty, and all will be apt to dwindle into mere routine and form. You will hardly keep the faculties of the soul awake, if you do not still think of death and judgment as near at hand. The greatest certainty of the greatest change, and the greatest joy or misery forever, will not keep our dull hearts awake, unless we look at all as near, as well as certain. This is plain in the common difference that we find among all men, between their thoughts of death in health, and when they see indeed that they must die at once. Those who in health could think and talk of death with laughter, or lightly, without any awakening of soul, when they come to die are often as much changed as if they had never heard before that they are mortal. By which it is plain that to live in the house of mirth is more dangerous than to live in the house of mourning; and that the expectation of long life is a grievous enemy to the operations of grace, and the safety of the soul.

And it is one of the greatest strengtheners of your temptations to luxury, ambition, worldliness, and almost every sin. When men think that they will have many years' leisure to repent, they are apt the more boldly to transgress: when they think that they have yet many years to live, it tempts them to pass away time in idleness, and to loiter in their race, and trifle in all their work, and to overvalue all the pleasures, and honors, and shadows of happiness that are here below. He who has his life in his house or land, or has it for inheritance, will value it more, and bestow more upon it, than if he thought he must go out of it the next year. To a man who thinks of living many years, the favor of great ones, the raising of his estate, and name, and family, and the comforts and pleasing of his flesh, will seem great matters to him, and will do much with him, and will make self-denial a very hard work.

Therefore, though health is a wonderfully great mercy, as enabling him to duty who has a heart to use it to that end; yet it is incidentally a very great danger and snare to the heart itself, to turn it from the way of duty. The best life for the soul is that which least endangers it by being over-pleasing to the body, and in which the flesh has the smallest interest, to set up and plead against the Spirit. Not but that the largest resources must be accepted and used for God, when he trusts us with them; for when he sets us the hardest work, we may expect his greatest help. But a dwelling as in tents, in a constant unsettledness, in a movable condition, having little, and needing little, never feeling anything in the creature to tempt us to say, "Soul, take your rest;" this is to most the safest life, which gives us the freest advantages for heaven.

Take heed therefore, as you love your souls, of falling into the snare of worldly hopes, and laying plans for rising, and riches, and pleasing yourselves in the thoughts and pursuit of these things, for then you are in the readiest way to perdition; even to idolatrous worldliness, and apostasy of heart from God, and opening a door to every sin that seems but necessary to your worldly ends, and to odious hypocrisy for a cloak to all this, and to quiet your guilty minds with something that is like religion. When once you are saying, with worldly security, as he, Luke 12:17-19, "I will tear down my barns and build bigger ones, and there I will store all my grain and my goods. And I will say to myself, 'You have plenty of goods stored up for many years; relax, eat, drink, celebrate!'" you are then fooling yourselves, and near being called away as fools by death, verses 20, 21. And when, without a sense of the uncertainty of your lives, you are saying, as those in James 4:13, 14, "Today or tomorrow we will go into this or that town and spend a year there and do business and make a profit, whereas you do not know what will be on the morrow;" you forget what your lives are, that they are "a puff of smoke that appears for a short time and then vanishes," verse 14. "Do not boast about tomorrow; for you do not know what a day may bring forth," Proverbs 27:1.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Luke 12:17-19, and "Soul, take thy rest":** Baxter quotes the King James-style wording of the rich fool, "Soul, thou hast much goods laid up for many years, take thine ease, eat, drink, and be merry." The NET (fetched) reads "I will say to myself, 'You have plenty of goods stored up for many years; relax, eat, drink, celebrate!'" The NET has no "Soul" here; the man speaks "to myself." Baxter's "I will say to my soul, Soul" leans on the address to the soul, which the NET wording loses. His later phrase "Soul, take thy rest" (in the paragraph on tents) is not a quotation of Luke 12:19 in either translation; it is his paraphrase, kept in his words modernized, and it carries no reference in his text. The NET also has "tear down" where Baxter's text has "pull down," and reads "grain and my goods" where he has "fruits and goods."
- **Luke 12:20-21, "called away as fools by death":** In the NET, God (not death) says "You fool! This very night your life will be demanded back from you." Baxter's phrase paraphrases that verse; "by death" is his gloss.
- **James 4:13-14:** The NET (fetched) reads, in verse 13, "Today or tomorrow we will go into this or that town and spend a year there and do business and make a profit," and then, as a separate sentence in James's own voice (verse 14), "You do not know about tomorrow." Baxter's quotation runs the traders' words and the apostle's together with "whereas you know not what will be on the morrow." The first part is set in the NET wording; the closing clause is Baxter's own words, modernized, because the NET has no "whereas" clause there and it is a separate sentence. "a vapour appearing a little while, and then vanishing away" is the King James; the NET has "a puff of smoke that appears for a short time and then vanishes," and that is what the text now reads.
- **Proverbs 27:1:** NET wording; Baxter's "Boast not thyself therefore of to-morrow, for thou knowest not what a day may bring forth" is the King James with a "therefore."
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "house of mirth" and "house of mourning" (Ecclesiastes 7:2-4); "wait for the coming of their Lord" (cf. Luke 12:36); "what we shall do to be saved" (cf. Acts 16:30); "a dwelling as in tents" (cf. Hebrews 11:9); "flesh... against the Spirit" (cf. Galatians 5:17); "the quick" in "goes to the quick" is an old word for living flesh (the sensitive part).
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): covetous, greedy; designs, plans; sensibly, clearly; confute, refute; set more by, value more; upon the most, upon most people; apprehensions, awareness; discourse, talk; honours, honors; impertinent, out of place; vilify, despise; perusing, reviewing; casting up our accounts, adding up our accounts; voluptuous, pleasure-loving; cavils, quibbles; easilier, more easily; considerate, thoughtful; presently, at once; felicity, happiness; stupid, dull; customariness, mere routine; stock, resources; by accident, incidentally; accommodations, comforts; befooling, fooling; prosecution, pursuit; holy conference, holy conversation; ere long, before long; "ver.", verses.
- **savory** (every word of exhortation is savory to us): kept; it means pleasing or relished.
- **luxury:** kept; in Baxter's day it often meant self-indulgence and lust, not only wealth.
- **worldly security:** a technical term of Baxter's, careless confidence in worldly things (see CONVENTIONS.md on "security").
- **the creature:** kept as Baxter's word for anything created, as opposed to the Creator.
- **Latin** (note 75): Cicero's *De Finibus* and *Cato Maior* are as Baxter cites them; we have not checked the exact passages. The last Latin sentence has no source given and we have not identified one.
- **Footnote [77]** (Plato, from Diogenes Laertius) falls in the next section, not in this range.
