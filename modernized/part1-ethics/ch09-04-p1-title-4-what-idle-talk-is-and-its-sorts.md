# Chapter 9: Title 4, Direction 1 against idle talk (what is and is not idle talk, and its sorts)

**Title 4. Special directions against idle talk, and babbling.**

**Direction 1. Understand well what idle talk is; for many take that to be vain which is not, and many do not take that to be vain which is.**

I shall therefore explain this before I go any further.

*In the margin: What is not idle talk.*

The judgment of unbelievers and ungodly men here are of little regard. 1. Some of them think prayer to be but vain words, because God knows our needs and hearts (Job 22:2–3), and our service is not profitable to him: as if he had bid us, "Seek me in vain!" (Isaiah 45:19). [521] These I have elsewhere refuted. 2. Others think frequent preaching vain, and say, as the unbelievers of Paul, "What does this foolish babbler want to say?" (Acts 17:18); and as Pharaoh, "pay no attention to lying words!" (Exodus 5:9); but God says, "Keep in mind all the words I am solemnly proclaiming to you today ... For this is no idle word for you—it is your life!" (Deuteronomy 32:46–47). 3. Some carnal wretches think all vain in God's service, which is spiritual, and which they do not understand, or which is above the reach of a fleshly mind. [522] 4. And some think all vain in preaching, conversation, writing, or prayer, which is long. But Christ spoke no vain words when he "spent all night in prayer" (Luke 6:12). Nor are we bid to pray in vain, when we are bid "pray continually, earnestly, and persistently" (1 Thessalonians 5:17; Acts 6:4; Luke 18:1–2). Nor did Paul speak idly when he preached till midnight (Acts 20). Godliness is not vain which "is valuable in every way" (1 Timothy 4:8). Indeed as to their own salvation the wicked may make our preaching vain; but the word of God does not return empty. The offerings of the disobedient are vain (Isaiah 1:13), and the "prayer of the wicked, abominable to the Lord, but the prayer of the upright is his delight" (Proverbs 15:8). 5. Some think all preaching vain, of that which they know already, whereas they have most need to hear of that, lest they condemn themselves by sinning against their knowledge (2 Peter 1:12–13; Romans 14:22). 6. Some think it vain if the same things are often preached on, or repeated (see Philippians 3:1), though yet they never received and obeyed them; or if the same words are often repeated in prayer, though it is not from emptiness or affectation but fervor (Mark 14:39; Psalm 136; 119). 7. Unbelievers think our boasting in God is vain (2 Kings 18:20; Isaiah 49:4–5). 8. And some malicious adversaries charge it on ministers as preaching in vain, whenever the hearers are not converted. See Hebrews 4:2; Galatians 5:2; 3:4; 4:11; Isaiah 53:1.

On the other side many that are godly mistake in thinking, 1. That all talk is vain which is not of absolute necessity to some great use and end. [523] 2. And that all mirth and pleasant conversation is vain. Whereas the Holy Spirit says, "A cheerful heart brings good healing, but a crushed spirit dries up the bones" (Proverbs 17:22). "A joyful heart makes the face cheerful, but by a painful heart the spirit is broken" (Proverbs 15:13). King Abimelech saw Isaac sporting with Rebekah his wife (Genesis 26:8): laughing (as the Hebrew is), or playing (as the Chaldee, and Samaritan, and Septuagint), or jesting (as the Syriac, Arabic, and Latin Vulgate).

Observe these qualifications, and your mirth and playful talk will not be idle. 1. Let it be such and so much as is useful to maintain that cheerfulness of mind and liveliness of spirits which is profitable to your health and duty; for if bodily recreations are lawful, then tongue recreations are lawful when they are suited to their end. 2. Let your speech be wholesome, seasoned with salt, and not corrupt and rotten talk: do not jest with filthiness or sin. 3. Let it be harmless to others: do not make yourselves merry with the sins or miseries of other men. Do not jest to their wrong. 4. Let it be timely, and not when another frame of mind is more fitting, nor when graver or weightier conversation should take place. 5. Let it be moderate and not excessive, either wasting time in vain, or tending to accustom the mind of the speakers or hearers to levity, or to estrange them from things that should be preferred. 6. See that all your mirth and speech is sanctified by a holy end; that your intent in all is to sharpen your spirits and cheer up and fit yourselves for the service of God, as you do in eating and drinking, and all other things. 7. And mix (with cautious reverence) some serious things, that the end and use are not forgotten, and your mirth may not be altogether as empty and fruitless as that of the unsanctified is. Playful, pleasant, and refreshing talk is not vain, but lawful upon these conditions. 8. Still remembering that the most holy and profitable conversation must be most pleasant to us, and we must not, through a weariness of it, turn aside to carnal mirth, as more desirable, but only to natural honest mirth as a necessary accompaniment to exhilarate the spirits. [524]

*In the margin: What is idle talk. The sorts of it.*

Idle or vain words, then, are such as are unprofitable and do not tend to do good. [525] I here refrain from speaking of those idle words which are also worse than vain, as mentioned before among the sins of the tongue. Idle words are, 1. Either simply such which tend to no good at all. 2. Or comparatively such; which are about some small or inconsiderable good, when you should be speaking of greater things: the former sort are always idle, and therefore always sinful; the latter sort are sometimes lawful in themselves, that is, when greater matters are not to be talked of: in its season it is lawful to speak about the saving of a penny, or a point, or a pin; but out of season, when greater matters are in hand, this is but idle, sinful talk.

Also there is a great deal of difference between an idle word now and then, and a babbling, chattering habit, by which it becomes the daily practice of some loose-tongued persons, so that the greater part of the words of all their lives are merely vain.

The particular kinds of idle talk are scarcely to be numbered. Some of them are these.

1. When the tongue is like a vagrant beggar or masterless dog, that is never in the way, and never out of the way, being left to talk at random about any unprofitable matter that comes before it; and such will never lack matter to talk of; everything they see or hear is the subject of their chat; and one word begets occasion and matter for another, without end.

2. Another sort of idle talk is the vain conversations (by word or writing) of some learned men, in which they spend an excessive multitude of words about some small, irrelevant thing; not to edify, but to show their wit: [526] which Seneca rebukes at length.

3. Another sort of idle talk is vain and immoderate disputings about the smaller circumstances of religion, or frequent conversations about such unedifying things while greater matters should be talked of. "But avoid foolish controversies, genealogies, quarrels, and fights about the law because they are useless and empty" (Titus 3:9). "But the aim of our instruction is love that comes from a pure heart, a good conscience, and a sincere faith. Some have strayed from these and turned away to empty discussion. They want to be teachers of the law, but they do not understand what they are saying or the things they insist on so confidently" (1 Timothy 1:5–7). "O Timothy, protect what has been entrusted to you. Avoid the profane chatter and absurdities of so-called 'knowledge.' By professing it, some have strayed from the faith" (1 Timothy 6:20–21). "But avoid profane chatter because those occupied with it will stray further and further into ungodliness" (2 Timothy 2:16). "There are many rebellious people, idle talkers," etc. (Titus 1:10–11).

4. Another sort of idle talk is the using of a needless multitude of words, even about that which is good and necessary in itself, but might better be explained in a briefer manner. [527] Even in preaching or praying words may be vain; which is when they are not suited to the matter and the hearers: for you must note that the same words are necessary to one sort of hearers which are vain as to another sort. And therefore as ministers must be careful that they suit their manner of speech to their listeners, so hearers must be careful lest they censoriously and rashly call that vain which is unnecessary to them, or such as they: there may be present many ignorant persons that the preacher is better acquainted with than you: and the ignorant lose that which is concisely uttered: they must have it at length, in many words, and often repeated, or else they do not understand it, or do not remember that which they understand. But yet a real excess of words even about holy things must be avoided. "Do not be rash with your mouth or hasty in your heart to bring up a matter before God, for God is in heaven and you are on earth! Therefore, let your words be few. Just as dreams come when there are many cares, so the rash vow of a fool occurs when there are many words." [528] Two causes of idle words in prayer must be avoided: 1. Emptiness and rashness. 2. Affectation: that is, (1.) Affectation of words, as if you should be heard for saying so many words over and over (as the papists in their Jesus Psalter say over the name Jesu nine times together, and those nine times, fifteen times over, besides all their repetitions of it in the petitions themselves between. [529] So in the titles of the blessed Virgin, in her Litany, p. 525.) Hypocrites in all ages and religions have the same trifling way of devotion; as Christ shows of the very heathen that used this way: "When you pray, do not babble repetitiously like the Gentiles because they think that by their many words they will be heard. Do not be like them" (Matthew 6:7). (2.) There is an affectation of length that causes idle words in prayer; when men think that it is for the credit of their abilities to spend so much time, and speak so long together, or when their superstitious consciences in secret tie them to keep on so long, and do not have matter or affection corresponding to fill up the time, no wonder if it is filled up with words that are too much guilty of emptiness.

5. Another kind of idle talk is that which is purposely contrived to humor idle fancies, and amuse vicious minds, and pass away men's precious time: such are abundance of love-books, romances, plays, and play-books; volumes of emptiness, and hours full of studied emptiness (and worse): and such is much of the talk of pretended fools and jesters: vices which I can hardly express as odious as I perceive them.

6. Another sort is a habit of inordinate jesting: this vein or disposition is so strong in some that when they have a desire to vent a jest, they cannot hold, but out it must come whatever it costs, and be it ever so frivolous and vain (Ephesians 5:4).

7. Another sort is foolish talk, that does not have wit enough to make it edifying (Ephesians 5:4). And among idle talkers how much of it is foolish! How weary would it make a man to hear the talk of many babblers! How insipid it is! How stupid! Like the talk of a madman, or a drunken man, or a man in his sleep: it is far pleasanter not only to hear a bird chirp, but a swine grunt, than to hear much of their conversation. See Proverbs 10:14; 12:11; 28:19; 1 Peter 2:15; Proverbs 15:2, 14.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized; Scripture references put in modern form). The draft English translation of the Latin in note 525 was prepared for this project and is not from a published edition; have a Latinist check it before relying on fine points.

[521] Job 21:15; Malachi 3:14.

[522] Job 24:9; Hebrews 13:15.

[523] 1 Kings 18:27; Proverbs 29:9.

[524] James 5:13, "Is anyone in good spirits? He should sing praises."

[525] Otiosum verbum est quod justæ necessitatis aut intentione piæ utilitatis caret. Gregor. Moral. (English: An idle word is one that lacks either just necessity or the intention of godly usefulness. Gregory, *Moralia*.)

[526] 1 Corinthians 3:20; Romans 1:21.

[527] Job 35:16. Hugo says there is a time when nothing, and a time when something should be spoken; but never a time when all should be spoken.

[528] Ecclesiastes 5:2–3. The Spartan banished an orator for saying he could speak all day on any subject. Erasmus.

[529] See the Manual of Prayers printed at Antwerp, 1658, p. 507.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). All verses quoted here were fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Title:** "Tit." is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," a heading or division. Chapter 9 ("Directions for the Government of the Tongue") is divided into six Titles; this file opens Title 4, against idle talk. Title 3 (against lying) ends just before this range. The margin note "The aggravations of it" is the last line of the range; the paragraph it heads begins the next file.
- **Direction 1:** Baxter's heading is "Understand well what is idle talk"; his next sentence ("I shall therefore open this…") is set as the first body paragraph, following the layout used for Directions in this project. "open" (explain) is rendered "explain."
- **vain:** kept throughout, because this Title is Baxter's definition of "idle or vain" words, and "in vain" is still ordinary English; it means useless, pointless, without profit (not conceited). Where he writes "vanity" (emptiness), we use "emptiness."
- **Exodus 5:9 (important):** Baxter quotes Pharaoh, "Let them not regard vain words" (King James), as an example of someone calling God's word vain. The NET reads "pay no attention to lying words!", so the NET has Pharaoh call the words lies, not vain; the link to Baxter's point about "vain" words is weakened. Flagged.
- **Isaiah 45:19:** Baxter writes "as if he had bid us 'seek him in vain'" (adapting the King James "Seek ye me in vain" to the third person). The NET has God speaking: "Seek me in vain!" We quote the NET's first person. The sense (God did not tell his people to seek him for nothing) is the same.
- **Acts 17:18:** Baxter's "What will this babbler say" becomes the NET's "What does this foolish babbler want to say?" The NET adds "foolish." "unbelievers" is Baxter's "infidels" (here the Epicurean and Stoic philosophers).
- **Deuteronomy 32:46–47:** Baxter quotes "Set your hearts to all the words which I testify among you … for it is not a vain thing for you, because it is your life." NET wording, with "..." where Baxter skips. The NET's "no idle word" keeps the point Baxter leans on.
- **Luke 6:12:** Baxter's "prayed all night" is the NET's "spent all night in prayer."
- **"pray continually, earnestly, and persistently":** Baxter's own blend of 1 Thessalonians 5:17, Acts 6:4, and Luke 18:1–2 ("pray continually, instantly, and importunately"), kept in his wording; "instantly" (earnestly, urgently) and "importunately" (persistently, as the widow in Luke 18) are modernized. NET: "constantly pray" (1 Thessalonians 5:17); "we will devote ourselves to prayer and to the ministry of the word" (Acts 6:4); "they should always pray and not lose heart" (Luke 18:1).
- **Acts 20:** Paul preaching until midnight is Acts 20:7 (Baxter cites the chapter only).
- **1 Timothy 4:8:** Baxter's "profitable to all things" is the NET's "valuable in every way."
- **Isaiah 1:13:** not quoted; Baxter's "oblations" is rendered "offerings." The NET has "meaningless offerings."
- **Proverbs 15:8 (Baxter's wording kept):** Baxter writes "prayer of the wicked, abominable to the Lord," but Proverbs 15:8 (King James and NET alike) speaks of the *sacrifice* of the wicked; the prayer of the wicked as an abomination is Proverbs 28:9. Baxter's phrase looks like a blend, so we keep his words. NET for 15:8: "The Lord abhors the sacrifice of the wicked, but the prayer of the upright pleases him." Flagged.
- **Item 6 and item 7:** the source runs "Mark xiv. 39; Psal. cxxxvi.; cxix. 7. Unbelievers…"; the "7." is the number of Baxter's next point, not a verse, so the references are Mark 14:39, Psalm 136, and Psalm 119 (no verse given).
- **Proverbs 17:22:** the King James "doeth good like a medicine" is the NET's "brings good healing"; the sense Baxter uses (cheerfulness is good for you) stands.
- **Proverbs 15:13:** NET wording; the King James "merry heart" is the NET's "joyful heart."
- **Genesis 26:8 (important):** not quoted, but Baxter's argument rests on the King James "sporting" and on how the ancient versions render it (laughing, playing, jesting), as a warrant for mirth. The NET reads "observed Isaac caressing his wife Rebekah," which makes it a scene of physical affection between husband and wife rather than merry talk. Flagged. We kept "sporting" here because Baxter goes on to compare the versions' renderings of that one word; elsewhere his "sporting" talk is rendered "playful." The reference is moved to follow "his wife," where it can be given in parentheses. "the Chaldee" is Baxter's name for the Aramaic (Targum) rendering; "vulgar Latin" is the Latin Vulgate. We have not checked his account of each version.
- **Allusions (unmarked):** "the word of God does not return empty" (compare Isaiah 55:11); "wholesome, seasoned with salt" (Colossians 4:6, where the King James has "seasoned with salt"); "corrupt … talk" (Ephesians 4:29, King James "corrupt communication"); "as you do in eating and drinking, and all other things" (compare 1 Corinthians 10:31).
- **Ecclesiastes 5:2–3 (important; reference and wording):** Baxter gives no reference in the text; his note 528 prints "Eccles. v. 23," and Ecclesiastes 5 has no verse 23, so it is evidently 5:2, 3 run together; we print 5:2–3 in the note. NET wording. The King James ends "a fool's voice is known by multitude of words," which is Baxter's point (many words mark a fool). The NET reads "so the rash vow of a fool occurs when there are many words," which turns it toward rash vows. Flagged. (The NET notes that in the Hebrew numbering 5:2 is 5:1.)
- **Matthew 6:7:** Baxter cites 6:7 and quotes through "be ye not therefore like unto them," which is the start of verse 8. NET wording: the King James "use not vain repetitions, as the heathen do" is the NET's "do not babble repetitiously like the Gentiles." Baxter's own phrase "the very heathen" is kept in his prose.
- **Titus 3:9; 1 Timothy 1:5–7; 1 Timothy 6:20–21; 2 Timothy 2:16; Titus 1:10–11:** NET wording. Changes from the King James that bear on Baxter's theme: "vain jangling" becomes "empty discussion"; "profane and vain babblings" becomes "profane chatter"; "opposition of sciences falsely so called" becomes "absurdities of so-called 'knowledge'"; "unruly and vain talkers" becomes "rebellious people, idle talkers"; "charity" becomes "love." In 1 Timothy 6:20–21 Baxter quotes only to "erred concerning the faith," so the NET's closing "Grace be with you all" is left out. In 2 Timothy 2:16 the NET's verse ends mid-sentence. Baxter's "&c." after Titus 1:10 is kept as "etc."
- **James 5:13 (note 524):** Baxter's "Is any merry? Let him sing psalms" is the NET's "Is anyone in good spirits? He should sing praises." Baxter quotes only the second question; NET wording for that question.
- **Notes 521–523, 526:** reference lists, converted to modern form. We have not checked how each bears on the point (for example Job 24:9 in note 522); they are kept as printed.
- **Note 525:** the Latin is kept as printed. "Gregor. Moral." presumably refers to Gregory the Great's *Moralia* on Job; we have not checked the passage.
- **Note 527:** "Hugo" is probably Hugh of St. Victor, but we have not confirmed which Hugh Baxter means.
- **Note 528:** Baxter credits the Spartan anecdote to Erasmus, without naming the work; not checked.
- **Note 529 and the Jesus Psalter:** "papists" is Baxter's word for Roman Catholics, and the remarks on the Jesus Psalter and the Litany of the Virgin are his polemic, kept as written. "Jesu" is kept as he printed it. We have not checked the Antwerp *Manual of Prayers* (1658) or his page numbers.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): infidels → unbelievers; impious → ungodly; wants → needs; confuted → refuted; conference → conversation (religious talk, not a meeting); spake → spoke; oblations → offerings; returneth not → does not return; fervency → fervor; discourse(s) → conversation(s); alacrity → liveliness; accommodate → suited; savoury → wholesome; communication → talk; seasonable → timely; convenient → fitting; habituate → accustom; whet → sharpen; cautelous → cautious; recreating → refreshing; divert → turn aside; concomitant → accompaniment; forbear → refrain; prating → chattering; custom → habit; want (lack) → lack; bestow → spend; impertinent → irrelevant; reprehends at large → rebukes at length; open → explain; take heed → be careful; auditors → listeners; at large → at length; parts → abilities; hold on → keep on; answerable → corresponding; no marvel → no wonder; recreate (vicious minds) → amuse; vanity → emptiness; feigned → pretended; apprehend → perceive; list → desire; sottish → stupid; scarce → scarcely.
- **a point, or a pin:** a "point" was a lace or tie for fastening clothing; kept as written.
- **never in the way, and never out of the way:** Baxter's saying, left as written.
- **affectation:** kept; Baxter means artificial striving or putting on a show, here of many words or of length in prayer.
- **Seneca** (item 2): Baxter refers to Seneca's criticism of wordy display; he does not name the work, and we have not checked it.
- **love-books, romances, plays, and play-books:** Baxter's list, kept as written.
- **the sins of the tongue** ("as mentioned before"): a cross-reference to Title 1 of this chapter, not checked here.
