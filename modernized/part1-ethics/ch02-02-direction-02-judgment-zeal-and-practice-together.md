# Chapter 2: Direction 2

**Direction 2. Take care not to be religious only in opinion, without zeal and holy practice; or only in zealous affection, without a sound, well-grounded judgment; but see that judgment, zeal, and practice be joined.**

Of the first part of this advice (against a bare religion of mere opinion) I have spoken already, in my *Directions for a Sound Conversion*. To change your opinions is an easier matter than to change the heart and life. Holding the truth will save no man without a love and practice of the truth. This is the meaning of James 2, where he speaks so much of the unprofitableness of a dead, unaffected belief, that does not work by love, and does not command the soul to practice and obedience. To believe that there is a God, while you neglect him and disobey him, is unlikely to please him. To believe that there is a heaven, while you neglect it, and prefer the world to it, will never bring you there. To believe your duty, and not to perform it, and to believe that sin is evil, and yet to live in it, is to sin with aggravation, and have no excuse, and is not the way to be accepted or justified with God. To be of the same belief with holy men, without the same hearts and conduct, will never bring you to the same happiness. "That servant who knew his master's will but did not get ready or do what his master asked" shall be so far from being accepted for it that he "will receive a severe beating" (Luke 12:47). To believe that holiness and obedience is the best way will never save the disobedient and unholy.

And yet if judgment is not your guide, the most zealous affections will but rush you headlong, and make you run, though quite out of the way, like horses that have thrown the coachman or the riders. [48] To ride at full speed when you are quite out of the way is but laboriously to lose your time, and to prepare for further labor. The Jews that persecuted Christ and his apostles had the testimony of Paul himself that they "are zealous for God, but their zeal is not in line with the truth" (Romans 10:2). And Paul says of the deceivers and troublers of the Galatians (whom he wished even cut off) that they "court you eagerly, but for no good purpose" (Galatians 4:17). And he says of himself, while he persecuted Christians to prison and to death, that he "was zealous for God just as all of you are today" (Acts 22:3–4). Was not the papist, St. Dominic, who stirred up the persecution against the Christians in France and Savoy, to the murdering of many thousands of them, a very zealous man? And are not the butchers of the Inquisition zealous men? And were not the authors of the third canon of the General Council at the Lateran, under Pope Innocent the Third, very zealous men, who decreed that the pope should depose temporal lords, and give away their dominions, and absolve their subjects, if they would not exterminate the godly, called heretics? Were not the papists' powder-plotters zealous men? Has not zeal caused many of latter times to rise up against their lawful governors? and many to persecute the church of God, and to deprive the people of their faithful pastors without compassion on the people's souls? Does not Christ say of such zealots that "a time is coming when the one who kills you will think he is offering service to God" (John 16:2)? Therefore Paul says, "it is good to be sought eagerly for a good purpose at all times" (Galatians 4:18); showing you that zeal indeed is good, if sound judgment is its guide. Your first question must be, Whether you are in the right way? and your second, Whether you go quickly? It is sorrowful to observe what odious actions are committed in all ages of the world by the instigation of misguided zeal! And what a shame an imprudent zealot is to his profession! while making himself ridiculous in the eyes of the adversaries, he brings his profession itself into contempt, and makes the ungodly think that the religious are but a company of transported, brain-sick zealots; and thus they are hardened to their perdition. How many things unadvised affection provokes well-meaning people to that afterward will be their shame and sorrow.

Labor therefore for knowledge, and soundness of understanding; that you may know truth from falsehood, good from evil; and may walk confidently while you walk safely; and that you do not become a shame to your profession by a furious persecution of that which you must afterward confess to be an error; by drawing others to that which you would afterward wish that you had never known yourselves. And yet see that all your knowledge has its effect upon your heart and life; and take every truth as an instrument of God to reveal himself to you, or to draw your heart to him, and conform you to his holy will.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows the Latin passages in parentheses; it was prepared for this project and is not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check it before relying on fine points.

[48] Scientia quæ est remota à justitia, calliditas potium quam sapientia appellanda est. P. Scalig. (Knowledge that is cut off from justice should be called cunning rather than wisdom. P. Scaliger. The source prints "potium," apparently for "potius.") Of the necessity of prudence in religious men, read Nic. Videlius, *de Prudent. Veterum* (*On the Prudence of the Ancients*). The imprudences of well-meaning men have done as much harm to the church sometimes as the persecution of enemies. For example, when Constantine, the son of Constans, was emperor, some busy men would prove from the orthodox doctrine of the Trinity that his two brothers, Tiberius and Heraclius, should reign with him, saying, Si in Trinitate credimus, tres etiam coronemus (If we believe in the Trinity, let us crown three as well); which cost the chief of them a hanging. Abbas Urspergens. Edit. Melancth. p. 162. (The Abbot of Ursperg, in Melanchthon's edition, p. 162.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Romans 10:2 (important):** Baxter quotes the King James, "a zeal of God, but not according to knowledge." His whole Direction is that zeal needs knowledge and sound judgment. The NET reads "zealous for God, but their zeal is not in line with the truth." "Truth" is a narrower word than "knowledge" and loses part of Baxter's point about judgment. Flagged.
- **Galatians 4:17–18 (important):** Baxter's reading rests on the King James word "zealously": the false teachers "zealously affect you, but not well," and "it is good to be zealously affected always in a good matter." The NET reads "court you eagerly, but for no good purpose" and "it is good to be sought eagerly for a good purpose at all times." The NET makes the verbs about courting or seeking someone's attention, which is the Greek's main sense in the context, and drops the idea of zeal that Baxter draws out. Flagged: Baxter's use of verse 18 as a general statement that zeal is good in a good cause does not follow from the NET wording.
- **Acts 22:3–4; John 16:2:** NET wording. Baxter quotes "I was zealous towards God as ye are all this day" and "whosoever killeth you will think he doth God service." Baxter adds his own gloss on John 16:2, "or offereth a service (acceptable) to God," which we omit since the NET's "offering service to God" already says it.
- **Luke 12:47:** Baxter quotes without a reference ("He that knoweth his master's will … shall be beaten with many stripes"). The NET reads "That servant who knew his master's will but did not get ready or do what his master asked will receive a severe beating."
- **James 2** (cited only): Baxter's reference to the argument that faith without works is dead.
- **Historical claims** (paragraph 2): Baxter's list of "zealous" persecutors includes St. Dominic and his supposed role in persecuting the Albigensians ("Christians in France and Savoy"), the canon of the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) under Pope Innocent III about deposing rulers who tolerated heretics, the Inquisition, and the Gunpowder Plot (1605). These are Baxter's examples and his characterizations, written from his own Protestant standpoint, and we have kept them as written. We have not independently checked them against the historical record.
- **papist:** kept as Baxter's word. **powder-plotters:** the Gunpowder Plot conspirators.
- **rush you headlong:** Baxter's "precipitate you." **ride at full speed:** his "ride post." **go quickly:** his "go apace."
- **conduct:** Baxter's "conversations." **happiness:** his "felicity." **is unlikely:** his "is unlike."
- **sorrowful:** Baxter's "sad." **brain-sick:** left as written; it means mentally disordered.
- **in the right way … whether you go quickly** (paragraph 2): Baxter's two questions: first, whether you are on the right road; second, how fast you are going.
- **second Direction, two halves:** Baxter warns against (a) opinion without practice and (b) zeal without knowledge. The first is covered in his earlier book; the second he develops here.
- **Constantine … Tiberius and Heraclius** (note 48): Constantine IV (reigned 668–685) of the Byzantine Empire; the "Three Crowns" demand of 681. Baxter uses the story as an example of an absurd inference from the doctrine of the Trinity.
