# Chapter 4: Direction 6, the sorrowful effects of hard-heartedness

**Direction 6. Take notice of the sorrowful effects of hard-heartedness in the world.**

This fills the world with wickedness and confusion, with wars and bloodshed; and leaves it under that lamentable desertion and delusion, which we see in far greatest part of the earth. How many kingdoms are left in the blindness of heathenism and Islam, for hardening their hearts against the Lord! How many Christian nations are given up to the most gross deceits of popery, and princes and people are enemies to reformation, because they hardened their hearts against the light of truth! What vice so odious, even beastly filthiness, and bitterest hatred, and persecution of the ways of God, which men of all degrees and ranks do not carelessly wallow in through the hardness of their hearts! This is the thing that grieves the godly, that wears out good magistrates, and breaks the hearts of faithful ministers: when they have done their best, they are forced, as Christ himself before them, to grieve for the hardness of men's hearts. Alas! we live among the dead; our towns and countries are in a sadder case than Egypt, when every house had a dead man. Even in our churches, it were well if the dead were only under ground, and most of our seats had not a dead man, who sits as if he heard, and kneels as if he prayed, when nothing ever pierced to the quick. We have studied the most quickening words, we have preached with tears in the most earnest manner, and yet we cannot make them feel! as if we cried like Baal's worshippers, O Baal, hear us! or, like the Irish to their dead, Why would you die, and leave your house, and lands, and friends? So we talk to them about the death of their souls, and their willful misery, who never feel the weight of anything we say: we are left to ring them a peal of lamentation, and weep over them as the dead who are not moved by our tears: we cast the seed into stony ground (Matthew 13:5, 20); it stops in the surface, and it is not in our power to open their hearts, and get within them. I confess that we are much to blame ourselves, that ever we did speak to such miserable souls, without more pressing earnestness and tears; (and it is because the stone of the heart is much uncured in ourselves; for which God now justly sets so many of us aside;) but yet, we must say, our persistence is such, as leaves them without excuse. We speak to them of the greatest matters in all the world; we speak it to them in the name of God; we show them his own word for it; and plead with them the arguments which he has put into our mouths; and yet we speak as to posts and stones, to men past feeling. What a pitiful sight was it to see Christ stand weeping over Jerusalem, for the hardness of their hearts, and the nearness and greatness of their misery! while they themselves were so far from weeping for it, that they raged against the life of him who so much pitied them! We bless God that it is not thus with all. He has encouraged some of us with the heart-yielding, obedient attention of many great congregations: but, among the best, alas! how many of these hardened sinners are mixed! and, in many places, how do they abound! Hence it is that such odious abominations are committed; such filthiness, and lying, and perjury, and acts of malicious enmity against the servants of the Lord; and that so many are haters of God and godliness. If Satan had not first hardened their hearts, he could never have brought them to such odious crimes, as now with impudence are committed in the land. As Lot's daughters had to make their father drunk, that he might commit the sin of incest; so the devil first deprives men both of reason and feeling, that he might bring them to such heinous wickedness as this, and make them laugh at their own destruction, and abhor those most who would gladly save them. And they are not only past feeling, but so hate any quickening ministry, or truth, or means which would recover their feeling, that they seem to go to hell as some condemned criminals to the gallows, who make themselves drunk before they go, as if it were all they had to care for to keep themselves hoodwinked, from knowing or feeling where they go, till they are there.

See what a picture of a hardened people God gives to Ezekiel, "But the house of Israel is unwilling to listen to you, because they are not willing to listen to me, for the whole house of Israel is hardheaded and hardhearted" (Ezekiel 3:7). Observe but what a case it is that they are so insensible of, and then you will see what a hard-hearted sinner, past feeling, is.

1. They are the servants of sin (Romans 6:16); in the power of it, corrupted by it; and yet they do not feel it.

2. They have the guilt of many thousand sins upon them, all is unpardoned that ever they committed; and yet they do not feel it.

3. They have the threatenings and curses of God in force against them in his word; even words so terrible, as you would think might frighten them out of their sins or their wits; and they claim to believe this word of God; and yet they do not feel.

4. They are in the power of the devil; ruled and deceived by him, and taken captive by him at his will (Acts 26:18; 2 Timothy 2:26).

5. They may be certain that if they die in this condition they shall be damned, and they are uncertain whether they shall live another day; they are never sure to be one hour longer out of hell; and yet they do not feel.

6. They know that they must die, and that it is a great change, and of the greatest endless consequence, that death will make with them; and they know that this is sure and near, and are past doubt of it; and yet they do not feel it. [146]

7. They must shortly appear before the Lord, and be judged for all that they have done in the body, and be doomed to their endless state; and yet they do not feel.

8. They know that life is short, and that they have but a little time to prepare for all this terrible change, and that it must go with them forever, as they now prepare; and yet they do not feel.

9. They hear and read of the case of hardened, wicked men, who have gone before them, and have resisted grace, and lost their time, as they now do; and they read or hear of the miserable end that such have come to; and yet they do not feel.

10. They have a world of examples continually before them; they see the filthy lives of many for their warning, and the holy lives of others for their imitation, and see how Christ and Satan strive for souls; and yet they do not feel.

11. They are always before the eye of God, and do all things before his face; he warns them, and calls them to repentance; and yet they do not feel.

12. They have Christ as it were crucified before their eyes (Galatians 3:1): they hear of his sufferings; they may see in him what sin is, and what the love of God is; he pleads with them his blood and sufferings against their obstinate unkindness; and yet they do not feel.

13. They have everlasting joy and glory offered them, and heaven so opened to them in God's promises, that they may see it as in a mirror (1 Corinthians 13:12). They claim to believe how much the blessed spirits there abhor such wickedness as theirs; and yet they do not feel.

14. They have the torments of hell opened to them in the word of God; they read what impenitent souls must suffer to all eternity; they hear some in despair in this life, roaring in the misery of their souls; they hear the joyful thanksgivings of believers, that Christ delivers them from those torments; and yet they do not feel.

[146] Feriemini, moriemini, sentietis: an cæci autem an videntes, id in vestra manu est. Optate igitur bene mori (quod ipsum nisi bene vixeritis frustra est). Optate, inquam, nitimini, et quod in vobis est facile: reliquum illi committite; qui vos in hanc vitam ultro non vocatos intulit; egressuris, non nisi vocatus et rogatus manum dabit. Non mori autem nolite optare. Petrarch. Dial. 107. 1. 2. (English, rendered loosely: You will be struck, you will die, you will feel it; whether blind or seeing is in your own hand. Wish, therefore, to die well (which itself is in vain unless you have lived well). Wish, I say, and strive, and what lies in you is easy; commit the rest to him who brought you into this life unasked [literally, "of his own accord, though you were not called"]; to those about to depart, he will give a hand only when called upon and asked. But do not refuse to wish not to die [the double negative is as printed; the sense is probably "do not wish for non-death," or it may be a misprint]. Petrarch, *Dialogue* 107, book 2 [the exact work is not confirmed; probably the *Remedies for Fortune Good and Bad*].)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). The text of this section begins at the start of Direction 6, so no earlier Direction or section heading appears in this file. The section heading for the Directions against hardness of heart is in the preceding file.
- **Ezekiel 3:7:** NET wording, fetched. Baxter's source text has the King James "impudent and hard-hearted"; the NET reads "hardheaded and hardhearted." Baxter's "impudent" suggests shamelessness, which "hardheaded" does not plainly say, though both describe stubborn brazenness. The sense Baxter uses (insensibility) rests on "hardhearted," which the NET keeps.
- **Matthew 13:5, 20:** Baxter gives a reference without quoting. His "stony ground" is the King James phrase; the NET has "rocky ground." We keep Baxter's words and add the modern reference.
- **1 Corinthians 13:12:** Baxter's "as in a glass" alludes to the King James ("through a glass, darkly"). In the NET: "we see in a mirror indirectly." "Glass" meant a mirror, so we wrote "mirror"; the NET also adds "indirectly," which is the sense of the King James "darkly" and fits Baxter's point that heaven is seen only dimly.
- **Other references** (Romans 6:16; Acts 26:18; 2 Timothy 2:26; Galatians 3:1) are given by Baxter without quotation marks; they are converted to modern form and Baxter's wording kept. His "Gal. iii. 1" ("crucified before their eyes") corresponds to the NET's "Before your eyes Jesus Christ was vividly portrayed as crucified."
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "every house had a dead man" (Exodus 12:30); "Baal's worshippers, O Baal, hear us" (1 Kings 18:26); Lot's daughters (Genesis 19:30-38); Christ weeping over Jerusalem (Luke 19:41); Christ grieved at "the hardness of men's hearts" (Mark 3:5); "past feeling" (Ephesians 4:19); "judged for all that they have done in the body" (2 Corinthians 5:10); "seed ... stony ground" also in Mark 4 and Luke 8 parallels.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): behold, see; affright, frighten; doleful, sorrowful; Mahometanism, Islam; securely (careless confidence), carelessly; fain, forced / had to; wearieth, wears out; importunate, pressing; importunity, persistence; layeth by, sets aside; malefactors, criminals; wilful, willful; impudency, impudence; "take on them to believe," claim to believe; "forever" for "for ever"; "do not feel" for "feel not" and "do not feel it" for "feel it not" (modern negation, no added meaning).
- **heathenism, popery:** Baxter's words, kept. "Popery" and the charge that "many Christian nations are given up to the most gross deceits of popery" are his polemic against the Roman Church; kept as he wrote it.
- **the Irish to their dead:** Baxter's illustration of an Irish custom of addressing the dead with the question "Why did you die?" We have not confirmed the custom and give it as his claim.
- **hoodwinked:** kept; it means blindfolded.
- **the stone of the heart ... uncured in ourselves; for which God now justly sets so many of us aside:** Baxter's parenthesis. We have not confirmed what he refers to by ministers being "laid by" (his phrase); we keep it as his and make no claim.
- **Numbered list (1.-14.):** Baxter's numbering kept. In items 1-14 the refrain "and yet they feel not" (items 1, 2 and 6: "feel it not") is modernized to "and yet they do not feel" ("do not feel it"); item 4 has no refrain in the source.
- **[146] Petrarch:** The note's source is Petrarch (Francesco Petrarca); "Dial." probably refers to the *De remediis utriusque fortunae*, but we have not confirmed it. The final sentence, "Non mori autem nolite optare," is printed as in the source and may be corrupt. "cæci" is the source's spelling of *caeci*.
