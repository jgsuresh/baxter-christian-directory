# Chapter 9: Title 6, Direction 1 continued: the misery and guilt of scorning holiness (points 3-19)

*This file begins partway through Direction 1 of Title 6, in the middle of Baxter's numbered list of what the scorner does (the list began at point 2 in the previous file), and ends partway through the same Direction.*

3. Consider what a terrible badge of misery you carry about you! you bear the mark of Satan, death, and hell in your forehead, as it were. If there were any doubt whether a swearer, or drunkard, or fornicator may be in a state of grace, yet it is past all doubt that a scorner of godliness is not: it would be strange indeed for that man to be holy that derides holiness: there is scarcely any sort of men in the world that are more undoubtedly in a state of damnation than you are. It is dark to us what God will do with unbelievers, and heathens that never had the means of salvation; but what he will do with all the unbelieving and ungodly that have had the means, we know past doubt; much more what he will do with those that are not only devoid of holiness, but deride it. I do not deny that yet if you are converted you may be saved: and oh that God would "grant [you] repentance and then knowledge of the truth," that you might escape out of the devil's snares, who leads you captive at his will (2 Timothy 2:25–26). It is written of Basil that by his prayers he caused the devil to give back a writing by which a wretched man had sold his soul to him, that he might enjoy his master's daughter; and that the man repented and was delivered: if you may be so recovered it will be a happy day for you. But till then it is as sure as the Scripture is sure that you are a miserable creature, and a ruined man if you die in that condition that you are in. Oh with what fear should you rise and lie down, if you had your wits about you, lest you should die before you are converted. [554]

4. To scorn holiness is a defiance of grace, as if you renounced God's mercy: you do your worst to drive away all hope, and make your case incurable and desperate. For if ever you are saved, it must be by this grace and holy life which you deride: and is scorning grace the way to get it? And is it likely that the Holy Spirit will come and dwell in the man that scorns his sanctifying works?

5. To scorn godliness is a daring of God to give up his patience, and at once to execute his vengeance on you! Can you wonder if he should make you a monument of his justice, and set you up for all others to take warning by? Who is fitter for this than the scornful opposers of his grace and service? Do not hasten vengeance, man; it will come soon enough. Will a worm defy the God of heaven?

6. How little do you understand of all that you oppose! Did you ever try a holy life? If you had, you would not speak against it; if you have not, are you not ashamed to speak evil of that which you do not understand? It is a thing that none can thoroughly know without experience: try it awhile, and then speak your mind.

7. Did you ever consider how many judgments are against you, and whom you contradict and scorn? (1.) If you scorn serious godliness, preaching, hearing, reading, praying, meditating, and strictly avoiding sin, you contradict God himself; for none in all the world is so holy, or so much for holiness, as he: and therefore ultimately it is him that all your malice is against; even God the Father, and the Redeemer, and the Sanctifier. (2.) You set yourself against all the evidence of Scripture; (3.) And against all the works of God: for all conspire to call the world to holiness and strict obedience to God. (4.) And you contradict all the prophets and apostles, and all the ancient fathers of the church; and all the martyrs and saints of God that were ever in the world; and all the learned faithful ministers and pastors of the church that are or have been; and all the godly throughout the world; and all that ever had experience of a holy life: and what are you, that you should scorn all these? Are you wiser than all the ministers and godly persons in the world? than all the apostles and holy martyrs of Christ that ever were? indeed, than God himself?

8. Did you ever notice how unlike the speech of Christ and his apostles was to yours? Did they deride men for being too diligent for the pleasing of God and saving of their souls? Just read these places following and judge: Matthew 5:8, 11, 20; 6:21, 33; 7:13–14; 1 Peter 4:18; 2 Peter 3:11; 1:10; Hebrews 11:6; Matthew 5; Romans 8:1, 5–9, 13; Philippians 3:18–19; Hebrews 12:28–29.

9. Do you not yourself do as much for the world as those that you oppose do for heaven? Are you offended that they preach and pray so long? Are you not longer about your worldly business? And are not gallants longer at a feast, or visit, or games and recreations? Are you offended that they talk so much of heaven? And do you not talk more of earth? And which of these do you think in your conscience does better deserve to be sought and talked of? Which will prove better at the last? And whose labor will be more worthy of derision?

10. What gain would it be to you if you had your will, and praying, and preaching, and holiness were as much banished from the world as you would have it? and if men to please you should displease God, and cast away their souls forever? Would it do you good for earth to be so like hell? It is the grief of godly men already to think how little holiness is in the world: there is scarcely a sadder thought that ever came into my heart than to survey all the nations of the earth; and to think how ignorance and ungodliness abound, and how few there are that are truly holy; and what an inhuman creature is that who yet would have them fewer, and scorn out of the world the little wisdom and piety that is left! [555]

And would it be any pleasure to you in hell if men should accompany you there to humor you? No, it would be your everlasting torment to see there so many forever ruined by listening to your wicked counsel. Do not say that you are not so cruel, and it is not their damnation that you desire: no more is it your own that you desire; but it is all one as to the effect, if you desire the way to it. You may as well give one man poison, and deride another for eating and drinking, and yet say, it is not your death that I desire. But die they must, if they are ruled by you.

11. Would he not be a cruel man that would not do as much for the saving of his neighbor's soul as that which you deride them for in the saving of their own? If you were sick, should I refuse to pray for your life? Or if I knew that it might save another's soul, should I think any means or pains too much? If not, I think I may be allowed to do as much for myself as charity bids me do for another.

12. Is it a season to mock holiness, when at the same time there are so many millions of souls in heaven that all came there by the way of holiness? and so many millions of souls in hell that all came there for lack of holiness? and while you are chattering against it, they are crying out in despair at the folly of their neglecting it? Would one of the souls in heaven regard your mocks if he were to live on earth again? Or would one of the souls in hell be mocked into going there, if they were only tried with another life? If you saw at this hour what unholy souls in hell are suffering, and what holy souls in heaven enjoy, would you ever mock holiness again? For shame consider what you do; and see by faith the things that mortal eyes do not behold.

13. What if men should yield to your derisions, and forsake a holy life to please you? Would you undertake to justify them or be answerable for them before that God that required holiness, and will condemn all the unholy? Would you get them off, and save them from damnation? Alas! poor soul, how unable you will be to save yourself! And will you take them for wise men, if they displease the Lord, and go to hell to humor such a one as you?

14. You will not yourself be mocked out of your house, or land, or right, nor from your food, or drink, or rest: would you cast these away if another should only mock you for using them? I think you would not. And would you have wise men be mocked out of their salvation?

15. You would not think it reasonable that your children or servants be derided for loving or obeying you? or your very horse faulted for serving you? And do they owe you more than we all owe God?

16. God highly honors them and dearly loves them for that very thing that you hate and deride them for. John 16:27 and 14:21, "The person who has my commandments and obeys them is the one who loves me. The one who loves me will be loved by my Father, and I will love him and will reveal myself to him." Psalm 11:7, "Certainly the Lord is just; he rewards godly deeds. The upright will experience his favor." Psalm 146:8, "The Lord loves the godly." 2 Corinthians 6:16–18, "For we are the temple of the living God, just as God said, 'I will live in them and will walk among them, and I will be their God, and they will be my people.' Therefore 'come out from their midst, and be separate,' says the Lord, 'and touch no unclean thing, and I will welcome you, and I will be a father to you, and you will be my sons and daughters,' says the All-Powerful Lord." And do you dare scorn the sons and daughters of the Almighty? even for that very thing for which he has promised to receive them, and to be a Father to them? How contrary then are you to God! Malachi 3:16–18, "A scroll was prepared before him in which were recorded the names of those who respected the Lord and honored his name. 'They will belong to me,' says the Lord of Heaven's Armies, 'in the day when I prepare my own special property. I will spare them as a man spares his son who serves him'": and do you dare scorn God's jewels, and those that are thus precious to him? "For I will honor those who honor me, but those who despise me will be cursed!" (1 Samuel 2:30). And will you be one of his despisers, opposing that in others for which God himself has promised to honor them?

17. To hate and scorn holiness is to hate and scorn God's own image; and the clearest image of God that is under heaven; even that which Christ came down from heaven to give us the first draft of; even that copy of the holy life of Christ which by the Spirit of God is drawn upon the heart. [556] And he that scorns this image of God scorns the Holy Spirit that made it, and scorns Christ who gave us the first pattern, and scorns God himself whose image it is. Says Chrysostom, God is loved and hated in his servants, as a king is honored or despised in his image. And he that dares scorn God, and scorn Jesus Christ, and scorn the Holy Spirit, in the image of God upon his children, I think should never have the nerve once to expect to be saved by the God that he scorns.

18. You are the shame of human nature; and make man so like a devil that it is hard to prove that the devils can do much worse than you. [557] Can there be a greater sin than for a creature to scorn and deride the image and laws of his Creator? and hate and oppose, or persecute men for obeying him, and seeking to please him, and to save their souls? What could you do worse if you would study to be as bad as you can? What a shame it is to your understanding to be so blind! and to your heart to be so wicked! It would not be half so great a shame to scorn the sun for shining, or the earth for bearing fruit; for though these are God's creatures, yet they do not bear the image of his holiness as his children do. When he condemns men at last it will be upon this account. Matthew 25:40, 45, "I tell you the truth, just as you did not do it (or did it) for one of the least of these (brothers or sisters of mine), you did not do it (or did it) for me." Oh wonderful, that the nature of man can ever come to this, to hate, and oppose, and scorn the image and obedience of his Maker, and make a mock of the holiness of God! It is a great question whether the very tempting of men to such sins as these is not the devil's greatest sin: and to commit it is worse than to tempt you to commit it (*cæteris paribus*). And for a man that has a Savior offered him thus to scorn his Savior's grace, and mock his servants, must be far worse than for the devil to do it who has no Savior, no pardon offered, and no hope, but is shut up under endless desperation: as it is worse for a child to curse his father, or scorn him, than for an enemy to do it. Think and tremble how near this deriding or opposing the work of the Holy Spirit comes to the unpardonable blasphemy against him. [558]

19. What villainy may not be expected from you, that can commit such a sin as this? May not your neighbor look for any mischief that your carnal interest shall lead you to do against him? Is it any wrong to you to think that you are a thief, a murderer, a whoremonger, a deceiver, unless it is for lack of a temptation to commit them; or that you would be a traitor against your king and country; or treacherous to your truest friend, if you were tempted to it; when you scorn men for obeying God himself? [559] Can that man balk at any wickedness that he is equally tempted to, who dares scorn his Maker, the Redeemer, and the Sanctifier; and spit contempt upon holiness itself, the image of his Judge? For my part, if ever I trust you or any such man as you with life or liberty, or with the worth of a groat, it shall be your interest and not your honesty and conscience that I will trust; I will trust you little further than I would trust the devil himself that governs you.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized; Scripture references put in modern form). A draft English translation follows the Latin in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[554] Cyrillus Arrianorem Episcopus, Hunnericum Regem persuasit, non posse pacatum atque longævum obtinere regnum, nisi nomen perderet innocentum. Qui tamen Dei judicio post non multos dies turpissima morte præventus, scatens vermibus expiravit. Victor. Utic. p. 369. (English: Cyril, bishop of the Arians, persuaded King Huneric that he could not hold a peaceful and long-lasting reign unless he destroyed the name of the innocent. Yet he [Huneric], by the judgment of God, was overtaken not many days later by a most shameful death, and died swarming with worms. Victor of Utica, p. 369.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). Every NET quotation here was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Title:** "Tit." in Baxter is his abbreviation for "Title," a heading or division. Chapter 9 is divided into six Titles; this file belongs to Title 6, against profane deriding and scorning.
- **Where this file starts:** the range opens at point 3 of a numbered list inside Direction 1 of Title 6. The previous file ends with Baxter's turn from "what it is that you oppose" to "the aggravations of the sin," which he begins at "2." (probably counting the preceding list, points 1-20 on what the scorner opposes, as his first consideration); this file continues with 3 through 19. The list continues in the next file.
- **2 Timothy 2:25–26:** Baxter quotes only the phrase "give thee repentance to the acknowledging of the truth" (King James); the rest of the sentence ("escape out of the devil's snares, who leads you captive at his will") is his paraphrase of verse 26, kept in his words. We give the NET phrase "grant ... repentance and then knowledge of the truth"; the NET has "them," and we have bracketed "[you]" to keep Baxter's address to the reader.
- **John 16:27 and 14:21:** Baxter cites both but quotes only 14:21; we give the NET of 14:21. Its "obeys" stands for the King James "keepeth."
- **Psalm 11:7:** the NET wording changes the sense Baxter leans on. The King James read "The righteous Lord loveth righteousness; his countenance doth behold the upright," which is exactly his point (God loves the very thing the scorner derides). The NET reads "he rewards godly deeds. The upright will experience his favor." The idea of God's favor toward the upright remains, but "loves righteousness" is gone.
- **Psalm 146:8:** Baxter quotes only the last clause; King James "The Lord loveth the righteous," NET "The Lord loves the godly." The sense is the same for his argument.
- **2 Corinthians 6:16–18:** Baxter's reference reads "2 Cor. vi. 16-19," but 2 Corinthians 6 has only 18 verses, and the words he quotes end at verse 18. We have corrected the reference to 6:16–18. He starts mid-verse at "For ye are the temple"; the NET reads "we," following a different Greek reading. Baxter's next sentence ("for which he has promised to receive them") rests on the King James "I will receive you"; the NET has "I will welcome you," which keeps the sense.
- **Malachi 3:16–18:** Baxter quotes, abridged, parts of verses 16–17 (nothing from verse 18). We give the NET of the parts he quotes. The NET changes the sense he leans on in two places: the King James "a book of remembrance was written ... for them that ... thought upon his name" is in the NET "a scroll was prepared ... recorded the names of those who ... honored his name"; and the King James "in that day when I make up my jewels" is in the NET "in the day when I prepare my own special property." Baxter's following phrase "God's jewels" (kept in his words) builds on the King James "jewels"; the NET's "special property" keeps the idea of something treasured but loses the image.
- **1 Samuel 2:30:** Baxter quotes the last part of the verse. The King James ended "they that despise me shall be lightly esteemed"; the NET has "those who despise me will be cursed!", a stronger word. His point (God honors those who honor him) is unchanged. In the text the reference stands after the quotation, as Baxter has it.
- **Matthew 25:40, 45:** Baxter blends the two verses into one sentence, inserting "(or did it)" and "(my brethren)" to cover both. We keep his blend and his parentheses, using the NET wording of the two verses: "brothers or sisters of mine" for his "my brethren," "I tell you the truth" for "Verily I say unto you," and "for me" for "unto me." The NET of verse 45 alone reads "just as you did not do it for one of the least of these, you did not do it for me"; verse 40 reads "just as you did it for one of the least of these brothers or sisters of mine, you did it for me."
- **Reference placement:** in point 16 and point 18 Baxter puts each reference before its quotation as part of the sentence ("Psal. xi. 7, '...'"). We left them where he put them, in modern form, rather than move them after the quotation.
- **Point 8 references:** converted to modern form; we have not checked each one against Baxter's argument. "2 Peter 3:11; 1:10" means 2 Peter 3:11 and 2 Peter 1:10.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording, references not in his text): "speak evil of that which you do not understand" (compare Jude 10 and 2 Peter 2:12); "see by faith the things that mortal eyes do not behold" (compare 2 Corinthians 4:18 and Hebrews 11:1); "the unpardonable blasphemy" against the Holy Spirit (Matthew 12:31–32, which his note 558 cites as Matthew 12).
- **Basil** (point 3): Baxter repeats a story about Basil (probably Basil of Caesarea) and a man who sold his soul to the devil. We have not traced which source he used.
- **Chrysostom** (point 17): Baxter gives no source for the saying, and we have not traced it.
- **Note 554:** "Arrianorem" is printed so in the source, probably for *Arrianorum* ("of the Arians"); we kept it as printed. "Victor. Utic." is Victor of Utica, probably his history of the Vandal persecution in North Africa; we have not checked the page number or edition. Huneric was probably the Vandal king of that name; we have not confirmed this against Baxter's source. "innocentum" is translated "the innocent"; Victor presumably means the orthodox Christians the Vandals persecuted.
- **Note 557:** "Martin. Dumiens." is probably Martin of Dumio (Martin of Braga); we have not confirmed the work Baxter means by "de Morib."
- **Notes 555, 556, 559:** these are bare references as printed. We have not confirmed why Baxter attached each one where it falls; for example, note 555 (Romans 11:1–2) may point to Elijah's complaint in Romans 11:2–4 that few were left faithful.
- ***cæteris paribus*** (point 18): Latin, "other things being equal." Kept as printed.
- **judgments** (point 7): opinions, verdicts; here, the judgment of all those he lists.
- **gallants** (point 9): fashionable young men about town. Kept.
- **groat** (point 19): an old English coin of small value (four pence). Kept.
- **whoremonger** (point 19): kept, as Baxter's blunt word.
- **carnal** (point 19): unspiritual, ruled by the flesh. Kept.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): "scorn at / mock at / deride at" to "scorn / mock / deride" (the "at" dropped throughout); infidels to unbelievers; void of to devoid of; undone (ruined) to ruined; uncurable to incurable; Holy Ghost to Holy Spirit; give over to give up; presently to at once; time enough to soon enough; mark (notice) to notice; strict avoiding sin to strictly avoiding sin; yea to indeed; Read but to Just read; labour to labor; Nay to No; hearkening to listening; humour to humor; all is one to it is all one; methinks to I think; for want of to for lack of; prating to chattering; mocked thither to mocked into going there (one added word to carry "thither"); if they were but tried to if they were only tried; bring them off (get them acquitted) to get them off; meat (food in general) to food; mock but thee to only mock you; dispraised to faulted; draught (sketch) to draft; have the face (the boldness) to have the nerve; must needs be to must be; villany to villainy; stick at to balk at; perfidious to treacherous; Saviour to Savior; neighbour to neighbor.
- **"you bear the mark ... "** (point 3): Baxter continues after the exclamation mark with a lowercase word; we keep his punctuation. The same holds for the lowercase questions after question marks in points 7, 10, 12, and 18.
