# Chapter 1, Part 2 (continued): Temptations 16–17

**Temptation 16.** Another temptation is their own prosperity: they think God, when he prospers them, is not so angry with them as preachers tell them: and it is a very hard thing in health and prosperity to lay to heart either sin or threats, and to have such serious, lively thoughts of the life to come as men that are awakened by adversity have; and especially men that are familiar with death. Prosperity is the greatest temptation to security, and delaying repentance, and putting off preparation for eternity. Overcome prosperity, and you overcome your greatest snare.

**Direction 16.** Go into the sanctuary, indeed go into the churchyard, and see the end; and judge by those skulls, and bones, and dust, if you cannot judge by the forewarnings of God, what prosperity is. [43] Judge by the experience of all the world. Does it not leave them all in sorrow at last? Woe to the man that has his portion in this life! O miserable health, and wealth, and honor, which procures the death, and shame, and utter destruction of the soul! Was not he in as prosperous a case as you (Luke 16) who quickly cried out in vain for a drop of water to cool his tongue? There is none of you so senseless as not to know that you must die. And must you die? must you certainly die? and shall that day be no better prepared for? Shall present prosperity make you forget it, and live as if you must live here forever? Do you make so great a difference between that which is and that which will be as to make as great a matter of it as others do when it comes, and to make no more of it when it is but coming? O man, what is an inch of fleeting time? How quickly it is gone! You are going hence apace, and almost gone! Does God give you the mercy of a few days or years of health to make all your preparations in for eternity, and does this mercy turn to your deceit, and do you turn it so much contrary to the ends for which it was given you? Will you gorge yourself on mercy, and destroy your soul with it? The senses feel and perceive what now is, but you have reason to foresee what will be? Will you play in harvest, and forget the winter?

**Temptation 17.** Another great temptation to hinder conversion is the example and support of great ones that are ungodly. When landlords and men in power are pleasure-loving, and enemies to a holy life, and speak reproachfully of it, their inferiors, by the reverence which they bear to worldly wealth and greatness, are easily drawn to say as they do: also when men reputed learned and wise are of another mind: and especially when subtle enemies speak that reproach against it which they cannot answer.

**Direction 17.** To this I spoke at the end of the first part of this chapter. No man is so great and wise as God. See whether he says as they do in his word. The greatest that provoke him can no more save themselves from his vengeance than the poorest beggars. What work he made with a Pharaoh! and got himself a name by his hard-heartedness and impenitence! He can send worms to eat an arrogant Herod when the people acclaim him as a god! Where are now the Caesars and Alexanders of the world? The rulers and Pharisees did not believe in Christ (John 7:48); will you therefore not believe in him? The governor of the country condemned him to die; will you condemn him? "The kings of the earth form a united front; the rulers collaborate against the Lord and his anointed king. They say, 'Let's tear off the shackles they've put on us. Let's free ourselves from their ropes'" (Psalm 2:2–3); will you therefore join in the conspiracy? When "the one enthroned in heaven laughs in disgust; the Lord taunts them" ... "You will break them with an iron scepter; you will smash them like a potter's jar" ... "So now, you kings, do what is wise; you rulers of the earth, submit to correction. Serve the Lord in fear. Repent in terror. Give sincere homage. Otherwise he will be angry, and you will die because of your behavior, when his anger quickly ignites" (Psalm 2:4, 9–12). If your landlord, or great ones, shall be your god, and be honored and obeyed before God and against him, trust to them, and call on them in the hour of your distress, and take such a salvation as they can give you. Do not teach God what choice to make, and to whom to reveal his mysteries: he does not always choose the learned scribe, nor the mighty man. Christ himself says, "I praise you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because you have hidden these things from the wise and intelligent, and have revealed them to little children. Yes, Father, for this was your gracious will" (Matthew 11:25–26). If this reason does not satisfy you, follow them, and fare as they do. If they are greater and wiser than God, let them be your gods. [44] "Think about the circumstances of your call, brothers and sisters. Not many were wise by human standards, not many were powerful, not many were born to a privileged position. But God chose what the world thinks foolish to shame the wise, and God chose what the world thinks weak to shame the strong. God chose what is low and despised in the world, what is regarded as nothing, to set aside what is regarded as something" (1 Corinthians 1:26–28). It is another kind of greatness, honor, and wisdom which God bestows on the poorest saints than the world can give. Worldlings will shortly be weary of their portion. In your baptism you renounced the world with its pomps and vanity; and now do you deify what you then defied?

## Baxter's notes

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Psalm 2:12 (important):** Baxter quotes the King James, "kiss the Son, and serve the Lord with fear." The NET reads "Give sincere homage," because the Hebrew of this phrase is hard to translate and the NET chooses a general sense. Baxter, like most Christian readers of his time, takes it as a call to submit to Christ, the Son. The NET loses the explicit "Son." Flagged as another place where the translation matters to Baxter's argument.
- **Psalm 2:2–3, 4, 9–12:** the NET reads "form a united front" and "collaborate" for the King James "set themselves" and "take counsel together," "shackles" and "ropes" for "bands" and "cords," "laughs in disgust" for "shall laugh," and "iron scepter" for "rod of iron." In verse 3 the NET puts the rebels' words in plain modern speech ("Let's tear off the shackles…").
- **Matthew 11:25–26:** NET wording. Baxter's "hid these things from the wise and prudent" is "hidden these things from the wise and intelligent." His "babes" is "little children," and his verse 26 ("even so, Father; for so it seemeth good in thy sight") is "Yes, Father, for this was your gracious will."
- **1 Corinthians 1:26–28:** NET wording. Baxter's "not many wise men after the flesh, not many mighty, not many noble" is "not many were wise by human standards, not many were powerful, not many were born to a privileged position." "Base things of the world" is "what is low and despised." "To bring to nought things that are" is "to set aside what is regarded as something."
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "go into the sanctuary" (Psalm 73:17); "Woe to the man that has his portion in this life" (compare Psalm 17:14); the rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19–31, cited by chapter only); Pharaoh's hard-heartedness and God's name declared (Exodus 9:16; Romans 9:17); "worms to eat an arrogant Herod" (Acts 12:21–23); "the pomps and vanity of this wicked world," from the baptismal vow in the *Book of Common Prayer*.
- **security** (Temptation 16): in the older sense of careless confidence. **forewarnings:** Baxter's "fore-warnings."
- **countenance** (Temptation 17): Baxter's word for approval or support given by example. **great ones:** left as written. **pleasure-loving:** his "sensual."
- **acclaim him as a god:** Baxter's "cry him up as a god." **gorge yourself:** his "surfeit." **fleeting:** his "hasty." **apace:** quickly; kept in "going hence apace."
- **"The senses feel … what will be?"** (Direction 16): Baxter ends the sentence with a question mark; we kept it, though it reads as a rhetorical question.
- **"What work he made with a Pharaoh!"** (Direction 17): Baxter means what a display God made of Pharaoh, how God used his stubbornness to make his own name known. Left in his wording.
- **deify what you then defied:** Baxter's wordplay, kept. **honor … before God:** his "honoured and obeyed before God," meaning above God.
- **"first part of this chapter"** (Direction 17): Baxter's reference back to Chapter 1, Part 1, possibly to Direction 3, which makes a similar argument about the slanders of ungodly men. We have not confirmed which passage he means.
- **Mr. Bolton** (note 44): Robert Bolton, a Puritan preacher.
- **impenitence:** Baxter's "impenitency."
