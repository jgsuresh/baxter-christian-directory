# Chapter 4: Signs 26-28 of Pride, and Direction 3 (the Dreadful Consequences of Pride)

*This file begins in the middle of Direction 2 ("Observe the motions and discoveries of pride"), at Sign 26, and ends partway through Direction 3, at the third of its numbered points about the misery pride brings.*

**Sign 26.** Proud persons are ashamed and troubled if any necessity forces them to go lower in apparel, or provisions, or deportment, than others do of their degree; to show you that it is not as a duty that decency is regarded by them, but as the ornaments of pride, else they would be quiet when Providence makes it cease to be their duty. They are not so much ashamed of sin, and the neglect of God and their salvation, as they are to be seen in sordid attire, or in a poor and homely garb: beggars and servants show here that they are as proud as lords. What abundance of them go but seldom to church, and give this as a reason, I lacked clothes! as if they would neglect their souls, their God, their greatest duty, rather than do it in such clothes as they do their common work. Does Christ appoint you to meet him, that by his ministers he may instruct you for salvation, and that you may ask and receive the pardon of your sins; and will you disappoint him, and refuse to come, for lack of better clothes? Sure you do not think that these are the wedding garment which he requires you to bring. You would beg if you were naked or in rags, and will you not come to beg of God, because you have no better clothes? Do you set more value on the reputation of your clothes than the means of your salvation? How little do such wretches value God, and his mercy now, that will shortly on their death-beds cry for mercy, without any such regard for clothes! Naked they come into the world, and naked they must go out, and yet they will turn their backs on the worship of God, for lack of clothes. They are not ashamed nor afraid to be ungodly, and to forsake their duty, but they are ashamed of torn or poor attire. What, say they, shall we make ourselves ridiculous! When their pride and ungodliness are the cause of a thousand-fold more shame. We read of thousands, even of the poor, that crowded after Christ to hear him; but of none that stayed at home for lack of clothes; when it is likely they had no better than yours.

**Sign 27.** If a proud man is wronged, he looks for great submission before he will forgive: you must lie down at his feet, and make a very full confession, and behave yourself with great submission; especially if the law is in his hands. And he is prone to revenge, and cruel in his revenge: but if he has wronged others, he is brought with difficulty to confess that he wronged them; and with more difficulty to humble himself for reconciliation, and ask them forgiveness: when a humble person is ready to let go his right for peace, and easily forgives, and easily stoops to ask forgiveness.

**Sign 28.** Lastly; Pride makes men excessively desire to have an honorable memorial kept of their names when they are dead (if they are persons that rise to the hopes of such a remembrance;) Many a monument has pride erected; [228] many a book has it written to this end; many a good work materially has it done, and made it bad by such a base intention! Many a hospital, and almshouse, and school-house it has built; and many a pound has it given to charitable uses in pretense, but to proud and selfish uses in intention. Not that any should causelessly suspect another's ends, or blemish the deserved honor of good works, which it is lawful moderately to regard; but we should suspect our own hearts, and be careful of so horrible a sin, which would turn the most excellent parts and works into poison or corruption. And remember how heinous a thing it is, for a man to be laying proud designs, when he is turning to the dust, and going to appear before his Judge! yes, to set up the monuments of his pride over his rotten flesh and bones; and to show that he dies in so great a sin without repentance, by endeavoring that as much as may be of it may survive, when he is dead and gone! If such wicked ends do sometimes try to intrude into necessary, excellent works, an honest heart must abhor them, and cast them out, and beg forgiveness; and not for that reason stop his work, nor refuse the comfort of his more sincere desires and intents: but such good works do sink the hypocrite into hell, that are principally done as a service to pride, to leave a name on earth behind him.

Thus I have been long in showing you the signs of pride, because the discovery is a great part of the cure: not that every proud person has all these signs; for every one does not have the same temptations or occasions to show them; but every one has some, and many of these; and he that has any one of them, has a sign of pride. And again I say, that for all this, our reputation, as it serves the honor of God and our religion, and our brethren's good, must be carefully by all just means preserved, and by necessary defenses vindicated from slanderers; though we must quietly bear whatever infamy or slander we are tried with.

**Direction 3. Having understood the nature and the signs or effects of pride, consider next the dreadful consequences and tendency of it, both as it leads to further sin, and unto misery.** Which I shall briefly set out to you in some particulars.

1. At present it is the heart of the old man, and the root and life of all corruption, and of dreadful significance, if it is predominant. If any man's "heart is lifted up, the Lord will have no pleasure in him, or it is not upright in him" (Habakkuk 2:4). I would rather have my soul in the condition of an obscure humble Christian, that is taken notice of by few, or none but God, and is content to approve himself to him, than in the condition of the highest and most eminent and honorable in church or state, that looks for the observation and praise of men. [229] God does not judge men by their great abilities, and profession, and name; but justifies the humbled soul that is ashamed to lift up his face to heaven, and thinks himself unworthy to speak to God, or to have communion with his church, or to come among his servants; but standing afar off, strikes upon his breast, and says, (in true repentance,) O "God, be merciful to me, sinner that I am!" (Luke 18:13). Pride is as a plague-mark on the soul.

2. There is scarcely a sin to be thought of that is not a spawn in the bowels of pride. To give a few examples (besides all that are expressed in the signs): 1. It makes men hypocrites, and seem what they are not, for the praise of men. 2. It makes men liars: most of the lies that are told in the world, are to avoid some disgrace and shame, or to get men to think highly of them. When a sin is committed against God or your superiors, instead of humble confession, pride would cover it with a lie. 3. It causes greed, that they may not lack provision for their pride. 4. It makes men flatterers, and time-servers, and man-pleasers, that they may win the good esteem of others. 5. It makes men run into profaneness, and riotousness, to do as others do to avoid the shame of their reproach and scorn, that else would account them singular and precise. 6. It can take men off from any duty to God that the company is against; they dare not pray, nor speak a serious word of God, for fear of a jeer from a scorner's mouth. 7. It is so contentious a sin, that it makes men firebrands in the societies where they live; there is no quiet living with them longer than they have their own saying, will, and way; they must bear the sway, and not be crossed; and when all is done, there is no pleasing them, for the missing of a word, or a look, or a compliment, will catch on their hearts, as a spark on gunpowder. 8. It tears in pieces church and state. Where was ever civil war raised, or kingdom endangered or ruined, or church divided, oppressed, or persecuted, but pride was the great and evident cause? 9. It devours the mercies and good creatures of God, and sacrifices them to the devil. It is a costly sin: how much does it consume in clothes, and buildings, and attendance, and entertainments, and unnecessary things! 10. It is an odious thief and prodigal of precious time. How many hours that should be better employed, and must one day be accounted for, are cast away upon the aforesaid works of pride! especially in the needless compliments and visits of gallants, and the dressings of some vain, light-headed women, in which they spend almost half the day, and can scarcely find an hour in a morning for prayer, or meditation, or reading the Scriptures, because they cannot be ready; forgetting how they disgrace their wretched bodies, by telling men that they are so filthy or deformed, that they cannot be kept sweet and cleanly and seemly, without so long and much ado. 11. It is odiously unjust. A proud man makes no bones of any falsehood, slander, deceit, or cruelty, if it seems but necessary to his greatness, or honor, or preferment, or ambitious ends. He cares not who he wrongs or betrays, that he may rise to his desired height, or keep his greatness. Never trust a proud man further than his own interest bids you trust him. 12. Pride is the pander of whoredom and uncleanness: it is an incentive to lust in themselves, and draws the proud to adorn and set forth themselves in the most enticing manner, as tends to provoke the lust of others. Gladly they would be thought comely, that others may admire them, and be taken with their comeliness. If they thought that none would see them, they would spare their ornaments. And if a common decency were all that they desired, they would spare their curiosities and fashionable superfluities. Even they that would not be unclean in gross fornication with any, yet would be esteemed beautiful and desirable, and do that which tends to corrupt the minds of fools that see them. These, and indeed almost all sin, are the natural progeny of pride.

3. As to the misery which they bring on themselves and others, (1.) The greatest is, that they forsake God, and are in danger to be forsaken by him: for God abhors the proud, and beholds them as afar off. So far as you are proud you are hated by him, and have no acceptance or communion with him. Pride is the highway to utter apostasy. It blinds the mind; it makes men confident in their own notions; and venturesome upon any new opinion; and ready to quarrel with the word of God before they understand it. When anything seems hard to them, they at once suspect the truth of the matter, when they should suspect their dark, unfurnished minds. Mark those that are proud in any town, or any company of professors of piety; and if any infection of heresy or infidelity comes into that place, these are the men that will soonest catch it. Mark those that have turned from truth or godliness, and see whether they are not such as were proud and superficial in religion before. But God gives grace, and more grace to the humble: he dwells with them, and delights in them. [230]

(2.) A proud man is a tormentor of himself. Setting his mind on the thoughts of men, and desiring more of their esteem than he can attain, and that which is unsatisfying emptiness when he has obtained it, he is always under fruitless, vexatious desires, and frequent disappointments; everything that he sees, and every word almost that he hears, or every compliment omitted, can disturb his peace, and break his sleep, and cast him into a fever of passion or revenge. This wind that swells him, is running up and down, and disquieting him in every part. Who would have such a fire in his breast, that will not allow him to be quiet? [231]

(3.) Pride brings sufferings, and then makes them seem intolerable. It makes the sinner more vex and gall his mind, with striving and impatient aggravating his afflictions, than the suffering of itself would ever do.

## Baxter's notes

The Latin is reproduced as printed. A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[228] Anaxagoras (in Laert. p. 87.) Cum vidisset mausoli sepulchrum: monumentum, inquit, pretiosum et lapidibus ornatum, divitiarum imago. (English: Anaxagoras, in Laertius, p. 87. When he had seen the tomb of Mausolus, he said, "A costly monument, adorned with stones, an image of riches.")

[229] Æneas Sylvius in Boem. c. 65, speaking of the boasting of the monk Capistrinus, says, Superaverat seculi pompas, calcaverat avaritiam, libidinem subegerat, gloriam contemnere non potuit: nemo est tam sanctus qui dulcedine gloriæ non capiatur. Facilius regna viri excellentes, quam gloriam contemnunt. (English: He had overcome the pomps of the world, had trodden greed underfoot, had subdued lust; he could not despise glory: no one is so holy that he is not captured by the sweetness of glory. Excellent men more easily despise kingdoms than glory.) Inter omnia vitia tu semper es prima, semper es ultima: nam omne peccatum te accedente committitur, et te recedente dimittitur. Innocent. de Contemp. Mundi. l. 2. c. 31. (English: Among all the vices you are always first, always last: for every sin is committed when you approach, and let go when you withdraw. Innocent, *On Contempt of the World*, book 2, chapter 31. The second sentence is addressed to vainglory or pride; rendered loosely.)

[230] James 4:6; 1 Peter 5:5; Isaiah 57:15; Proverbs 16:19; 29:23.

[231] Vainglorious men are the scorn of wise men, the admiration of fools, the idols of flatterers, and the slaves of their own pride. Lord Bacon, Essay 54.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®, fetched from labs.bible.org; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Range begins mid-Direction:** the file opens at Sign 26 of Direction 2's list; Baxter's "Sign XXVI." and so on are rendered "Sign 26." Direction 3 ("_Direct._ III.") is rendered in bold; the bold covers its first sentence, as Baxter's "Which I shall briefly open..." is a continuation.
- **Habakkuk 2:4 (Baxter's blend, not the NET):** Baxter quotes "heart be lifted up, the Lord will have no pleasure in him, or it is not upright in him." This is his own rendering, apparently built on the King James ("his soul which is lifted up is not upright in him") with a "no pleasure" clause (compare Hebrews 10:38, not confirmed as his source); it is not the NET wording. We kept his words, modernized. NET: "Look, the one whose desires are not upright will faint from exhaustion, but the person of integrity will live because of his faithfulness." The NET reads the verse as about "desires" that are not upright, not a lifted-up heart, so it does not carry Baxter's point about pride directly.
- **Luke 18:13:** NET wording: "God, be merciful to me, sinner that I am!" Baxter (King James style) has "God, be merciful to me, a sinner." The sense is the same. Baxter's "standing afar off, smiteth upon his breast" is an unmarked allusion to the same verse; NET has "stood far off ... beat his breast."
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "wedding garment" (Matthew 22:11-12); "Naked they come into the world, and naked they must go out" (compare Job 1:21; 1 Timothy 6:7); "the old man" (compare Romans 6:6; Ephesians 4:22); "God gives grace, and more grace to the humble" (James 4:6, listed in note 230; NET: "he gives greater grace" and "God opposes the proud, but he gives grace to the humble").
- **Note 230 (Scripture list):** converted to modern form and not otherwise checked against Baxter's wording; none is quoted in the body.
- **Word swaps (Baxter's word, then ours):** wanted (lacked); want (lack); give him the meeting (meet him); set by (value, with the word order adjusted in "set more by" to "set more value on"); regard of (regard for); is like (is likely); staid (stayed); hardly brought... more hardly (brought with difficulty... with more difficulty); inordinately (excessively); ordinately (moderately); take heed (be careful); excellentest (most excellent); yea (yes); offer to intrude (try to intrude); forbear (stop); subserveth (serves); calumniators (slanderers); consequents (consequences); open (set out); signification (significance); in the case of (in the condition of); parts (abilities); to instance in (to give a few examples); covetousness (greed); chargeable (costly); what a deal (how much); foresaid (aforesaid); fain (gladly); affected (desired); venturous (venturesome); presently (at once); vanity (emptiness); still (always); suffer (allow); pretension (pretense).
- **Terms kept as Baxter's:** *materially* (Sign 28; "has done a good work materially" means a work good in its outward substance, whatever the motive); *predominant* (ruling in the heart; compare *reigning sin*); *professors of piety* (people who openly profess religion); *precise* and *singular* (points 2.5; "precise" was a common term of mockery for strict Puritans, and "singular" means standing out from the crowd); *firebrands* (troublemakers); *gallants* (fashionable men of the court or town); *vain* (point 2.10, "vain, light-headed women": conceited, not only empty); *curiosities* (elaborate refinements of dress); *infidelity* (point 3.(1), disbelief in the faith); *pander* (go-between who serves another's lust).
- **Punctuation and syntax oddities:** "Lastly; Pride..." keeps Baxter's semicolon. In Sign 28 the sentence ending "such a remembrance;)" is followed by "Many a monument..." with no period in the source; kept. "Sure you do not think..." is Baxter's. The second clause of point 3.(3), "more vex and gall his mind, with striving and impatient aggravating his afflictions," is garbled in the source and is kept as written ("more vex and gall" is probably "vex and gall his mind more"; "striving and impatient" modifies the "aggravating"). "Where was ever civil war raised..." is a question; kept.
- **Sign 26 and "Providence":** Baxter's "Providence" is God's ordering of events, as elsewhere.
- **Sign 28, "pound":** the English currency unit; "charitable uses" are gifts to charity.
- **Anaxagoras / Laertius (note 228):** Baxter's "Laert." probably means Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of the Philosophers*; we have not confirmed this, nor the page. We make no claim about the historical setting of the saying, and the translation assumes Baxter's "mausoli" refers to the tomb of Mausolus.
- **Æneas Sylvius; Capistrinus (note 229):** "Æneas Sylvius in Boem." is probably Æneas Sylvius Piccolomini's history of Bohemia, and "Capistrinus" the Franciscan John of Capistrano; both identifications are probable and not confirmed by us. **Innocent, *De Contemptu Mundi*:** probably the work attributed to Pope Innocent III (we have not confirmed it). The English translations are drafts.
- **Lord Bacon, Essay 54 (note 231):** Baxter's reference to Francis Bacon's *Essays* (probably "Of Vainglory"); we have not confirmed the number or the wording against an edition.
