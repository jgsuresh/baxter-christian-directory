# Chapter 8: Part 4, Title 1 (against gluttony): the last aggravations, and Directions 1–7

*This section begins partway through section III, "The Greatness of the Sin of Gluttony," at item 18 of Baxter's numbered list.*

18. Those are the worst sins that have least repentance; but gluttony is so far from being truly repented of by the self-indulgent epicure, that he loves it, and cares and contrives how to commit it, and buys it with the price of much of his estate.

19. It is the greater sin, because it is so frequently committed; men live in it as their daily practice and delight; they live for it, and make it the end of other sins: it is not a sin that they seldom fall into, but it is almost as familiar with them as to eat and drink: being turned into beasts, they live like beasts continually.

20. Lastly, it is a spreading sin, and therefore has become common, even the sin of countries, of rich and poor; for both sorts love their bellies, though both do not have the like provision for them. And they are so far from taking warning from one another, that they are encouraged by one another; and the sin is scarcely noticed in one of a hundred that daily lives in it: nor are there almost any that reprove it, or help one another against it (unless by impoverishing each other), but most by persuasions and examples encourage it (though some much more than others): so that by this time you may see that it is no rare, nor venial, little sin.

And now you may see also that it is no wonder if no one of the commandments expressly forbids this sin (not only because it is a sin against ourselves directly, but also) because it is also against every one of the commandments. And do not think that either riches or poverty will excuse it, when even princes are restrained so much as from unseasonable eating (Ecclesiastes 10:16). If it was one of the great sins that Sodom was burned with fire for, judge whether England is in no danger by it. Read, O England, and know yourself, and tremble: "See here—this was the iniquity of your sister Sodom: She and her daughters had majesty, abundance of food, and enjoyed carefree ease, but they did not help the poor and needy" (Ezekiel 16:49).

**IV. The Directions or Helps against It.**

**Direction 1. Mortify the flesh, according to the directions in Chapter 4, Part 7.**

Subdue its inclinations and desires; and learn to esteem and use it only as a servant. Think what a pitiful price a little gluttonous pleasure of the throat is, for a man to sell his God and his salvation for. [416] Learn to be indifferent whether your food is pleasing to your appetite or not; and make no great matter of it: remember always what an odious, swinish, damning sin it is for a man's heart to be set upon his belly. "Those who belong to Christ have crucified the flesh with its passions and desires" (Galatians 5:24).

**Direction 2. Live faithfully to God, and upon spiritual, durable delights.**

And then you will fetch the measure of your eating and drinking from their tendency to that higher end. [417] There is no using any inferior thing rightly until you have first well resolved on your end, and use it as a means to it, and mark how far it is a means.

**Direction 3. See all your food as provided and given you by God, and beg it and the blessing of it at his hand, and then it will much restrain you from using it against him.**

He is a wretch indeed that will take his food as from his father's hand, and throw it in his face, though perhaps a petulant child would do so to a fellow-servant: he that thinks he is most indebted to himself for his plenty will say as the fool, "Soul, take your ease, eat, drink, and be merry, you have enough laid up for many years" (Luke 12:19–20). But he that perceives it is the hand of God that hands it to him will use it more reverently. It is a horrid aggravation of the gluttony of this age that they play the hypocrites in it, and first (for custom) ask God's blessing on their food, and then sit down and sin against him with it: such are the prayers of hypocritical pleasure-seekers. But a serious discerning of God as the giver would teach you "whether you eat or drink to do all to his glory" from whom it comes. [418]

**Direction 4. See by faith the blood of Christ as the purchasing cause of all you have;**

and then surely you will bear more reverence to his blood than to cast the fruit of it into the sewer of indulgence in physical pleasure, and to do worse than throw it upon the dunghill. What! must Christ be a sacrifice to God, and die to recover for you the mercies which you had forfeited, and now will you cast them to the dogs? and please a sinful appetite with them? Did he die to purchase you provisions for your lusts, and to serve the flesh with?

**Direction 5. Do not forget how the first sin came into the world, even by eating the forbidden fruit.**

And let the slain creatures whose lives are lost for you remind you of that sin which brought the burden on them for your sakes. And then every piece of flesh that you see will appear to you as with this caution written upon it: O do not sin as your first parents sinned by pleasing your appetite; for this our death, and your devouring the flesh of your fellow-creatures, is the fruit of that sin, and warns you to be temperate. Do not revel to excess in your fellow-creatures' lives.

**Direction 6. Keep an obedient, tender conscience, not scrupulously perplexing yourselves about every bit you eat (as melancholy persons do), but checking your appetite, and telling you of God's commands, and teaching you to fear all physical excess.**

It is a graceless, disobedient, senseless heart that makes men so boldly obey their appetite; when the fear of God is not in their hearts, no wonder if they are "feasting without reverence, feeding only themselves" (Jude 12). Either they make a small matter of sin in general, or at least of this in particular: it is usually the same persons that do not fear to spend their time in idleness, amusements, or empty pursuits, and to live in worldliness or fleshly lusts, who live in gluttony to feed all this. The belly is a brute that does not much trouble itself about reason: where conscience is asleep and seared, reason and Scripture do little to move a pleasure-loving belly-god; and anything will serve instead of reason to prove it lawful, and to answer all that is said against it. There is no disputing the case with a man that is asleep; especially if his guts and appetite are awake: you may almost as well bring reason and Scripture to keep a swine from over-eating, or persuade a hungry dog from a bone, as to draw a glutton away from the pleasing of his throat, if he has once grown dull-witted, and has mastered his conscience by unbelief, or stilled it with a stupefying opiate. His taste then serves instead of reason, and against reason; then he says, I feel it does me good (that is, he feels that it pleases his appetite, as a swine feels that his food does him good when he is ready to burst); and this answers all that can be said against it. Then he can sacrifice his time and treasure to his belly, and make a jest of the abstinence and temperance of sober men, as if it were only a needless self-affliction, or fit only for some weak and sickly persons. If the constant fear and obedience of God do not rule the soul, the appetite will be unruled; and if a tender conscience is not doorkeeper, the throat will be open to anything that the appetite requires. One sight of heaven or hell, to awaken their reason and sleepy consciences, would be the best remedy to convince them of the odiousness and danger of this sin.

**Direction 7. Understand well what is most conducive to your health; and let that be the ordinary measure of your diet for quantity, and quality, and time. [419]**

Surely your nature itself, if you are yet men, should have nothing to say against this measure, and consequently against all the rest of the directions which assume it: nature has given you reason as well as appetite, and reason tells you that your health is more to be regarded than your appetite. I hope you will not say that God is too strict with you, or would diet you too hard, as long as he allows you (ordinarily) to choose that (when you can have it lawfully) which is most for your own health, and forbids you nothing but that which hurts you. What heathen or unbeliever that is not either mad or swinish will not allow this measure and choice, as well as Christians? Yes, if you do not believe in a life to come, I think you should be loath to shorten this life which now you have. God would only keep you from hurting yourselves by your excess, as you would keep your children or your swine. Though he has a further end in it, and so must you, namely, that a healthy body may be serviceable to a holy soul in your Master's work; yet it is the health of your bodies which is to be your nearest and immediate end and measure.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). All NET wording was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Where this section sits:** Chapter 8, Part 4 is on the taste and appetite. Within Parts 4 and 5 Baxter has divisions headed "Tit. 1." and "Tit. 2."; "Tit." is his abbreviation for "Title," that is, a heading or division. This section belongs to Title 1 (against gluttony). It opens at item 18 of section III (the greatness of the sin of gluttony), and then begins section IV, the Directions against it; the next file continues the Directions.
- **Ezekiel 16:49:** NET wording. The King James has "pride, fulness of bread, and abundance of idleness ... neither did she strengthen the hand of the poor and needy." The NET's "abundance of food" keeps the point Baxter leans on (Sodom's overfeeding), but it renders "pride" as "majesty" and "abundance of idleness" as "enjoyed carefree ease," so the NET does not name pride and idleness as plainly as the text Baxter used.
- **Galatians 5:24:** NET wording, without its opening "Now" (Baxter likewise leaves off the King James "And"). The King James has "affections and lusts" where the NET has "passions and desires."
- **Luke 12:19–20:** Baxter's quotation is a loose rearrangement of the King James verse 19 ("Soul, thou hast much goods laid up for many years; take thine ease, eat, drink, and be merry"), so we keep his wording, modernized. The NET reads: "And I will say to myself, 'You have plenty of goods stored up for many years; relax, eat, drink, celebrate!'" Verse 20 (God calls the man a fool) is cited by Baxter but not quoted.
- **1 Corinthians 10:31** (Direction 3, and note 418): Baxter works the verse into his own sentence, so we keep his wording. The NET reads: "So whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God."
- **Jude 12:** NET wording ("feasting without reverence, feeding only themselves"); we add "are" so the phrase fits Baxter's sentence. Baxter quoted the King James fragments "feed" and "feast themselves without fear," and his point is the absence of the fear of God. The NET's "without reverence" keeps that point, but its "feeding only themselves" (shepherds who care only for themselves) is a different picture from the King James "feeding themselves."
- **Ecclesiastes 10:16:** cited, not quoted. Baxter's "unseasonable eating" refers to princes who eat at the wrong time; the NET reads "your princes feast in the morning."
- **Hebrews 13:9** (note 417): cited only. It speaks of the heart being strengthened by grace rather than by foods.
- **Chapter 4, Part 7** (Direction 1): Baxter's "chap. iv. part vii.," a cross-reference to his directions on mortifying the flesh. We have not checked the cross-reference.
- **epicure:** kept. In Baxter it means one devoted to the pleasures of eating and drinking, a glutton. **self-indulgent:** Baxter's "luxurious," which in his day meant given to excess and self-indulgence, not costly comfort.
- **venial** (item 20): kept as Baxter's technical term: a lesser, pardonable sin, as opposed to a mortal one.
- **noticed:** Baxter's "noted." **food:** his "meat" (food in general), in Directions 1, 3, and 6. **always:** his "still" (Direction 1). **rightly:** his "aright." **resolved on:** his "resolved of." **indebted:** his "beholden." **hands it to him:** his "reacheth it to him." **ask:** his "crave" (beg). **pleasure-seekers:** his "sensualists." **remind you:** his "remember you." **physical excess:** his "sensual excess." **amusements; empty pursuits:** his "sports; vanity." **does not much trouble itself about reason:** his "sticks not much upon reason" (makes little difficulty over reason). **draw ... away from:** his "take off ... from." **dull-witted:** his "blockish." **doorkeeper:** his "porter." **open to anything:** his "common for any thing." **assume:** his "suppose" (Direction 7). **unbeliever:** his "infidel." **I think:** his "methinks." **healthy:** his "healthful." **only:** his "but."
- **sewer** (Direction 4): Baxter's "sink," a cesspool or drain for filth. **indulgence in physical pleasure:** his "sensuality."
- **flesh** (Direction 5): here Baxter means the meat of slain animals, while keeping in view the "flesh" of Direction 1; we keep the word.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): Sodom burned with fire (Genesis 19:24); the first sin by eating the forbidden fruit (Genesis 3:6); death and the eating of animals after the fall and the flood (compare Genesis 9:3); "belly-god" (compare Philippians 3:19, "their god is the belly" in the King James); conscience "seared" (compare 1 Timothy 4:2); "cast them to the dogs" (compare Matthew 7:6).
- **Plato** (note 416) and **Diogenes Laertius** (note 419): Baxter gives no exact source for the Plato saying, and we have not traced it. "Laertius in Socrat." probably refers to Diogenes Laertius's life of Socrates in his *Lives of the Philosophers*; we have not checked the passage.
- **Direction 4** has no full stop after its opening directive in the source (it runs on with "and then sure you will..."); we bold the directive clause and continue the sentence in the next paragraph, as the finished files do with Direction 14 of Part 1.
