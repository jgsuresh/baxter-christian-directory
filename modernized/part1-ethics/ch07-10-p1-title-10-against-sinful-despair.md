# Chapter 7: Title 10, Directions against sinful despair (introduction, Directions 1-5, Objections 1-2)

**Title 10. Directions against sinful despair (and doubting).**

Despair is the contrary to hope. [365] There is a despair that is a duty, and a despair that is a sin, and a despair that is indifferent, as being only of natural and not of moral kind. Despair is a duty when it is contrary to the sinful hope described before; that is, 1. When we despair of anything which God has told us shall never come to pass: for we are bound to believe his word: as that all the world should be saved or converted, or that our bodies should not die and perish, and many such things. 2. It is a duty to despair of ever attaining a good end by means, or upon terms, which God has told us it shall never be attained by: and so it is a great duty for an unregenerate person to despair of ever being saved without regeneration, conversion, and holiness; and to despair of ever being pardoned or saved, if he lives according to the flesh and does not have the Spirit of Christ, and does not repent sincerely of his sin, and is not a new creature, and does not crucify the flesh with its affections and lusts. [366] Such a despair is one of the first things necessary to the conversion of a sinner, because the false hopes of being pardoned and saved without regeneration are the present hindrance to be removed.

Despair is a sin when it is contrary to any hope which God commands us (provided it is not only a negative despair, or bare not-hoping, which in sleep and other times may be innocent, but a positive despair, which concludes against hope). As, 1. Particular despair of the benefit of some particular promise; as if Israel had despaired of deliverance from Egypt, or Abraham of a son. 2. General despair of the fulfilling of some general promise; as if we despaired of the resurrection, or the kingdom of Christ in glory. 3. When by misapplication we despair of that pardon and salvation to ourselves, which yet we believe shall be to others.

Yes, despair is sinful sometimes when it is not contrary to any promise or commanded hope: for if God has not revealed his will one way or other, it is no duty to expect the thing, and yet it is a sin to conclude positively that it will not be: for then we shall say more than we know, or than God has revealed. If hope is taken for the comfort that arises in us from the perception of a mere possibility, then indeed it is a duty to hope for that good which is possible only; but if hope is taken for a confident expectation, then both such hope, and also the contrary despair, would be a sin. We may (so) *non-sperare* but not *desperare*. Possibilities must be taken only for possibilities, yet still for such.

He that despairs only of some common mercy which he should not despair of, *ratione materiæ* commits a sin of the smaller sort: he that despairs of a great mercy to others (though not promised) commits a greater sin *ratione materiæ*; as if you despair of the conversion of a bad child, or of the continuance of the gospel to the kingdom, etc. But he that despairs of his own pardon and salvation sins more perilously *ratione materiæ*.

The despairing of pardon and salvation upon a despair of the truth of the gospel, or sufficiency of Christ, is damnable, and a certain mark of a wretched unbeliever, if it is predominant. [367] But to believe all the gospel to be true, and desire Christ and life as best, and yet to despair upon too bad thoughts of oneself, or through some other mistake, this is a sin of infirmity, consistent with grace (unless the despair is so total and powerful as to make the sinner in a settled way cast off a godly life, and give himself up to a life of wickedness). The Scripture speaks little of this humble sort of despair, and nowhere threatens it as it does unbelief.

The commonest despair (like Spira's) which comes immediately from invincible predominant melancholy (though occasioned first by sin) is no more sinful or dangerous than the despair or raving of a madman, or one in a delirious fever, is. It is the too humble despair, through personal misapplication, and particular mistakes, that I shall speak of in this place.

**Direction 1. Beware of being ignorant of, or misunderstanding, the three great general grounds of faith and hope; that is, 1. The infinite goodness of God, and his unmeasurable love and mercy. 2. The relation of Christ's office to all, and the sufficiency of his ransom and sacrifice for all. 3. The universality of the promise, or the amnesty, or deed of gift of free pardon and salvation to all on condition of penitent belief and acceptance, which is procured and given by Christ, and contained in the gospel.**

If you are so mistaken about any one of these as not to believe or understand them, or if you do not well consider and make use of them, no wonder if you are left under continual doubts and liable to despair.

**Direction 2. Understand well the true nature of the condition of this universal promise; how much it consists in the will or acceptance of Christ and life as offered by the gospel; or in our hearty consent to the baptismal covenant, that God be our God and Father, our Savior and Sanctifier: and that in God's account the will is the man, and he is a true believer, and has part in Christ, that is truly willing to have him to the ends of his office: and that he has right to all the benefits of the covenant of grace who heartily consents to it. [368]**

This is true faith; this is the condition of pardon; and on these terms Christ and life are given. This is the infallible evidence of a state of grace. If you do not know this, but look for something else as necessary which is separable from this, no wonder you are perplexed, and inclined to despair.

**Direction 3. Understand the extent of this pardoning covenant as to the sins which it pardons: that it contains the forgiveness of all sin without exception, to them that perform the condition of it (that is, to consenters).**

So that directly no sin is excepted but the non-performance of the condition; but consequently, all sin is excepted and none at all forgiven by it, to them that do not perform the condition. Every conditional grant expressly excepts the non-performance of the condition by the making of it to be the condition. He that says, All sin is forgiven to them that believe and repent, and no other, plainly implies that not believing and not repenting are not forgiven, while they continue; nor any other sin to such. But to penitent believers or consenters, all sin is pardoned. Which made the ancients say that all sin is washed away in baptism, supposing the person baptized to be a fit subject, and to have the condition of the covenant which is by baptism sealed and delivered to him.

**Direction 4. Do not misunderstand the excepted sin against the Holy Spirit; which is no other than an aggravated non-performance and refusal of the condition of the covenant; namely, when unbelievers are so obstinate in their unbelief that they will rather impute the miracles of the Holy Spirit to the devil than they will be convinced by them that Christ is the true Messiah or Savior. [369]**

This is the true nature of the sin against the Holy Spirit, of which I have written the third part of my *Treatise of Infidelity*. So that no one has the sin against the Holy Spirit that confesses that Jesus is the Christ and Savior; or that confesses that the miracles done by Christ, and his apostles, were done by the Holy Spirit; or that confesses the gospel is true; or that does not justify his sin and unbelief. He must be a professed unbeliever against confessed miracles that commits this sin. And yet many despair because they fear they have committed this sin, that never understood what it is, nor have any reason but bare fear, and some blasphemous temptations which they abhor, to make them imagine that this sin is theirs. But the truth is, in their fearing condition, if any other sin had been as terribly spoken of, they would have thought it was theirs.

**Direction 5. Understand the time to which the condition of the gospel extends, namely, to the end of our life on earth: the day of his grace has no shorter end.**

For the gospel does not say, He that believes this year or the next shall be saved; but absolutely, without limitation to any time short of death, He that believes shall be saved. So that to doubt whether true repentance and faith will be accepted at any time before death is but to be ignorant of the gospel, or to doubt whether it is true. And therefore for a despairing soul to say, If I did repent, it is too late, because the day of grace is past, is but to contradict the gospel covenant itself, or to say he knows not what. God never refused a soul that truly repented and believed before death.

*Objection 1.* But (they will say) do not some theologians say that some men's day of grace is sooner past, and God has forsaken them, and it is too late, because they did not come in time.

*In the margin: When the day of grace is past.*

*Answer.* They that understand what they say must say only this: that this word, "the day of grace," has various senses. 1. Properly by the day of grace is meant the time in which, according to the tenor of the gospel, God will pardon and accept those that repent; and in this sense the time of life is the time of grace; whenever a sinner repents and is converted, he is pardoned. 2. Sometimes by the day of grace is meant the time in which the means of grace is continued to a nation or a person. And thus it is true that the day of grace is sooner past with some countries than others: that is, God sometimes takes away the preachers of his gospel from a people that reject them, and so by preaching offers them his grace no more. But in this sense a man may easily know whether his day of grace is past or not; that is, whether Bibles, and books, and Christians, and preachers are all gone, or not. (And yet if they were, he that receives Christ before they are gone is safe.) No man in his right mind can think this day of grace is past with him while Christ is offered him, or while there is a Bible, or preacher, or Christians about him. 3. Sometimes by the day of grace is meant the certain time which we are sure of as our own. And so it is only the present minute that is the time of grace; that is, we cannot beforehand be sure of another minute; but yet the next minute when it has come is as much the time of grace as the former was. 4. Sometimes by the day of grace is meant the time in which God actually works and gives grace; and that is no more than the day of our conversion. And in this sense, to have the day of grace past is a happiness and comfort; that is, that the day is past in which we were converted. 5. And sometimes by the day of grace is meant that day in which God moves the hearts of the impenitent more strongly towards conversion than he formerly did. And this is what theologians mean when they talk of the day of grace being past with men before their death; that is, though such never have a day of effectual grace, yet their stirrings were stronger towards it than hereafter they shall be, and they were in a fairer way for conversion than afterward when they have gone further from it. This is true, and this is all: and what is this to a soul that is willing to come in, and ignorantly questions whether he shall be accepted, because the day of grace is past?

*Objection 2.* But Christ says, "If you had only known on this day—" (Luke 19:42).

*Answer.* That was the day of the offers of grace by preaching: we grant that nations have only their day of enjoying the gospel, which they may shorten by sinning it away.

- **Title:** "Tit." is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," his name for each of the ten main divisions (headings) of Chapter 7 ("Directions for the Government of the Passions"). This file covers Title 10 from its heading through Direction 5 and the first two objections that follow it; the next file continues with Objection 3.
- **Direction layout:** in the source each Direction's opening runs straight into the rest of its paragraph. Following the project's format, the Direction's opening sentence is set as the bold line and the following sentences form the body. In Direction 1 the opening sentence includes Baxter's numbered list of the three grounds; in Directions 2 and 4 it runs to the footnote marker. No words were moved or dropped.
- **Luke 19:42 (NET flag, minor):** Baxter quotes only the opening of the verse, in the King James form "If thou hadst known in this thy day--" (the King James reads "If thou hadst known, even thou, at least in this thy day"). The NET reads "If you had only known on this day, even you, the things that make for peace!" We give the NET for the same extent, stopping at "on this day" to keep Baxter's fragment and his dash, and so leave out "even you," which in the NET follows "on this day." The King James "thy day" makes the day Jerusalem's own, which suits Baxter's answer (a nation's "day" of the gospel); the NET "on this day" loses the possessive "thy" but still carries the word "day" he relies on, so the argument stands.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "live after the flesh," "have not the Spirit of Christ," "new creature," "crucify the flesh with its affections and lusts" (Romans 8:9, 13; 2 Corinthians 5:17; Galatians 5:24, all in his note 366).
