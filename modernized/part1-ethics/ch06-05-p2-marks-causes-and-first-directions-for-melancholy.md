# Chapter 6: Title 5 continued: the last marks of melancholy, its causes, and Directions 1-4

*This section begins in the middle of Baxter's numbered list of the marks of melancholy under Title 5 (directions to the melancholy), with the last words of item 25; the previous file carries the start of that sentence.*

... they are thinking. 26. When melancholy grows strong, they are almost always troubled with hideous, blasphemous temptations, against God, or Christ, or the Scripture, and against the immortality of the soul; which comes partly from their own fears, which make them think most (against their will) of that which they are most afraid of thinking: as the spirits and blood will rush to the part that is hurt. The very pain of their fears draws their thoughts to what they fear. As he that is over-desirous to sleep, and afraid that he shall not sleep, is sure to stay awake, because his fears and desires keep him waking: so do the fears and desires of the melancholy thwart themselves. And besides, the malice of the devil plainly intervenes here, and takes advantage of this disease, to tempt and trouble them, and to show his hatred to God, and Christ, and Scripture, and to them. For as he can much more easily tempt a choleric person to anger, than another, and a phlegmatic, fleshly person to sloth, and a sanguine or hot-tempered person to lust, and wantonness; so also a melancholy person to thoughts of blasphemy, unbelief, and despair. And often they feel a violent urgency, as if something within them urged them to speak such or such a blasphemous or foolish word; and they can have no rest unless they yield in this, and other such cases, to what they are urged to. And some are ready to yield in a temptation to be quiet: and when they have done it, they are tempted utterly to despair because they have committed so great a sin: and when the devil has got this advantage of them, he is still setting it before them. 27. As a result they are further tempted to think they have committed the sin against the Holy Spirit; not understanding what that sin is, but fearing it is theirs, because it is a fearful sin: at least they think they shall not be forgiven; not considering that a temptation is one thing, and a sin another; and that no man has less cause to fear being condemned for his sin, than he that is least willing toward it, and most hates it. And no man can be less willing toward any sin, than these poor souls are toward the hideous, blasphemous thoughts which they complain of. 28. As a result some of them come to think that they are possessed by devils: and if it but enters their imagination how possessed persons used to act, the very strength of imagination will make them do so too: so that I have known those that would swear, and curse, and blaspheme, and imitate an inward alien voice, thinking themselves that it was the devil in them that did all this. But these that go so far are but few. 29. Some of them that are near madness truly think that they hear voices, and see lights and apparitions, that the curtains are opened on them, that something meets them, and says this or that to them, when all is but the error of a disordered brain and sick imagination. 30. Many of them are weary of their lives, through the constant, tiring perplexities of their minds; and yet afraid of dying: some of them resolutely starve themselves: some are strongly tempted to murder themselves, and they are haunted with the temptation so restlessly, that they can go nowhere but they feel as if something within them pushed them on, and said, Do it, do it; so that many poor creatures yield, and take their own lives. 31. Many of them are restlessly troubled with fears of want, and poverty, and misery to their families; and of imprisonment or banishment; and that somebody will kill them; and everyone that they see whisper, they think is plotting to take away their lives. 32. Some of them lay a law upon themselves that they will not speak, and so live long in resolute silence. 33. All of them are unmanageable, and stiff in their own notions, and hardly persuaded out of them, however irrational they are. 34. Few of them are the better for any reason, conviction, or counsel that is given them: if it seems to satisfy, and quiet, and gladden them at the present, tomorrow they are as bad again: it being the nature of their disease, to think as they do think; and their thoughts are not cured while the disease is uncured. 35. Yet in all this disorder, few of them will believe that they are melancholy; but hate to hear people tell them so, and say it is but the rational sense of their unhappiness, and the forsakings and heavy wrath of God. And therefore they are hardly persuaded to take any medicine or use any means for the cure of their bodies, saying that they are well, and being confident that it is only their souls that are distressed.

This is the miserable case of these poor people, greatly to be pitied, and not to be despised by any. I have spoken nothing but what I have often seen and known. And let none despise such, for men of all sorts do fall into this misery; learned and unlearned, high and low, good and bad, yes, some that have lived in greatest merriment and indulgence in physical pleasure, when God has made them feel their folly.

The causes of it are, 1. Most commonly some worldly loss, or hardship, or grief, or care, which made too deep an impression on them. 2. Sometimes excess of fear upon any common occasion of danger. 3. Sometimes over-hard and unbroken studies, or thoughts, which screw up and rack the imagination too much. 4. Sometimes too deep fears, or too constant, and serious, and emotional thoughts and cares about the danger of the soul. 5. The great predisposing causes of it (which are indeed the principal cause) are a weak head, and reason, joined with strong passion, which are most often found in women, and those to whom it is natural. 6. And in some it is brought in by some heinous sin, the sight of which they cannot bear, when conscience is but once awakened.

When this disease has gone very far, directions to the persons themselves are useless, because they have not reason and free will to practice them; but it is their friends about them that must have the directions. But because with most of them, and at first, there is some power of reason left, I give directions for the use of such.

**Direction 1. See that no error in religion is the cause of your distress: especially understand well the covenant of grace, and the riches of mercy shown in Christ.**

Among others, it will be useful to you to understand these following truths.

*In the margin: Special truths to be known for preventing causeless troubles.*

1. That our thoughts of the infinite goodness of God should bear proportion with our thoughts concerning his infinite power and wisdom.

2. That the mercy of God has provided for all mankind so sufficient a Savior, that no sinner shall perish for lack of a sufficient satisfaction made for his sins by Christ, nor is it made the condition of any man's salvation or pardon, that he satisfy for his own sins.

3. That Christ has in his gospel covenant (which is an amnesty) made himself over with pardon and salvation, to all that will penitently and believingly accept the offer. And that none perish that hear the gospel, but the final, obstinate refusers of Christ and life.

4. That he that so far believes the truth of the gospel, as to consent to the covenant of grace, namely that God the Father be his Lord and reconciled Father, and Christ his Savior, and the Holy Spirit his Sanctifier, has true, saving faith, and right to the blessing of the covenant.

5. That the day of grace is so far commensurate or equal to our lifetime, that whoever truly repents and consents to the covenant of grace, before his death, is certainly pardoned, and in a state of life: and that it is every man's duty so to do, that pardon may be theirs.

6. That Satan's temptations are none of our sins, but only our yielding to them.

7. That the effects of natural sickness or diseasedness are not (in themselves) sins.

8. That those are the smallest sins (formally) and least likely to condemn us, which we are most unwilling toward, and are least in love or liking of.

9. That no sin shall condemn us which we hate more than love, and which we would rather leave and be delivered from than keep: for this is true repentance.

10. That he is truly sanctified who would rather be perfect in holiness of heart and life, in loving God, and living by faith, than to have the greatest pleasures, riches, or honors of the world; taking in the means also by which both are attained.

11. That he who has this grace and desire may know that he is elect; and the making of our calling sure by our consenting to the holy covenant is the making of our election sure.

12. That the same thing which is a great duty to others may be no duty to one who by bodily disorder (such as fevers, frenzies, melancholy) is unable to perform it.

**Direction 2. Beware of worldly cares, and sorrows, and discontents.**

Do not set so much store by earthly things, as to enable them to disquiet you; but learn to cast your cares on God. You can have less peace in any affliction which comes by such a carnal, sinful means. It is much more safe to be distracted with cares for heaven than for earth.

**Direction 3. Meditation is no duty at all for a melancholy person, except some few that are able to bear a diverting meditation, which must be of something furthest from the matter which troubles them; or except it is short meditations like arrow prayers.**

A set and serious meditation will but confound you, and disturb you, and disable you for other duties. If a man has a broken leg, he must not walk on it till it is knit, lest all the body fare the worse. It is your thinking faculty, or your imagination, which is the broken, pained part; and therefore you must not use it about the things that trouble you. Perhaps you will say, That this is to be profane, and forget God and your soul, and let the tempter have his will. But I answer, No; it is but to refrain from that which you cannot do at present, that by doing other things which you can do, you may come again to do this which you now cannot do: it is but to refrain from attempting that which will but make you less able to do all other duties. And at the present, you may conduct the affairs of your soul by holy reason. I persuade you not from repenting or believing, but from set, and long, and deep meditations, which will but hurt you.

**Direction 4. Do not be too long in any secret duty which you find you are not able to bear.**

Prayer itself, when you are unable, must be performed only as you can; short confessions and requests to God must serve instead of longer secret prayers, when you are unable to do more. If sickness may excuse a man for being short, where nature will not hold out, the case is the same here, in the sickness of the brain and spirits. God has appointed no means to do you hurt.

## Translator's notes

- **Where this file sits:** Chapter 6 is "Directions for the Government of the Thoughts." Baxter divides it into six "Titles" (his abbreviation *Tit.*), meaning headings or divisions. Title 5 is addressed to "the melancholy." This file continues Title 5 from the middle of his list of the marks of melancholy (items 25 to 35), then gives his account of its causes and his first four Directions. The Title heading itself is in the previous file.
- **melancholy:** kept as Baxter's word (see `GLOSSARY.md`): a lasting low, fearful, or brooding state, understood in his day as partly bodily. It is not a modern clinical diagnosis, and we have not substituted one. Baxter's medical views (humors, "spirits," a "sickness of the brain") are those of his time; they are rendered as he wrote them.
- **Suicide (item 30):** Baxter describes, pastorally and without condemnation, sufferers who are "strongly tempted to murder themselves" and some who yield. His phrase "make away with themselves" is rendered "take their own lives." Readers in distress today should seek immediate help from a doctor, a crisis line, or emergency services.
- **choleric, phlegmatic, sanguine** (item 26): the four-temperament theory of Baxter's day, with melancholy as the fourth; each temperament was thought to incline a person to particular faults. Kept, since Baxter is using the scheme on purpose.
- **the spirits and blood will rush to the part that is hurt** (item 26): Baxter's "will have recourse to." The "spirits" are the "animal spirits" of older physiology, thought to carry sense and motion through the body. Likewise "the brain and spirits" in Direction 4.
- **thwart themselves** (item 26): Baxter's "cross themselves," meaning the fears and desires work against each other. Changed, since "cross themselves" now suggests making the sign of the cross.
- **besides; intervenes; more easily; often; violent:** Baxter's "withal," "interposeth," "much easier," "ofttimes," "vehement."
- **wantonness** (item 26): kept; it means sexual looseness or lewdness.
- **unbelief** (item 26): Baxter's "infidelity" (lack of faith, not marital unfaithfulness), following the project's "infidel to unbeliever" decision.
- **ready to yield in a temptation to be quiet** (item 26): left as written; Baxter means they yield in order to get relief from the pressure.
- **As a result** (items 27, 28): Baxter's "Hereupon."
- **the sin against the Holy Spirit** (item 27): Baxter's "Holy Ghost." Unmarked allusion to Matthew 12:31-32 (compare Mark 3:28-29); not in Baxter's text.
- **least willing toward it** (item 27, also truth 8): Baxter's "least willing of it," meaning least consenting to it.
- **possessed by devils** (item 28): Baxter's "possessed of devils." **imagination** here and in causes item 3: Baxter's "fantasy" (the image-making faculty of the mind).
- **near madness; truly; disordered brain** (item 29): Baxter's "near distraction," "verily," "crazed brain" ("crazed" then meant cracked or broken). **curtains** are probably bed-curtains.
- **starve themselves; nowhere; something ... pushed them on** (item 30): Baxter's "famish themselves," "no whither," "somewhat within them put them on."
- **troubled** (item 31): Baxter's "vexed." **want** is kept in the sense of need.
- **unmanageable; notions; however irrational they are** (item 33): Baxter's "intractable," "conceits," "be they never so irrational."
- **gladden** (item 34): Baxter's "rejoice" (used transitively).
- **disorder; hate to hear people tell them so; medicine** (item 35): Baxter's "distemper," "abhor to hear men tell them so," "physic." "the forsakings ... of God" is kept.
- **merriment and indulgence in physical pleasure:** Baxter's "jollity and sensuality" (see `GLOSSARY.md` on *sensual*).
- **hardship; unbroken; emotional** (causes 1, 3, 4): Baxter's "cross," "unintermitted," "passionate" (see `GLOSSARY.md`).
- **The great predisposing causes of it** (cause 5): Baxter's "The great preparatives to it," things that prepare or dispose a person for it. His parentheses are kept; the commas around them are dropped.
- **"most often found in women"** (cause 5): Baxter's generalization about women, kept as he wrote it; it reflects the assumptions of his time.
- **useless; free will; practice** (paragraph after the causes): Baxter's "vain," "free-will," "practise."
- **Direction 1:** Baxter's "manifested in Christ" is rendered "shown in Christ." Only the first sentence is set as the bold Direction heading, as in the finished files; the next sentence opens the body.
- **satisfaction** (truth 2): kept as the technical term for Christ's making amends to God's justice for sin.
- **amnesty** (truth 3): Baxter's "act of oblivion" (see `GLOSSARY.md`). **made himself over:** Baxter's "made over himself," meaning conveyed or granted himself, as in a legal deed.
- **namely** (truth 4): Baxter's "even" in its older sense of "that is."
- **the day of grace** (truth 5): the time in which God offers pardon. Baxter is answering the fear that it may already have ended for someone before death. **in a state of life:** kept; it means in a state of salvation.
- **(formally)** (truth 8): kept. It is a scholastic term: considered as to what makes a thing a sin (the will's consent), rather than as to the outward matter.
- **taking in the means also** (truth 10): kept; Baxter means one must desire the means to holiness too, not only the end.
- **making our calling ... election sure** (truth 11): unmarked allusion to 2 Peter 1:10; not in Baxter's text, which gives no reference and is not a quotation.
- **frenzies** (truth 12): Baxter's "phrensies," acute fevered delirium or madness. **bodily disorder:** his "bodily distemper." "such as" for his "as."
- **cast your cares on God** (Direction 2): unmarked allusion, probably to 1 Peter 5:7 (compare Psalm 55:22); not in Baxter's text. Not quoted, so no NET wording is used.
- **Beware of; Do not set so much store by** (Direction 2): Baxter's "Take heed of," "Set not so much by."
- **arrow prayers** (Direction 3): Baxter's "ejaculatory prayers," that is, very short prayers darted up to God in the moment. "Ejaculatory" has shifted in sense, so we use "arrow prayers," a common modern name for the same thing. Baxter's "except it be" is "except it is."
- **disable you for; walk on it; refrain from** (Direction 3): Baxter's "disable you to," "go on it," "forbear." "I persuade you not from" is kept: I am not trying to persuade you away from.
- **secret duty** (Direction 4): private devotion, such as prayer alone. **only as you can:** Baxter's "but as you can."
- No Scripture is quoted in this range, so no NET text was needed.
