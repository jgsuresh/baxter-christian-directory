# Chapter 4: Signs of Pride toward God (Signs 16-20), and the First Signs of Pride against God

*This file begins in the middle of Direction 2 ("Observe the motions and discoveries of pride"), partway through Baxter's list of signs of pride, at Sign 16.*

**Sign 16.** A proud man would have his reason to be the rule of all the world, or at least, of all that he has to do with. If there were laws or canons to be made, he would have the making of them: he would have all men take his counsel, as an oracle: he would have all the world of his opinion; and sets more by those that thus esteem him and are of his opinion, and yield to all that he says and does, than by those that most earnestly desire to conform their minds to the word of God, and differ from him in the understanding of any part of it. He loves them better that inquire of him and take his word, than them that inquire of the word of God. Though he cannot deny that it is God's prerogative to be infallible, and the rule of the world.

**Sign 17.** A proud man desires the reputation of God's immutability as well as his infallibility. He will stand to an error when once he has vented it, and resist the truth when once he has appeared against it, to avoid the dishonor of being accounted mutable, or one that formerly was deceived. His pride keeps him from repenting of any fault or error that he can but find a cloak for. If he has done wrong to God and mischief to the church, he will do as much more to make it good, and justify it by any cruelty or violence. If he has once done you wrong, he will do more for fear of seeming to have wronged you. If he has slandered you, he will stab or hang you if he can, to justify his slander, rather than seem so mutable as to retract it.

**Sign 18.** A proud man desires a participation in God's omniscience, and is eager to know more than God reveals (if he is an inquiring man whose pride runs this way). Thus our first parents sinned, by desiring to be as God in knowledge. This has filled the world with proud contentions, and the church with divisions; while proud wits heretically make things unrevealed the matter of their ostentation, imposition, censures, or furious disputes; while humble souls are taken up in studying and practicing things revealed, and keep themselves within God's bounds, as knowing that God best knows the measure fittest for them, and that knowledge is to be desired and sought, but so far as it is useful to our serving or enjoying God, and the good which truth reveals to us; and that knowledge may else become our sorrow (Ecclesiastes 1:1, 8), and truth the instrument to torment us, as it does the miserable souls in hell.

**Sign 19.** A proud man is discontented with his degree, especially if it is low. He would be higher in power, and honor, and wealth; yes, he is never so high but he would gladly be one step higher. If he had a kingdom, he would have another: and if he had the dominions of the Turkish or Tartarian emperor, he would desire to enlarge them, and to have more; and would not be satisfied till he had all the world. Men do not feel this in their low condition; they think, If I had but so much or so much, I would be content: but this is their ignorance of the insatiable pride that dwells in them. Do you not see the greatest emperors on earth still seeking to be greater. Every man naturally would be a pope, the universal monarch of the world. And every such pope would have both swords, and have princes and people wholly at their will: and when they have no mind to hurt, they would have power to hurt; that all the world might hold their estates, and liberties, and lives, as by their clemency and gift, and they might be as God to other men. And if they had attained this, pride would not stop, till it had caused them to aspire to all the prerogatives of God, and to depose him, and dethrone him of his Godhead and majesty, that they might have his place.

**Sign 20.** A proud man would gladly have God's independence. Though need makes him stoop, yet he would willingly be beholden to none. Not only because in prudence he would keep his liberty, and not be unnecessarily the servant of men, nor under obligations to serve them in any evil way (for so the humblest would gladly be independent); but because he would be so great, and high, as to scorn to lean on any other. Thus you see how pride is that great idolatry that sets up man as in the place of God.

**Signs of the next Degrees of Pride as against God.**

**Sign 1.** A proud heart is with great difficulty brought to see the greatness of its sins, or to know its emptiness of grace, or to be convinced of its unpardoned, miserable state, or of the justice of God if he should damn it to everlasting torments. [212] Concerning others it may confess all this; but with difficulty of itself. Its own unbelief and aversion from God and holiness, seems to it a small and tolerable fault; its own pride, and lust, and worldliness, and indulgence in physical pleasure, seem not to be so bad as to deserve damnation; much less the smallest sin which it commits. Though customarily they may say that God would be just, if he did condemn them, yet they do not believe it at the heart. The most convincing preacher will have a great deal of trouble to bring a proud man heartily to confess that he is an enemy to God, a child of wrath, and under the guilt of all his sins, and sure to be condemned unless he is converted. He will confess that he is a sinner, or anything else which the most godly must confess, or which does not conclude him to be in a damnable, unrenewed state. But to make an ungodly man know that he is ungodly, and an impenitent person know that he is impenitent, and an unsanctified person know that he is unsanctified, is wonderfully hard, because that pride has dominion in them. "We are not blind too, are we?" say the proud, incorrigible Pharisees to Christ (John 9:40).

**Sign 2.** A proud heart so much overvalues all that is in itself, that every common grace or duty seems to it to be a state of godliness. Their common knowledge seems to them to be saving illumination: every little sorrow for their sin, or wish that they had done better, when they have had all the sweetness of it, passes with them for true repentance; their heartless lip-labor passes for acceptable prayer; their image of religion seems to them to be the life of godliness; they take their own presumption for true faith, and their false expectation for christian hope, and their carnal security and block-headed stupidity for spiritual peace of conscience, and their reckless venturing their souls upon deceit they take for a trusting them with God. If they refrain from but such sins as their flesh can spare, as unnecessary to its ease, provision, or content, yes, or such sins as the flesh commands them to refrain from, as tending to their dishonor in the world, they take this for true obedience to God. Because they would rather have heaven than hell, when they must leave the earth, whether they will or not, they think that they are heavenly-minded, and lay up their treasure there, and take it for their portion: because conscience sometimes troubles them for their sin, they think they renew a sincere repentance, and think all is pardoned, because they daily ask for pardon. Their forced submission to the hand of God they take for patience; and a "Lord, have mercy on us, and forgive us, and save us," they take for a true preparation for death. Thus pride deceives sinners, by making them believe that they have what they have not, and do what they do not, and are something when they are nothing (Galatians 6:3), and by multiplying and magnifying the little common good that is in them.

**Sign 3.** A proud heart has very little sense of the necessity of a Savior, to die for his sins, and satisfy God's justice, and reconcile him to God: notionally he is sick of sin; and notionally he thinks he needs a physician; but practically, at the heart he feels little of his disease, and therefore little values Christ. He does not feel that which should thoroughly acquaint him with the reasons of this blessed work of our redemption; and therefore indeed is a stranger to the mystery, and an unbeliever at the heart, and would turn apostate if the trial were strong enough. He never felt himself a condemned man, under the curse and wrath of God, and liable to hell; and therefore never lay in tears with Mary at his Savior's feet, nor melted over his bleeding Lord; nor feelingly said with Paul, "He came to save sinners, of whom I am chief;" nor "esteemed all things as loss and dung for the knowledge of Christ, that he might be found in him" (Philippians 3:7, 8). He is a christian but as a Turk is a Mahometan, because it is the religion of the king, and the country in which he was bred.

**Sign 4.** A proud heart does not perceive his own necessity of so great a change as a new birth, and of the Holy Spirit to give him a new nature, and plant the image of God upon him. He finds perhaps some breaches in his soul; but he thinks there needs no breaking of the heart for them, nor pulling all down and building up his hopes anew. Amending his heart, he thinks may do, without making it and all things new (2 Corinthians 5:17). The new creature he takes to be but baptism, or some patching up of the former state, and amending some grosser things that were amiss. He will confess that without Christ and grace we can do nothing, but he thinks this grace is an ordinary help. Whereas a humble soul is so emptied of itself, and perceives its deadness and insufficiency to good, that it magnifies grace, and is wondrously thankful for it, as for a new and spiritual life.

**Sign 5.** A proud heart has so little experiential sense of the great accusations which Scripture brings against the corrupted heart of man, that it is easily drawn into any heresy which denies them: as about our original sin, and misery, and need of a Savior; about the desperate wickedness of the heart, and man's insufficiency and powerlessness to good, yes, aversion from it: whereas humble men are better acquainted with the sin within them, that bears witness to all these truths. [213]

**Sign 6.** The proud are unaware of the need and reason of all that diligence to mortify the flesh, and subdue corruptions, and watch the heart, and walk with God in holiness of life, which God requires. He says, what need all this fuss? he does not feel the need of it, and therefore thinks it is more fuss than is needed. But the humble soul is aware of that within him that requires it, and justifies the strictest ways of God. The rich think they have no need to labor, but labor is a poor man's life and maintenance; if he misses it a day, he feels the lack of it the next.

**Sign 7.** Proud men are very unaware of the lack of frequent and fervent prayer to God. Begging is the poor man's trade: the humble soul perceives the need of it: he finds as constant need of God, as of air, or bread, or life itself. And he knows that the exercise of our desires and faith, and the expression by prayer of our dependence upon God, is the way appointed for our supply. But the proud are full-stomached, and think this earnest, frequent praying is but hypocritical, needless work, and they cannot make a trade of begging, and therefore they are sent empty away.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®, fetched from labs.bible.org; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Range begins mid-Direction:** the file opens at Sign 16 of Direction 2's list; Baxter's "Sign XVI." etc. are rendered "Sign 16." and so on. His second list, headed "Signs of the next Degrees of Pride as against God," restarts at Sign 1 (Roman numerals in the source); we keep his numbering.
- **Ecclesiastes 1:1, 8 (reference doubtful):** Baxter cites these for "knowledge may become our sorrow." The NET of 1:1 is "The words of the Teacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem" and of 1:8 is "All this monotony is tiresome; no one can bear to describe it. The eye is never satisfied with seeing, nor is the ear ever content with hearing." Neither speaks of knowledge bringing sorrow. The verse that does is probably Ecclesiastes 1:18 (NET: "For with great wisdom comes great frustration; whoever increases his knowledge merely increases his heartache"). We kept Baxter's reference as printed (possibly a misprint for 1:18; not certain).
- **John 9:40:** Baxter's "Are we blind also?" is the King James wording. NET: "We are not blind too, are we?" We used the NET; the sense is the same (a question expecting "no").
- **Galatians 6:3:** cited by Baxter without quotation marks ("are something when they are nothing"). We kept his words and gave the reference. NET: "if anyone thinks he is something when he is nothing, he deceives himself."
- **Philippians 3:7, 8 (his paraphrase):** Baxter quotes "esteemed all things as loss and dung for the knowledge of Christ, that he might be found in him." This blends verses 7, 8 and part of 9 and is not the NET wording. We kept his words, modernized. NET (3:8): "I now regard all things as liabilities compared to the far greater value of knowing Christ Jesus my Lord, for whom I have suffered the loss of all things—indeed, I regard them as dung!—that I may gain Christ." The NET says "liabilities" where Baxter (King James) has "loss," and "gain Christ" where he has the later phrase "be found in him" (which is in verse 9 of the NET: "and be found in him"). The sense Baxter relies on is kept in his own words. Flagged.
- **"He came to save sinners, of whom I am chief" (his paraphrase, no reference in his text):** this is probably 1 Timothy 1:15 (reference ours, not Baxter's). NET: "Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners—and I am the worst of them!" His "chief" is the King James word; the NET has "the worst."
- **2 Corinthians 5:17:** cited without quotation marks. "New creature" is the King James phrase, which Baxter then glosses ("baptism, or some patching up"). NET has "new creation." We kept his word "creature" in the body since it is his phrase in the sentence that follows. "Making it and all things new" recalls the King James "all things are become new"; NET: "what is old has passed away—look, what is new has come!"
- **Unmarked allusions:** Sign 1, "unbelief, and averseness from God" and Sign 3, "child of wrath" (compare Ephesians 2:3); Sign 3, "lay in tears with Mary at his Savior's feet" (Luke 7 or John 11 or 12; Baxter does not say which Mary); Sign 7, "sent empty away" (compare Luke 1:53; this is a recognition by us, not stated by Baxter). Sign 18, "our first parents sinned, by desiring to be as God in knowledge" (Genesis 3:5).
- **Word swaps (Baxter's word, then ours):** affecteth (desires; in his sense "aims at, tries to claim"); fain (gladly); independency (independence); hardly (with difficulty); averseness (aversion); sensuality (indulgence in physical pleasure); much ado (a great deal of trouble; fuss); wonderful hard (wonderfully hard); doth go with them for (passes with them for); lip-labour (lip-labor); blockish (block-headed); desperate (reckless, in "desperate venturing"); forbear (refrain from); had rather (would rather); sets by (values); experimental (experiential, "from experience"); impotency (powerlessness); insensible of (unaware of; Baxter's sense is "has no feeling for"); Holy Ghost (Holy Spirit); serve the turn (do); Saviour (Savior). In Sign 6, "more ado than needs" is "more fuss than is needed" (two small added words for modern grammar).
- **Terms kept as Baxter's:** *common grace* (the ordinary goodness found in people who are not saved), *security* ("carnal security," careless confidence), *notionally* (in thought only, not in feeling or practice), *full-stomached* (here, haughty and well fed), *degree* (rank), *prerogative* and *clemency*. "Wits" in Sign 18 means clever persons.
- **Baxter's polemic and terms of his day:** "both swords" (Sign 19) is the old claim that the pope holds both spiritual and civil power; "every man naturally would be a pope" is his own charge, kept as he wrote it. "The Turkish or Tartarian emperor" (the Ottoman sultan and a ruler of the Tartars) and "a Turk is a Mahometan" are his terms; the second is his point that such a person follows the religion of his king and country. We have not checked his historical claims.
- **Plutarch (note 212):** Baxter's title is an English rendering of a Plutarch work on mental versus bodily ills; we have not confirmed its modern title or where this line falls.
- **Punctuation:** "Do you not see the greatest emperors on earth still seeking to be greater." ends with a period in the source though it is a question; kept. The trailing comma-separated "Though he cannot deny..." (Sign 16) is a sentence fragment in the source, kept. "Deny but" became "deny that."
