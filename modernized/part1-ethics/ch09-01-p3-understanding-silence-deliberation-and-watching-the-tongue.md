# Chapter 9: General directions for the tongue, Directions 5–15

**Direction 5. Labor for understanding in the matters on which you should talk.**

Ignorance denies provision for talk, or furnishes you only with chaff and emptiness, and makes you so speak that it would be better to say nothing. Knowledge and wisdom are continual storehouses of good and profitable talk: such as the "expert in the law who has been trained for the kingdom of heaven ... who brings out of his treasure what is new and old" (Matthew 13:52). When a man understands the matter which he is to speak of, he is equipped to speak understandingly of it to others, and to defend it against opponents. "The godly speak wise words and promote justice. The law of their God controls their thinking; their feet do not slip" (Psalm 37:30–31). "The speech of the righteous bears the fruit of wisdom, but the one who speaks perversion will be destroyed. The lips of the righteous know what is pleasing, but the speech of the wicked is perverse" (Proverbs 10:31–32). Wise men are never unprovided for wise speech; but the mouth of fools betrays their folly. "The tongue of the wise treats knowledge correctly, but the mouth of the fool spouts out folly" (Proverbs 15:2). "In the speech of a fool is a rod for his back, but the words of the wise protect them" (Proverbs 14:3). "The lips of a fool enter into strife, and his mouth invites a flogging. The mouth of a fool is his ruin, and his lips are a snare for his life" (Proverbs 18:6–7). But you will say, "To tell us that we should get wisdom is a word soon spoken, but not a thing that is easily or quickly done." It is very true; and therefore it is as true that the tongue is not easily well used and governed; for men cannot express the wisdom which they do not have, unless it is by rote: therefore you must take Solomon's counsel, "My child, if you receive my words, and store up my commands inside yourself, by making your ear attentive to wisdom and by turning your heart to understanding, indeed, if you call out for discernment—shout loudly for understanding—if you seek it like silver and search for it like hidden treasure, then you will understand how to fear the Lord, and you will discover knowledge about God. For the Lord gives wisdom," etc. (Proverbs 2:1–6).

**Direction 6. In the meantime learn to be silent until you have learned to speak.**

Do not let your tongues run before your wits; do not speak of that which you do not well understand, unless as learners, to receive instruction. Rather of the two speak too little than too much. [486] Those that insist on talking of things which they do not understand are accustomed either to speak evil of them (as in Jude 10) when they are good; or to speak evil of them, whether they are good or bad. He that cannot hold his tongue well cannot speak well. "A time to keep silent, and a time to speak" (Ecclesiastes 3:7). "There is a time so evil that the prudent should keep silence" (Amos 5:13). At such a time *Nihil æque proderit quam quiescere, et minimum cum aliis loqui et plurimum secum*, says Seneca: It is then the best way to be quiet, and to say little to others, and much to yourselves. You have two ears and one tongue; hear twice and speak once; we more often repent of speaking than of being silent. Few words are quickly answered for. To be wary and sparing of your speech not only avoids abundance of contention, danger, and repentance, but also gains you a reputation for wisdom. Plutarch says well that *Pauca loquentibus paucis legibus opus est*: There is need of but few laws for those that speak but few words. When one said to the cynic, when he was much silent, If you are a wise man, you act foolishly; if you are a fool, you act wisely. He answered, *Nemo stultus tacere potest*, A fool cannot hold his tongue; and he that cannot hold his tongue cannot hold his peace. Pythagoras's counsel in this agrees with Christ's, *Aut sile, aut affer silentio meliora*, Either be silent, or say something that is better than silence. It was a wise answer of him that, being asked whom greedy landlords and whom greedy lawyers hated most, answered to the first, Those that eat little and sweat much (for they usually live long, and so their leases do not soon expire); and to the second, Those that speak little and love much; for such seldom make any work for lawyers. Two things are required in the matter of your speech; that it is something needful to be spoken, and that it is a thing which you understand. Until then be silent.

**Direction 7. Beware of hasty rashness in your speech; and use deliberation, especially in great or in doubtful things. [487]**

Think before you speak: it is better to try your words before you speak them than after; a preventive trial is better than a repenting trial; but if both are omitted, God will try them to your greater cost. I know, in matters that are thoroughly understood, a wise man can speak without any further premeditation than the immediate exercise of the knowledge which he expresses; but when there is any fear of misunderstanding, or an inability to speak fitly and safely without forethought, there hasty speaking without deliberation (especially in weighty things) must be avoided: "You have seen someone who is hasty in his words—there is more hope for a fool than for him" (Proverbs 29:20). Especially be careful in speaking either to God in prayer, or in the name of God, or as from God in preaching or exhortation, or about the holy matters of God in any of your talk; "Be careful what you do when you go to the temple of God; draw near to listen rather than to offer a sacrifice like fools, for they do not realize that they are doing wrong" (Ecclesiastes 5:1): that is, watch yourself in public worship, and be more eager to learn of God and to obey him, as aware of your ignorance and subject to his will, than to offer him your sacrifice (as if he stood in need of you) while you neglect or reject his commands. "Do not be rash with your mouth or hasty in your heart to bring up a matter before God, for God is in heaven and you are on earth! Therefore, let your words be few. Just as dreams come when there are many cares, so the rash vow of a fool occurs when there are many words" (Ecclesiastes 5:2–3): that is, come to God as an obedient learner and a receiver, and not as a giver; and therefore be readier to hear what he has to command you than to pour out many words before him, as if he would accept and hear you for your babbling. If talkativeness and eagerness to talk many undigested words are a sign of folly among men, how much more when you speak to God that is in heaven!

**Direction 8. Keep a holy government over all your passions (as said before), and especially try all those words with suspicion which any passion urges you to vent.**

For passion is so apt to blind the judgment that even holy passions themselves must be warily managed, and feared, as you carry fire among straw or other combustible matter. As "a harsh word stirs up wrath" (Proverbs 15:1); so anger causes harsh words. "Do not let yourself be quickly provoked, for anger resides in the lap of fools" (Ecclesiastes 7:9). To govern the tongue when you are in any passion (either love, or fear, or grief, or anger) is like the governing of a ship in storms and tempests, or the managing of a horse that is fierce and heated. "A fool throws off restraint and is overconfident. A person who has a quick temper will do foolish things" (Proverbs 14:16–17). "It is better to live in the wilderness than with a quarrelsome and easily provoked woman" (Proverbs 21:19). "An angry person stirs up dissension, and a wrathful person is abounding in transgression" (Proverbs 29:22). There is no ruling the tongue if you cannot rule the passions: therefore it is good counsel, "Do not make friends with an angry person, and do not associate with a wrathful person, lest you learn his ways and entangle yourself in a snare" (Proverbs 22:24–25).

**Direction 9. Foresee your opportunities for profitable conversation, and your temptations to evil speeches.**

For we are seldom thoroughly prepared for sudden, unexpected events. Consider when you go out what company you are likely to fall into, and what good you are likely to be called to, or what evil you are most likely to be tempted to: especially consider the ordinary regular duties and temptations of your daily company and dealings.

**Direction 10. Accordingly (besides your aforementioned general preparations) be prepared particularly for those duties and those temptations: always carry about with you some special preservatives against those particular sins of speech which you are most in danger of; and some special provisions and helps to those duties of speech which you may be called to: as a surgeon will carry about with him his instruments and salves which he is likely to have use for among the persons that he has to deal with; and as a traveler will always carry such necessaries with him as in his travels he cannot do without.**

If you are to converse with angry men, be always furnished with patience and firm resolutions to "give place to wrath" (Romans 12:19). If you are to converse with ignorant, ungodly men, go furnished with powerful, convincing reasons, to humble them and change their minds. If you are to go among the quibbling or scorning enemies of holiness, go furnished with well-digested arguments for the defense of that which they are most likely to oppose, that you may shame and stop the mouths of such opponents. This must be done by "the sword of the Spirit (which is the word of God)" (Ephesians 6:17). Therefore be well acquainted with the Scripture, and with particular plain texts for each particular use: by them the "person dedicated to God may be capable and equipped for every good work" (2 Timothy 3:17).

**Direction 11. Continually walk as in the presence of God, and as under his government and law, and as those that are passing on to judgment. [488]**

Ask yourselves, whatever you say: 1. Whether it is fit for God to hear? 2. Whether it is agreeable to his holy law? 3. Whether it is such speech as you would hear of at the day of judgment? If it is speech unfit for the hearing of a grave and reverend man, will you speak it before God? Will you speak wantonly, or filthily, or foolishly, or maliciously, when God forbids it, and when he is present and hears every word, and when you must certainly give account to him of all?

**Direction 12. Pray every morning to God for preservation from the sins of speech that you are liable to that day.**

Commit the custody of your tongues to him; not so as to think yourselves discharged of it, but so as to implore and trust his grace. Pray as David, "Set a watch, O Lord, before my mouth; keep the door of my lips; do not incline my heart to any evil thing: and that the words of your mouth and the meditations of your heart may be acceptable to him" (Psalm 141:3–4; 19:14).

**Direction 13. Make it part of your continual work to watch your tongues.**

Carelessness and negligence will not serve the purpose in so difficult a work of government. James tells you that to tame and rule the tongue is harder than to tame and rule wild beasts, and birds, and serpents: and as the ruling of a horse by the bridle, and of a ship that is driven by fierce winds: and that the tongue "is a restless evil": and that "if someone does not stumble in what he says, he is a perfect individual, able to control the entire body as well" (James 3). Make it therefore your study and work, and watch it continually.

**Direction 14. Call your tongues daily to account, and ask yourselves what evil you have spoken, and what good you have omitted, every day; and be humbled before God in the penitent confession of the sin which you discover, and renew your resolution for a stricter watch for the time to come.**

If your servant is every day faulty, and never hears of it, he will take it as no fault, and be little careful to amend: indeed, you will remind even your ox of his fault when he goes out of the furrow, by a prick or stroke, and your horse when he is faulty, by a spur or rod. And do you think if you let yourselves, even your tongues, be faulty every day, and never tell them of it, or call them to account, that they are ever likely to be reformed, and not grow careless and accustomed to the sin? Your first care must be for preventing the sin, and doing the duty; saying, as David, "I decided, 'I will watch what I say and make sure I do not sin with my tongue. I will put a muzzle over my mouth while in the presence of an evil person.' I was stone silent; I held back the urge to speak" (Psalm 39:1–3). "My tongue shall speak of your righteousness and of your praise all the day long" (Psalm 35:28; 71:24). "May my tongue sing about your instructions" (Psalm 119:172). "My tongue is as skilled as the stylus of an experienced scribe" (Psalm 45:1). But your next care must be to repent of the faults which you commit, and to judge yourselves for them, and reform: remembering that "there is not a word in your tongues that is not altogether known to God" (Psalm 139:4).

**Direction 15. Make use of a faithful monitor or reprover.**

We are apt, through custom and partiality, to overlook the faults of our own speech. A friend is here exceedingly useful. Ask your friend therefore to watch over you in this: and amend what he tells you of; and do not be so foolish as to take part with your fault against your friend.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). All quoted verses were fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Where this file falls:** this is the third part of Chapter 9, Title 1 (general directions for the government of the tongue), and it begins at the start of Direction 5. "Title" is Baxter's word ("_Tit._") for a heading or division of a chapter; Chapter 9 has six of them. No Title heading falls inside this range.
- **Matthew 13:52** (Direction 5): Baxter's "scribe instructed to the kingdom of heaven" is the King James wording; the NET has "expert in the law who has been trained for the kingdom of heaven." We use the NET and mark with "..." the words Baxter skips ("is like the owner of a house").
- **Psalm 37:30–31; Proverbs 10:31–32:** NET wording. The King James "The mouth of the righteous speaketh wisdom" and "the froward tongue shall be cut out" become "The godly speak wise words" and "the one who speaks perversion will be destroyed." The NET's "speech" in place of the King James "mouth" weakens slightly the verbal link to the tongue that Baxter is drawing, though the sense of speech is kept.
- **Proverbs 14:3:** the King James "a rod of pride" becomes the NET "a rod for his back," a different reading of the Hebrew (punishment rather than pride).
- **Proverbs 2:1–6:** NET wording, ending where Baxter ends with "&c." ("etc."). The NET's "My child" stands for the King James "My son."
- **Ecclesiastes 3:7** (Direction 6): the source prints "Eccles. iii. 9," but the words "a time to keep silence, and a time to speak" are in 3:7. We have corrected the reference.
- **Amos 5:13:** Baxter's "There is a time so evil, that the prudent should keep silence" is his rewording of the verse, so we keep his words. The NET reads "For this reason whoever is smart keeps quiet in such a time, for it is an evil time."
- **Jude 10:** a reference only, not quoted. The NET reads "these men do not understand the things they slander."
- **Repeated "speak evil of them"** (Direction 6): the source has "either to speak evil of them ... when they are good; or to speak evil of them, be they good or bad." The repetition looks odd and may be a misprint in the 1846 text, but we have not checked another edition, so we keep it as printed.
- **Latin in the body** (Direction 6): Baxter gives his own English after each Latin saying (Seneca, Plutarch, the cynic, Pythagoras), so we keep the Latin as printed and modernize his English rather than adding our own. Who "the cynic" was Baxter does not say, and we have not identified him. Baxter's attributions of these sayings are his; we have not checked them.
- **"Few words are quickly answered for":** that is, few words are soon accounted for (at judgment or before others). Kept as Baxter wrote it.
- **try, trial** (Directions 7 and 8): Baxter's "try" means test or examine. We keep it because of his play on "trial." **a preventive trial:** Baxter's "a preventing trial," a trial that goes before (see "prevent" in the glossary), set against a "repenting trial" after the fact.
- **the immediate exercise of the knowledge:** Baxter's "the immediate actuating of the knowledge." **inability:** his "disability." **forethought:** his "forethoughts."
- **Proverbs 29:20:** Baxter's King James quotation is a question ("Seest thou a man...?"); the NET makes it a statement ("You have seen someone...").
- **Ecclesiastes 5:1–3:** NET wording. The NET notes that these verses are numbered 4:17–5:2 in the Hebrew Bible. Baxter's comment "watch yourself" explains the King James "Keep thy foot," which the NET renders "Be careful what you do." **The NET changes the sense of verse 3 that Baxter leans on:** the King James "a fool's voice is known by multitude of words" becomes "the rash vow of a fool occurs when there are many words." Baxter's closing sentence about talkativeness as a sign of folly rests on the King James wording. His comment on verse 2 ("come to God as an obedient learner") still fits the NET. In the source the reference "Ver. 2, 3" comes before the quotation; we have moved it into parentheses after it.
- **more eager:** Baxter's "forwarder." **aware of:** his "sensible of." **talkativeness:** his "loquacity." **eagerness:** his "forwardness."
- **Proverbs 15:1** (Direction 8): the King James "grievous words stir up anger" becomes the NET "a harsh word stirs up wrath." Baxter's own answering clause ("so anger causes grievous words") is rendered "harsh words" to keep his echo of the verse.
- **Ecclesiastes 7:9:** the King James "anger resteth in the bosom of fools" becomes "anger resides in the lap of fools."
- **Proverbs 14:16–17:** Baxter quotes the second half of verse 16 and the first half of verse 17. The King James "The fool rageth" becomes the NET "a fool throws off restraint," which shifts the sense from rage to recklessness; Baxter uses the verse in a passage about anger.
- **Proverbs 21:19; 29:22; 22:24–25:** NET wording. The King James "contentious, angry woman" becomes "quarrelsome and easily provoked woman."
- **as said before** (Direction 8): Baxter's "as aforesaid," referring back to an earlier direction we have not checked.
- **events:** Baxter's "accidents" (Direction 9). **regular:** his "stated." **dealings:** his "converse" (as a noun).
- **always:** Baxter's "still" (Direction 10, twice). **quibbling:** his "cavilling." **opponents:** his "gainsayers" (Directions 5 and 10).
- **Romans 12:19** (Direction 10): **the NET changes the sense Baxter leans on,** so we keep his phrase "give place to wrath." The NET reads "give place to God's wrath," that is, leave vengeance to God. Baxter applies the words to yielding before the anger of angry men.
- **Ephesians 6:17:** NET wording; the NET puts "which is the word of God" in parentheses.
- **2 Timothy 3:17:** Baxter's "man of God is complete, throughly furnished to every good work" follows the King James (with "complete," perhaps from its margin, for "perfect"). The NET reads "the person dedicated to God may be capable and equipped for every good work." The NET's "may be" turns Baxter's statement into a purpose clause; his point that Scripture equips a Christian for every use still stands.
- **unfit:** Baxter's "unmeet" (Direction 11). **give account to him of all:** compare Matthew 12:36 (an allusion, not marked by Baxter).
- **Psalm 141:3–4; 19:14** (Direction 12): Baxter's quotation is a blend of two psalms, and the second part is reworded into his own sentence ("and that the words of your mouth ... may be acceptable to him"), so we keep his wording, modernized. The NET reads: "O Lord, place a guard on my mouth. Protect the opening of my lips. Do not let me have evil desires or participate in sinful activities with men who behave wickedly" (141:3–4), and "May my words and my thoughts be acceptable in your sight, O Lord, my sheltering rock and my redeemer" (19:14).
- **James 3** (Direction 13): Baxter paraphrases James 3:3–7 (the bridle, the ship, the taming of beasts, birds, and serpents) in his own words, kept as written. He then quotes 3:8 and 3:2 in that order. The King James "unruly evil" becomes the NET "restless evil," and "offendeth not in word" becomes "does not stumble in what he says." **serve the purpose:** Baxter's "serve turn."
- **Psalm 39:1–3** (Direction 14): Baxter cites verses 1–3 but quotes through the first half of verse 2. The King James "I will keep my mouth with a bridle" becomes the NET "I will put a muzzle over my mouth"; the image is still restraint, but Baxter's bridle echoes the James passage above.
- **Psalm 35:28; 71:24:** Baxter's words, "My tongue shall speak of thy righteousness and of thy praise all the day long," follow the King James of 35:28. **The NET of 35:28 drops the word "tongue,"** which is Baxter's point here, so we keep his wording, modernized. The NET of 35:28 reads "Then I will tell others about your justice, and praise you all day long." The NET of 71:24 keeps "tongue": "All day long my tongue will also tell about your justice."
- **Psalm 119:172; 45:1:** NET wording. The King James "My tongue shall speak of thy word" becomes "May my tongue sing about your instructions," and "the pen of a ready writer" becomes "the stylus of an experienced scribe."
- **Psalm 139:4:** Baxter rewords the verse into the second person ("your tongues"), so we keep his wording. The NET reads "Certainly my tongue does not frame a word without you, O Lord, being thoroughly aware of it."
- **indeed:** Baxter's "nay" (Direction 14). **remind even your ox:** his "remember your very ox."
- **monitor** (Direction 15): in Baxter's sense, one who warns or admonishes. **Ask:** his "Desire."
- **Footnote 486:** James 1:19 in NET wording; the King James "slow to wrath" becomes "slow to anger." Proverbs 17:28 is cited without quotation (it is the verse on a fool counted wise when he holds his peace).
- **Footnote 487:** "Bias in Laert." probably refers to the saying of Bias (one of the Seven Sages of Greece) as reported by Diogenes Laertius in his *Lives of the Philosophers*; we have not checked the passage.
