# Chapter 8: Part 3, directions for the government of the ear (Directions 1-12)

**Part 3.**

**Directions for the Government of the Ear.**

**Direction 1. Employ your ears in the duties which they were made for; and to that end understand those duties.** [394]

Which are as follows: 1. To be the organ of reception of such communications from others as are necessary for our dealings in the world, and the duties of our various relations and vocations. 2. To hear the word of God delivered publicly by his appointed teachers of the church. 3. To hear the counsel of those that privately advise us for our good; and the reproofs of those that tell us of our sin and danger. 4. To hear the praises of God set forth by his church in public, and particular servants in private. 5. To hear from our ancestors and the learned in history what has been done in the times before us. 6. To hear the complaints and petitions of the poor, and needy, and distressed, that we may have compassion on them and seek their relief. 7. To be the passage for grief and hatred to our hearts, by the sinful words which we hear unwillingly.

**Direction 2. Know which are the sins of the ear, that you may avoid them.**

And they are such as follow: 1. A careless ear, which hears the word of God, and the private exhortations of his servants, as if it did not hear them. 2. A dull, sleepy ear, that hears the word of God but as a confused sound, and does not understand, nor feel what is heard. 3. A scornful ear, which despises the message of God, and the reproofs and counsel of men, and scorns to be reproved or taught. 4. An obstinate, stubborn ear, which does not heed advice or will not yield. 5. A profane and impious ear, which loves to hear oaths, and curses, and profane, and blasphemous expressions. 6. A carnal ear, which loves to hear of fleshly things, but does not savor the words which savor of holiness. 7. An airy, hypocritical ear, which loves more the music and melody than the sense and spiritual elevation of the soul to God; and regards more the rhythm and composition and tone than the matter of preaching, prayer, or other such duties; and serves God with the ear, when the heart is far from him. 8. A fastidious ear, which is nauseated by the most profitable sermons, prayers, or talks, if they are not accurately ordered and expressed; and slights or loses the offered benefit for a (modal) imperfection in the offer, or instrument; and casts away all the gold because a piece or two caught a little rust: and perhaps quarrels with the style of the sacred Scriptures, as not exact or fine enough for its expectations. 9. An itching ear, which runs after novelties, and a heap of teachers, and likes something extraordinary better than things necessary. 10. A selfish ear, which loves to hear all that tends to the confirmation of its own notions, and to be flattered or smoothed up by others, and can endure nothing that is contrary to its opinions. 11. A proud ear, which loves its own applause, and is much pleased with its own praises, and hates all that speak of him with low, undervaluing words. 12. A peevish, impatient ear, which is nettled by almost all it hears; and can endure none but silken words, which are oiled and sugared, and fitted by flattery or the lowest submission to their perverse minds; and is so hard to be pleased that none but graduates in the art of pleasing can perform it. 13. A bold, presumptuous ear, which will hear false teachers and deceivers in a proud opinion and confidence of their own abilities to discern what is true and what is false. 14. An ungodly ear, that can easily hear the reproach of holiness, and scoffs at the servants and ways of Christ. 15. A neutral, indifferent ear, that hears either good or evil without much love or hatred, but with a dull and cold indifference. 16. A dissembling, temporizing ear, which can compliantly hear one side speak for holiness, and the other speak against it, and suit itself to the company and conversation it meets with. 17. An uncharitable ear, which can willingly hear the censures, backbitings, slanders, revilings, that are used against others, indeed against the best. 18. An unnatural ear, which can easily and willingly hear the dishonor of their parents, or other near relations, if any carnal interest does but engage them against their honor. 19. A rebellious, disobedient ear, which does not listen to the just commands of magistrates, parents, masters, and other governors, but listens with more pleasure to the words of seditious persons that dishonor them. 20. A filthy, unclean, and adulterous ear, which loves to hear filthy, ribald speeches, and love-songs, and romances, and lascivious plays, and the talk of wanton lust and dalliance. 21. A self-provoking ear, that listens for all that others say against them, which may kindle hatred, or dislike, or passion in them. 22. A busy, meddling ear, which loves to hear of other men's faults, or matters which do not concern them, and to listen to tattlers, and talebearers, and troublemakers, and to have to do with evil reports. 23. A timid, cowardly, unbelieving ear, which trembles at every threatening of man, though in a cause which is God's, and he has promised to vindicate. 24. An idle ear, which can listen to idle, time-wasting talk, and make the sins of tattlers your own. All these ways (and more) you are in danger of sinning by the ear, and becoming partakers in the sins of all whose sinful words you hear, and of turning into sin the words of God, and his servants, which are spoken for your good.

*In the margin: When hearing evil is a sin.*

**Direction 3. Know when the hearing of evil, and not hearing good, is your sin:**

that is, 1. When it is not out of any imposed necessity, but of your voluntary choice; and when you might avoid it on lawful terms, without a greater hurt, and will not. 2. When you do not hate the evil which you are compelled to hear, and do not love the good which through necessity you cannot hear; but your hearts comply with your necessities. 3. When you do not show as much disowning and dislike of the evil which you hear as you might do without an inconvenience greater than the benefit; but make it your own by sinful silence or compliance. 4. When you are presumptuous and fearless of your danger.

*In the margin: The danger of hearing.*

**Direction 4. Know where the danger of such sinful hearing lies.**

As, 1. in displeasing God, who does not love to see his children listen to those that are abusing him, nor to see them playing too boldly about fire or water, nor to touch any stinking or defiling thing, but calls to them, "'come out from their midst, and be separate,' says the Lord, 'and touch no unclean thing, and I will welcome you'" (2 Corinthians 6:16–18). 2. It is dangerous to your imagination and memory, which quickly receives hurtful impressions by what you hear: if you should hear provoking words, even against your wills, yet it is hard to escape receiving some hurtful impression by them: and if you hear lascivious, filthy words against your wills (much more if willingly), it is two to one that they leave some thoughts in your minds which may breed further sin. And it is dangerous to your passions and affections, lest they catch fire before you are aware. And it is dangerous to your understandings, lest they be perverted and seduced: and to your wills, lest they be turned after evil, and turned away from good; and alas! how quickly is all this done! 3. It is dangerous to the speaker, lest your voluntary hearing encourage him in his sin, and hinder his repentance. 4. And it is dishonorable to God and godliness.

**Direction 5. Do your best to live in such company where you will hear that which is good and edifying, and to escape that company whose conversation is hurtful and corrupt.**

Do not run yourselves into this temptation: be sure you have a call; and your call must be discerned, 1. By your office and place; whether any duty of your office or relation binds you to be there. 2. By your ends: whether you are there as a physician to do them good (as Christ went among sinners), or to do the work of your proper calling; or whether you are there out of a carnal, man-pleasing, or temporizing disposition? 3. By the measure of your abilities to attain those ends. 4. By the measure of your danger to receive the infection. 5. By the quality of your company, and the probability of good or evil in the outcome.

*In the margin: When you are called into ill company.*

**Direction 6. When you are called into ill company, go fortified with defensive and offensive arms, as foreseeing what danger or duty you are likely to be cast upon.**

Foresee what talk you are likely to hear, and accordingly prepare yourselves: let your first preparation be to preserve yourselves from the hurt, and your next preparation to refute the evil, and convict the sinful speaker, or at least to preserve the endangered hearers, if you have ability and opportunity. If you are to hear a seducing, heretical teacher, there is another kind of preparation to be made. If you are to hear a beastly, filthy talker, there is another kind of preparation to be made. If you are to hear a cunning Pharisee, or malignant enemy of godliness, reproach, or quibble, or wrangle against the Scriptures, or the ways of God, there is another kind of preparation to be made. If you are to hear only the senseless scorns, or railings and bawlings of ignorant, profane, and pleasure-loving fools, there is another kind of preparation to be made. To give you particular directions for your preparations against every such danger would make my work too tedious; but remember how much lies on your own preparations or unpreparedness.

**Direction 7. Do not be sinfully lacking in good conversation yourselves, if you would not be ensnared by bad conversation from others.**

Your good conversation may forestall, or divert, or shame, or disappoint their evil conversation. Turn the stream another way; and do it wisely, that you do not expose yourselves and your cause to scorn and laughter; and do it with such zeal as the cause requires, that you are not borne down by their greater zeal in evil. And where it is unfit for you to speak, if it may be, let your face or departure signify your dislike and sorrow.

**Direction 8. Especially labor to mortify those sins which the unavoidable conversation of your company most tempts you to; that where the devil does most to hurt you, you may there do most in your own defense.**

Does the talk which you hear tend most to heresy, seduction, or to turn you from the truth? Study the more to be established in the truth; read more books for it; and hear more that is said by wise and godly men against the error which you are tempted to. Is it to profaneness or dislike of a holy life that your company tempts you? Apply yourselves the more to God, and give yourselves up to holiness, and let your study and practice be such as tend to keep your souls in relish with holiness, and hatred of sin. Is it pride that their applauding talk tempts you to? Study the more the doctrine of humiliation. Is it lust that they provoke you to, or is it drunkenness, gluttony, sinful recreations, or excesses? Labor the more in the work of mortification, and keep the strictest guard where they assault you.

**Direction 9. Do not be unacquainted with the particular weaknesses and dangers of your own hearts, or any of your sinful inclinations; that when you know where the wall is weakest, you may there make the best defense.**

That wanton word will set a wanton heart on fire which a sober mind hears with pity as a lunatic kind of speech. A peevish, hot-tempered heart is at once disturbed and kindled by those words which are scarcely noticed by a well-composed soul.

**Direction 10. Hear every sinful word as dictated by the devil; and suppose you saw him all the while at the speaker's elbow, putting each word into his mouth, and telling him what to say.**

For it is as truly the devil that suggests them all as if you saw him. Suppose you saw him behind the reviler, hissing him on, as boys do dogs in fighting, and bidding him, Call him this or that: suppose you saw him at the malignant's ear, bidding him revile a holy life, and speak evil of the ways and servants of the Lord: suppose you saw him behind the wanton, bidding him use such ribald talk, or on the stage, suggesting it to the actors; or at the ear of those that would provoke you to passion, to tell them what to say against you: this just supposition would much preserve you.

**Direction 11. Suppose you heard the end attached to every speech.**

As when you hear one tempting you to lust, suppose he said, Come, let us take our pleasures awhile, and be damned forever: so also in every word that tempts you to any other sin; if the tempter puts in the sin, you put in God's wrath and hell, and do not separate what God has joined, but with the serpent see the sting.

**Direction 12. Observe when the infection first seizes on you, and at once take an antidote to expel it, if you love your souls.**

The signs of infection are, 1. When your zeal abates, and you grow more indifferent to what you hear. 2. Next you will feel some little inclination to it. 3. Next you will venture a little upon an imitation. 4. And lastly you will come to a full consent, and so to ruin. If you feel only a slackening of your dislike and hatred, or any filth or tinge left on your thoughts and imagination, go at once and shake them off; bewail it to God in true repentance, and wash your souls in the blood of Christ, and cast up the poison by holy resolutions, and sweat out the remnant by the fervent exercises of love and holiness.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Part 3:** this file covers the whole of Baxter's Part 3 of Chapter 8 (Directions 1–12 on the ear). The Direction numbering restarts at 1 here, as in the source.
- **Direction headings split from their paragraphs:** in Direction 3 the directive sentence runs on after a colon ("is your sin: that is, 1. ..."); we bolded it through the colon and continued the same sentence in the next paragraph. No words were dropped.
- **2 Corinthians 6:16–18** (Direction 4): Baxter cites verses 16–18 but quotes only part of verse 17 (King James: "Come out from among them, and be ye separate, saith the Lord, and touch not the unclean thing, and I will receive you"). We give the NET wording of verse 17, which itself puts the Lord's words in quotation marks, hence the nested quotation marks. The NET's "come out from their midst" and "I will welcome you" do not change Baxter's point. The cited range is kept as Baxter gave it.
- **Footnote [394]:** references converted to modern form; Baxter cites by King James verse numbering. We have not checked each reference against the NET's verse numbering.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "serves God with the ear, when the heart is far from him" (compare Isaiah 29:13; Matthew 15:8); "an itching ear ... a heap of teachers" (2 Timothy 4:3, King James "heap to themselves teachers, having itching ears"); "as Christ went among sinners" (compare Luke 15:1–2; Matthew 9:10–13); "do not separate what God has joined" (Baxter's "separate not that which God hath adjoined," echoing Matthew 19:6).
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): converse → dealings (Direction 1, item 1: social dealings, not talk); several → various; compassionate (verb) → have compassion on; endeavour their relief → seek their relief; sottish → dull; regardeth not → does not heed; numbers → rhythm (meter, as in verse); composure → composition; curious → fastidious (overly particular, not inquisitive); nauseateth → is nauseated by; discourses → talks; conceits → notions (item 10); cross to → contrary to; mean → low (lowly, belittling; not "unkind"); nettled with → nettled by; froward → perverse; conceit → opinion (item 13, self-conceit); scorns at → scoffs at; indifferency → indifference; complyingly → compliantly; yea → indeed; hearkeneth → listens; hearkeneth after → listens for; carry-tales → talebearers; make-bates → troublemakers (people who stir up strife); timorous → timid; justify → vindicate (item 23: uphold the cause, not acquit a sinner); necessitated → compelled; fantasy → imagination (the faculty that forms images, not daydreaming); gender unto → breed; "two to one but" → "two to one that"; conference → conversation; humour → disposition; event → outcome; confute → refute; convince → convict (here: prove the speaker wrong, as in GLOSSARY); cavil → quibble; sots → fools (Baxter's "sot" is a stupid person, not only a drunkard); lieth upon → lies on; wanting → lacking; insnared → ensnared; discourse → conversation or talk; prevent → forestall; countenance → face; address yourselves → apply yourselves; bedlam → lunatic (from Bedlam, the London hospital for the insane); passionate → hot-tempered (prone to anger); presently → at once; railer → reviler; thus or thus → this or that; annexed → attached; adjoined → joined; remitting → slackening; tincture → tinge.
- **Kept words with notes:** *airy* (item 7: light, superficial); *(modal) imperfection* (item 8: a fault in the manner, not the substance, of what is offered; Baxter's parentheses); *savor ... savor of* (item 6: relish, and have the taste of; Baxter's wordplay is kept); *smoothed up* (item 10: soothed or flattered); *carnal* (unspiritual, ruled by the flesh); *affections* (emotions and desires); *mortify, mortification* (put to death sinful desires); *temporizing* (trimming to the times and company); *malignant* (Direction 6 and 10: Baxter's word for an enemy of godliness; in his day it was also a political label, which may be in the background here, but we do not assert it); *hissing him on* (urging on, as one sets dogs to fight); *cast up* (vomit up, continuing the image of poison and antidote); *relish* (taste for).
- **Pronoun shifts kept:** Baxter moves between "it," "him," and "their/them" within his list of ears (items 11, 12, 13, 18, 21) and ends item 24 with "your own." These are his and are left as written.
- **Item 2:** "But as a confused sound" means "only as a confused sound"; kept.
- **Direction 6, "hear only":** Baxter's "hear but" means "hear only."
- **Direction 11:** "you put in God's wrath and hell" renders Baxter's emphatic imperative "do you put in."
- **Direction 12, item 3:** "you will a little venture upon an imitation" lightly reordered to "you will venture a little upon an imitation."
- **romances, lascivious plays** (item 20; Direction 10 "on the stage"): Baxter's view of the stage and romances is his, kept as written.
