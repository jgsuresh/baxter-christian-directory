# Chapter 9: Title 3, Questions 2-9 on lying: "harmless" lies, the midwives and Rahab, deceit by action, equivocation, mental reservation, and reports

*This file continues the cases of conscience about lying that follow Direction 12 of Title 3; it begins at Question 2.*

*Question 2.* Is it not contrary to the light of nature to allow, for example, a parent, a king, myself, my country, rather to be destroyed, than to save them by a harmless lie?

*Answer.* No. Because, 1. Particular good must give place to common. And if once a lie may pass for lawful in cases where it seems to be good, it will overthrow human interaction, and corrupt man's nature and the world.

2. And if one evil may be made a means for good, it will imply that others may be so too, and so will confound good and evil, and leave wicked man to take all for good which he thinks will do good. That is not to be called a harmless lie which is simply evil, being against the law of God, against the order of nature, the use of human faculties, and the interest and interaction of the sociable world.

3. The error of the objectors chiefly consists in thinking that nothing is further hurtful and morally evil than as it does hurt to some men in bodily respects. Whereas that is evil which is against the universal rule of rectitude, against the will of God, and against the nature and perfection of the agent; much more if it also tends to the hurt of other men's souls, by giving them an example of sinning.

4. And though there may sometimes be some human probability of such a thing, yet there is no certainty that it ever will so turn out that a lie shall save the life of king, parent, or yourselves. For God can open the eyes of that enemy whom you think to blind by a lie, and cause him to know all the truth, and so take away that life which you thought thus to have saved.

5. And there are lawful means enough to save your lives when it is best for you to save them. That is, obey God, and trust him with your lives, and he can save them without a lie, if it is best: and if it is not, it should not be desired.

6. And if men did not erroneously overvalue life, they would not think that a lie was necessary for it. When it is not necessary to live, it is not necessary to lie for life. But thus one sin brings on another: when carnal men overvalue life itself, and think more of it than of the enjoyment of God in the glory of heaven, they must necessarily then overvalue any means which seems necessary to preserve it. See Job 13:7-10; Proverbs 13:17; Romans 6:15; 3:7-9; Psalm 5:7; Hosea 4:2; John 8:44; Revelation 21:27; 22:15; Colossians 3:9; 1 John 2:21.

7. Yet as to the degree of evil in the sin, I readily grant (with Augustine, *Enchiridion*) that *Multum interest quo animo et de quibus quisque mentiatur: non enim ita peccat qui consulendi, quomodo ille qui nocendi voluntate mentitur: nec tantum nocet qui viatorem mentiendo in adversum iter mittit, quantum is qui viam vitæ mendacio fallente depravat*.

*Objection.* Are not the midwives rewarded by God for saving the Israelite children by a lie?

*Answer.* I need not say with Augustine, "The deed was rewarded, and the lie pardoned;" for there is no such thing as a lie found in them. Who can doubt that God could strengthen the Israelite women to be delivered without the midwives? And who can doubt that, when the midwives had made known the king's murderous command, the women would delay to send for the midwives till, by the help of each other, the children were secured? Which yet is credited to the midwives, because they were in league with them, and delayed to that end. So that here is a dissembling and concealing part of the truth, but here is no lie that can be proved.

*Objection.* But in Hebrews 11:31 and James 2:25, Rahab is said to be justified by faith and works, when she saved the spies by a lie.

*Answer.* It is uncertain whether it was a lie, or only an equivocation, and whether her words were not true of some other men that had been her guests. But suppose them a lie (as is most likely), the Scripture no more justifies her lie than her having been a harlot. It is her believing in the God of Israel, whose works she mentioned, that she is commended for, together with the saving of the spies at the risk of her own life. And it is no wonder if such a woman in Jericho had not yet learned the sinfulness of such a lie as that.

*Objection.* But at least it could be no mortal sin, because Hebrews 11:31 and James 2:25 say she was justified.

*Answer.* It was no mortal sin in her (that is, a sin which proves one in a state of death), because it did not have those evils that make sin mortal: but a lie in one that does it knowingly, for lack of such a predominance of the authority and love of God in the soul as should prevail against the contrary motives habitually, is a mortal sin, of an ungodly person. It is pernicious falsehood and soul delusion in those teachers that make poor sinners think that it is the smallness of the outward act or hurt of sin alone that will prove it to be, as they call it, venial, or mortified, and not mortal.

*Question 3.* Is deceit by action lawful, which seems a practical lie? and how shall we interpret Christ's acting as if he would have gone farther, Luke 24:28; and David's feigning madness, and common stratagems in war, and doing things purposely to deceive another?

*Answer.* 1. I have proved before that not all deceiving of another is a sin, but some may be a duty: as a physician may deceive a patient to get down a medicine to save his life, so long as he does not do it by a lie.

2. Christ's seeming to go farther was nothing other than a lawful concealment or dissimulation of his purpose, to prompt their urging: for not all dissimulation is evil, though lying is. And the same may be said of lawful stratagems as such.

3. David's case was not sinful, as it was mere dissimulation to deceive others for his escape. But whether it was not a sinful distrust of God, and a dissimulation by too unmanly a way, I am not able to say, unless I had known more of the circumstances.

*Question 4.* Is it lawful to tempt a child or servant to lie, merely to test them?

*Answer.* It is not lawful to do it without sufficient cause, nor at any time to do that which invites them to lie, or gives any countenance to the sin, as Satan and bad men are accustomed to tempt men to sin, by commending it, or making light of it. But to lay an occasion before them merely to test them (as to lay money, or wine, or other things in their way, to know whether they are thieves or addicted to drink, that we may the better know how to cure them; and so to test their truthfulness) is not unlawful. For, 1. The sin is virtually committed when there is a will to commit it, though there should be no temptation or opportunity. 2. We do nothing which is either a commendation of the sin, or a persuading to it, or any true cause either physical or moral; but only an occasion. 3. God himself, who is more contrary to sin than any creature, does thus, by trial, provide such occasions of sin to men that are wickedly disposed, as he knows they will take; and his common mercies are such occasions. 4. God has nowhere forbidden this to us: we may not do evil that good may come by it; but we may do good when we know evil will come of it by men's vice. 5. It may be a needful means to the cure of that sin, which we cannot know till it is thus exposed.

*Question 5.* Is all equivocation unlawful?

*Answer.* There is an equivocating which is really lying: as when we forsake the usual or proper sense of a word, and use it in an alien, unusual sense, which we know will not be understood, and this to deceive such as we are bound not to deceive.

But there is a use of equivocal words which is lawful and necessary (for human language has few words which do not have various meanings). As, 1. When our equivocal sense is well understood by the hearers, and is not used to deceive them, but because usage has made those words fit; as all metaphors are equivocal, and yet may be used. 2. When the equivocal sense is the most usual or obvious, and if it is not understood, it is through the hearer's fault or extraordinary dullness. 3. When a robber, or usurping tyrant, or any cruel enemy, that has no authority to do it, shall seek to ensnare my life by questions, I may lawfully answer him in such ambiguous words as are purposely intended to deceive him, or leave him ignorant of my sense, provided they are not lies or false in the ordinary usage of those words. 4. And to such a person I may answer ambiguously, when it is apparent that it is an ambiguous answer, and that I do it as professing that I will answer him no more particularly nor plainly, but will conceal the rest.

*Question 6.* Whether all mental reservation is unlawful?

*Answer.* This needs no other answer than the former. If the expressed words are a lie, the mental reservation will not make them justifiable as a truth. But if the expressed words of themselves are true, then the mental reservation may be lawful, when it is no more than a concealment of part of the truth, in a case where we are not bound to reveal it.

But for both these cases I must refer the reader to what I have said about vows, Part 3, Chapter 5, Title 2, without which he will not know my meaning.

*Question 7.* May children, servants, or subjects, in danger, use words which tend to hide their faults?

*Answer.* 1. When they are bound not to hide the fault, they may not: which is, 1. When due obedience, or, 2. The greater good which will follow, requires them to reveal it.

2. When they are not bound to reveal it, they may hide it by just means, but not by lies or any evil. In what cases they may hide a fault by just means, I shall say no more about here.

*Question 8.* May I speak that which I think is true, but am not sure?

*Answer.* If you have a just call, you may say you think it is true; but not flatly that it is so.

*Question 9.* May I believe and speak that of another, by way of news, conversation, or character, which I hear reported by godly, credible persons, or by many?

*Answer.* 1. The main doubt is when you have a call to speak it, which is answered later, in Part 4, at length.

2. You may not so easily believe and report evil of another as good.

3. You must not believe ill of another any further than evidence constrains you; yet you may believe it according to the degree of evidence or credibility; and make use of the report for just caution or for good; but not to defame another on uncertainty, or without a call.

4. The sin of receiving and spreading false reports of others on hearsay is now so common among those that profess sobriety and religion, that all men should beware of it in all company, as they would of the plague in an infectious time. And now it is so notorious that false news and slanders of others are so common, that neither good men's words, nor common rumor, will allow you (or excuse you) to believe or report any evil of another, till you are able to prove that it is your duty; but all Christians should join in lamenting and reproving this common uncharitable sin.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). Baxter quotes no verse word for word in this range; all his Scripture here is cited by reference only, so no NET wording appears in the body. The verses checked below were fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Title:** "Tit." is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," a heading or division; Chapter 9 ("Directions for the Government of the Tongue") is divided into six Titles. This file continues Title 3, "Special directions against lying," and covers the last of the cases of conscience that follow Direction 12, from Question 2 to the end of Title 3. Baxter's "Quest.," "Answ.," and "Object." are rendered *Question*, *Answer*, and *Objection*, and his Roman numerals as Arabic numbers. No footnote markers fall in this range.
- **Augustine in Question 2, point 7:** the Latin is kept exactly as printed, in the body, because Baxter gives it there without a translation. Draft English (prepared for this project; have a Latinist check it): "It makes a great difference with what intent and about what things each person lies: for he who lies with the will to help does not sin in the same way as he who lies with the will to harm; nor does he who by lying sends a traveler on the wrong road do as much harm as he who corrupts the way of life by a deceiving lie." Baxter cites only "Enchirid." (Augustine's *Enchiridion*); we have not checked the exact chapter. **readily grant:** Baxter's "easily grant."
- **Augustine on the midwives:** Baxter's "Austin" is the usual older English form of Augustine's name; rendered "Augustine." The saying "The fact was rewarded, and the lie pardoned" is Baxter's attribution; we have not located it in Augustine's works. "Fact" here has the older sense "deed," and is rendered so.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording; references are ours, not in his text): the Hebrew midwives (Exodus 1:15-21); Rahab and the spies (Joshua 2); David's feigning madness (1 Samuel 21:13); "we may not do evil that good may come by it" (compare Romans 3:8, NET: "Let us do evil so that good may come of it").
- **Psalm 5:7:** kept as Baxter prints it. In the NET (as in the King James) the verse about destroying liars is Psalm 5:6 ("You destroy liars; the Lord despises violent and deceitful people"); verse 7 is about entering God's house. Baxter's "7" may follow a different verse numbering (for example, the Hebrew numbering, which counts the title as verse 1), but we have not confirmed which he used.
- **Romans 6:15; 3:7-9:** kept as cited. Romans 3:7-8 speaks directly to a lie told for God's glory and to doing evil that good may come; the bearing of Romans 6:15 ("Shall we sin because we are not under law but under grace?") is less direct, but we have not changed it.
- **Luke 24:28:** cited only. The NET reads "He acted as though he wanted to go farther," which matches Baxter's point. **Hebrews 11:31; James 2:25:** cited only. In the Rahab objection the source has "But, Heb. xi. 31, and James ii. 25, Rahab is said ..."; we added "in" so the references read as a modern sentence.
