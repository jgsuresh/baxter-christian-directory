# Chapter 6: Title 6, Directions 8–15 (diligence, delight, method, and the use of other men's learning)

**Direction 8. Do not cast away a moment of your precious time in idleness, or irrelevancies; but follow your work diligently, and with all your might.**

I do not mean that you should overdo, and overthrow your brains and bodies, nor forgo such sober exercise as is most necessary to your health; for a sick body is a poor companion for a student, and much more an impaired brain. But time-wasters are lovers of pleasure or idleness, more than of knowledge and holiness: and wisdom does not fall into idle, sluggish, dreaming souls. If you think it not worth your most painstaking and closest studies, you must settle for idle ignorance, and go about with swelling titles and empty brains, as the deceivers and the scourgers of the church.

**Direction 9. Keep up a delight in all your studies, and carry them on not in an unwilling weariness: and, if it is not by notable error in matter or method, gratify your delight with such things as you are best pleased with, though they bring some smaller inconvenience; because otherwise your weariness may bring much more.**

I know that a delight in sin and empty pursuits is not to be gratified; and force must be used with a reluctant mind in case of necessity and weight. But if it is only in the variety of subjects, and the choice of pleasing studies which are profitable, though simply some other might be fitter, something is to be yielded to delight. But especially the heart must be brought to a delight in holy things: and then, time will be put to good use; the memory will be helped; much will be done; and you will persevere; and it will preserve the mind from temptations to needless recreations, and from the deadly plague of youthful lusts, when your daily labor is a greater pleasure to you.

**Direction 10. Get some judicious man to draw up for you the headings of a threefold commonplace book: one part for definitions, axioms, and necessary doctrines; another part for what is useful for ornament and oratory; and another for references as a common index to all the books of that science which you read: for memory will not serve for all.**

Ordinarily students do not have judgment enough to form their own commonplace books until they are old in studies, and have read most of the authors which they would remember; and therefore the young must here have a judicious helper. And when they have done so, injudiciousness will be apt to fill it with less necessary things, and to make an unfit choice of matter, if they do not have care and an instructor.

**Direction 11. Highly esteem a correct method in divinity, and in all your studies; and labor to get an accurate scheme or skeleton, where at once you may see every part in its proper place. But remember that if it is not sound, it will be a snare; and one error in your scheme or method will be apt to introduce many more. [319]**

It is a poor and pitiful kind of knowledge to know many loose pieces, and broken limbs of truth, without knowing the whole, or the place and the relation which they have to the rest. To know letters and not syllables, or syllables and not words, or words and not sentences, or sentences and not the scope of the discourse, are all only an unprofitable knowledge. He knows no science rightly that has not anatomized it, and does not carry a true scheme or method of it in his mind. But among the many that are in print, to commend any one to you which I most esteem, or take to be without error, is more than I dare do.

**Direction 12. Always keep the first, fundamental truths in your mind, and see every other truth which you learn as springing out of them, and receiving their life and nourishment from them: and always keep in your minds a clear distinction between the truths of different degrees, both of necessity, and certainty, always reducing the less necessary to the more necessary, and the less certain to the more certain, and not conversely. [320]**

If God had made all points of faith, or Scripture revelation, of equal necessity, our baptism would not only have mentioned our belief in the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit; nor should we ever have seen the ancient creed, nor the ten commandments. And if all points were of equal evidence, and plainness, and certainty to us, we should not have some so much more disputed than others: "Some things" in Scripture are "hard to understand," but not "all things" (2 Peter 3:16). To claim that any truth is more necessary than it is tends to uncharitableness and contention: and to say that any is less necessary than it is tends to the neglect of it, and to the danger of souls. To claim any point to be more plain and certain than it is only shows our pride and ignorance. But to set up uncertain and unnecessary points, and make a religion of them, and reduce things certain or necessary to them, this is the method of turbulent heretics.

**Direction 13. Take nothing as universally necessary in religion which was not so taken in the days of the apostles, and primitive church; and take that for the safest way to heaven which the apostles went who certainly are there: value the apostolic purity, simplicity, charity, and unity; and do not follow those that, by being wise and pious overmuch, corrupt our sacred pattern by their additions, and fill the church with uncharitableness and strife.**

If it were not a thing too evident that dominion and riches pass for religion with them, and gain for godliness, and honor and money instead of argument, it would be a most stupendous wonder that so many learned men should be found among Christians in the world, to hinder the peace and unity of the church, as do it vehemently and implacably in the church of Rome; when so easy a thing, and so reasonable, would unite almost all the Christian world, as is the requiring no more as necessary to our union than what was made necessary in the days of the apostles, and the imposing of nothing as necessary to salvation which the apostles and primitive church were saved without. This easy, reasonable thing, which no man has anything of seeming sense and weight to speak against, would end all the ruinous differences among Christians.

**Direction 14. Be desirous to know all that God would have you know, and be willing to be ignorant of all that God would have you ignorant of; and do not pry into unrevealed things; and much less make them the matter of any uncharitable strife.**

A great many contentious volumes between the Dominicans, and Jesuits, and many others, are stuffed with bold inquiries, wranglings, or determinations of unsearchable mysteries, utterly unknown to those that voluminously debate them, and never revealed in the word or works of God. Keep off with reverence from concealed mysteries. Do not talk as boldly of the divine influx, and the priority, posteriority, dependence or reason of God's decrees, as if you were talking of your common affairs. Come with great reverence when you are called by God to search into those high and holy truths which he has revealed. But do not claim to know that which is not to be known. For you will only reveal your ignorance and arrogance, and know none the more, when you have doted about questions ever so long.

**Direction 15. Avoid both extremes, of those that study no more than to know what others have written and held before them, and of those that little regarded the discoveries of others. Learn all from your teachers and authors that they can teach you; but make all your own, and see things in their proper evidence; and improve on their discoveries by the utmost of your diligence; abhorring a proud desire of singularity, or to seem wiser than you are.**

Most students through slothfulness look no further for knowledge than into their books; and their learning lies only in knowing what others have written, or said, or held before them; especially where the least differing from the judgment of the party which is uppermost or in reputation tends to hazard a man's honor, or advancement, there men think it dangerous to seem to know more than is commonly known; and therefore think it needless to study to know it. Men are reluctant to take much pains to know that which tends to their ruin to be known, but does them no harm as long as they can keep themselves ignorant of it: which makes the opposed truth have so few welcomers or students among the papists, or any that persecute or reproach it. And others, discerning this extreme, run into the contrary; and under pretense of the loveliness of truth, and the need of liberty of judging, think the edifying way is first to pull down all that others have built before them, and little regard the judgment of their predecessors, but think they must take nothing on trust from others, but begin all from the very ground themselves. And usually their pride makes them so little regard the most approved authors that they do not have patience to read them until they thoroughly understand them; but reject that which is received before they understand it, merely because it was the received way: and while they say that nothing must be taken upon trust, they at once take upon trust themselves that very opinion, and with it the other opinions of those innovators that teach them this. And believing what such say to the discredit of others, they at the same time believe what they hold in opposition to those that they have discredited. But it is easy to see how sad a case mankind would be in, if every man must be a fabricator of all his knowledge himself, and posterity should be none the better for the discoveries of their ancestors; and the greatest labors of the wisest men, and their highest attainments, must be no profit to any but themselves. Why do they use a teacher, if they must do all themselves? If they do not believe their tutors, and take nothing on trust, it seems they must know every truth before they will learn it: and what difference is there between believing a tutor and an author? And is not that more credible which upon long experience is approved by many nations and ages, than that which is recommended to you only by one or a few? These students should have made themselves an alphabet or grammar, and not have taken the common ones on trust. It is easier to add to other men's inventions than to begin and carry on all ourselves. By their course of study, the world would never grow wiser; but every age and person be always beginning, and none proceed beyond their rudiments.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Where this file falls:** this range begins at the start of Direction 8 of Title 6 ("Title" is Baxter's word, abbreviated "Tit.", for a heading or main division of the chapter). Title 6 gives directions for young students; Directions 1–7 are in the previous file, and the next file continues with what follows Direction 15 (not checked).
- **2 Peter 3:16:** NET wording for "Some things ... hard to understand." In the verse, the "things" are things in Paul's letters (the NET reads "Some things in these letters"); Baxter widens this to "in Scripture," which he places outside the quotation marks. The "all things" in his quotation marks is his own contrast, not words from the verse; we keep his marks as printed. The King James has "hard to be understood"; the sense is the same.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "with all your might" (Ecclesiastes 9:10); "lovers of pleasure" (2 Timothy 3:4); "youthful lusts" (2 Timothy 2:22); "gain for godliness" (1 Timothy 6:5); "wise and pious overmuch" (compare Ecclesiastes 7:16, King James "righteous over much ... over wise"); "unrevealed things" (compare Deuteronomy 29:29); "doted about questions" (1 Timothy 6:4, King James "doting about questions," where the NET has "an unhealthy interest in controversies"). Our identification of these allusions is not in Baxter's text.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): impertinencies, irrelevancies; forbear, forgo; ill companion, poor companion; crazed brain, impaired brain (in Baxter's day "crazed" meant cracked or broken in health, not insane); painfullest, most painstaking; take up with, settle for; go abroad, go about (that is, go out in public); vanity, empty pursuits; backward, reluctant; got to, brought to; improved (of time), put to good use; titles, headings; unmeet, unfit; just method, correct method; abundance more, many more; parcels, pieces; members, limbs; extant, in print; still (twice in Direction 12), always; verities, truths; primitive (of truths), first; several, different; contrarily, conversely; controverted, disputed; pretend, claim; doth but show, only shows; go for, pass for; obtruding, imposing; ruinating, ruinous; abundance of, a great many; discover, reveal; never the more, none the more; never so long, ever so long; preferments, advancement; entertainers, welcomers (those who receive a truth as a guest); can but keep, can keep; presently, at once; novelists, innovators (people who bring in new opinions, not writers of novels); withal, at the same time; in disgrace of / disgraced, to the discredit of / discredited; still (last sentence), always.
- **scourgers** (Direction 8): kept; those who scourge, that is, afflict, the church.
- **simply** (Direction 9): kept; Baxter means "considered in itself, absolutely": some other study might in itself be better.
- **commonplace book** (Direction 10): a notebook in which a student copied quotations and notes under subject headings. **science:** kept; Baxter means any branch of learning, not only natural science.
- **And when they have done so** (Direction 10): Baxter has "And when they have done," that is, once they have made such a book; we added "so."
- **divinity** (Direction 11): kept, as elsewhere; theology. **scheme or skeleton:** an outline of the whole subject. **anatomized:** kept; dissected into its parts.
- **reducing** (Direction 12, and "reduce" in its paragraph): kept; Baxter means referring or subordinating one thing to another, ordering the lesser by the greater.
- **primitive church** (Direction 13): kept; the early church of the apostles' age.
- **Rome** (Direction 13) and **papists** (Direction 15): Baxter's polemic, kept as he wrote it. "Papist" is his word for a Roman Catholic.
- **Dominicans and Jesuits; the divine influx** (Direction 14): Baxter probably has in view the long Roman Catholic controversy over how God's grace and influence (the "influx") work with human free will and how God's decrees are ordered. We have not confirmed which writings he means. The phrase "priority, posteriority, dependence or reason of God's decrees" refers to debates about the order of God's decrees, which (we believe, but have not confirmed against sources) also divided Protestant theologians.
- **little regarded** (Direction 15): Baxter has the past tense "regarded" beside the present "study"; possibly a misprint for "regard." Kept as printed.
- **singularity** (Direction 15): kept; a wish to be different from everyone else, eccentricity.
- **from the very ground:** kept; from the foundation up.
- **be always beginning** (last sentence): Baxter leaves "would" understood ("every age and person be still beginning"); we keep his construction.
- **[319] Methodus Theologiae:** this is probably Baxter's large Latin work of theology, usually cited as *Methodus Theologiae Christianae*, which we believe appeared some years after the *Directory*; we have not confirmed the identification or the date. The note shows he was working on it after writing this Direction.
- **[320] Vincentius Lirinensis:** Vincent of Lérins (an early church writer, usually dated to the fifth century; not stated by Baxter), probably meant for his *Commonitorium*, generally known for its rule of holding what has been believed everywhere, always, and by all. That fits Directions 12 and 13, but Baxter does not name the work.
