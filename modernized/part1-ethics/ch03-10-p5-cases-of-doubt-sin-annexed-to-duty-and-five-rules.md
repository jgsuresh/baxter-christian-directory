# Chapter 3: Grand Direction X (part 5 of 6)

*This section begins partway through Direction 27 of Grand Direction 10, in the middle of Baxter's series of questions and answers on the case of duty joined with sin; it opens at Question 9 and ends partway through the rules for Question 13, whose Rule 5 is the last in this file. No new Direction heading is given here.*

*Question 9.* What if there is a great duty, which I cannot perform without committing a little sin? or, a very great good, which I cannot do except by an unlawful means; as, to save the lives of many by a lie?

*Answer.* 1. It is no duty to you, when you cannot do it without willful sin, be it never so little. Deliberately to choose a sin, that I may perform some service to God, or do some good to others, is to run before we are called, and to make work for ourselves which God never made for us; and to offer sin for a sacrifice to God; and to do evil that good may come of it; and abuse God, and reject his government, under pretense of serving him. "The wicked person's sacrifice is an abomination; how much more when he brings it with evil intent!" (Proverbs 21:27; 15:8). "The one who turns away his ear from hearing the law, even his prayer is an abomination" (Proverbs 28:9). "... draw near to listen rather than to offer a sacrifice like fools, for they do not realize that they are doing wrong" (Ecclesiastes 5:1). 2. If you will do good by sinning, you must do good in opposition to God: and how easily can he disappoint you, and turn it into evil! It is not good indeed, which must be accomplished by sin. The final good is never promoted by it; and all other good is to be estimated by its tendency to the end. You think that good which is not so, because you judge by the present feeling of your flesh, and do not foresee how it stands related to the everlasting good.

*Question 10.* Seeing then that I am sure beforehand that I cannot preach, or hear, or pray, or do any good action without sin, must I not, by this rule, forbear them all?

*Answer.* No; because your infirmities in the performance of your duty, which you would avoid and cannot, are not made the condition of your action, but are the diseases of it. They are not chosen and approved of. The duty is your duty notwithstanding your infirmities, and may be accepted of; for you cannot serve God in perfection till you are perfect; and to cast away his service is a far greater sin than to do it imperfectly. But you may serve him without such willful, chosen sin, if not in one way, yet in another. The imperfection of your service is repented of while it is committed; but so is not your approved, chosen sin. For a man to make a bargain against God, that he will commit a sin against him, though the action is the same which he has often done before in pardonable weakness; this is to turn it to a presumptuous, heinous sin. If he does it for worldly gain or safety, he sells his obedience to God for trifles. If he does it to serve God by, he blasphemes God; declaring him to be evil, and a lover of sin, or so powerless as not to be able to do good, or attain his ends by lawful means. It is most dangerous to give it in writing to the devil, that we will sin, on whatever pretense.

*Question 11.* What if I am certain that the duty is great, and uncertain whether the thing attached to it is a sin or not? Must I forbear a certain duty for an uncertain sin? or forbear doing a great and certain good, for fear of a small, uncertain evil?

*Answer.* 1. The question *de esse* must go before the question *de apparere*. Either that which you say you are uncertain of is indeed a sin, or it is none. If it is no sin, then you are bound both to search till you know that it is no sin, and not to forbear your duty for it. But if really it is a sin, then your uncertainty of it is another sin; and that which God binds you to is to forsake them both. 2. Your question contains a contradiction: you cannot be certain that it is a duty at all to you, any further than you are certain whether the condition or means is lawful or a sin. What if a hearer in Spain or Italy says, I am certain that it is a duty to obey my teachers; but I am uncertain whether their doctrines of the mass, purgatory, and the rest, have any untruth or sin in them; therefore, I must not forbear certain obedience for uncertain sin. Or if a priest among them says, I am certain that it is a duty to preach God's word, but I am not certain that the Trent Articles, which I must swear or subscribe, are sinful or false; therefore I must not leave a great and certain duty for an uncertain sin. The answer to them both is easy. 1. It is your sin that you are uncertain of the sinfulness of those things, which God has forbidden: and God commands you first to search the Scriptures, and cure that error. He made his law before your doubts arose, and will not change it because you doubt. 2. You contradict yourselves by a mistake. You have no more certainty that you should obey your teachers in these particulars, than you have that the things which they teach or command you are not against that law of God. You are certain that you must obey them in all things not forbidden by God, and within the reach of their office to require. And you are as certain that it is unlawful to obey them against the law of God, and that God must be obeyed before man. But whether you must obey them in this particular case, you cannot be certain, while you are uncertain whether it is forbidden by God. And the priest must be as uncertain whether it is any duty of his at all to preach God's word, as he is uncertain of the lawfulness of the Trent oath or subscription, unless he can do it without. If a subject says, I am certain that to govern the kingdom well is a great, good work and duty, but I am uncertain whether to depose the king if he does not govern well, and set up myself, is a sin; therefore, the certain good must overrule the uncertain evil. I give him the same answer: 1. It is your sin to be uncertain whether rebellion is a sin; and God binds you to lay by the sin of your judgment, and not to make it a shoehorn to more. 2. You are sure that governing well is a good work; but you should be as sure that it is no duty of yours, nor good work for you to do, as you are sure that you are but a private man and a subject, and never called to do the good of another's office. A private man may say, I am sure preaching is a good work; but I am not sure that a private, unordained man may not regularly separate himself to do it. But he can be no surer that it is a duty to him, than he is that he is called to it.

*Question 12.* Well, suppose my ignorance is my sin, and suppose that I am equally uncertain of the duty and of the sin attached, yet if I have done all that I am able, and remain still unresolved, and after my most diligent inquiry am as much in doubt as ever, what should I then do?

*Answer.* 1. If you had by any former sin so forfeited God's assistance, as that he will leave you to your blindness, this does not alter his law and your obligations, which are still the same (to learn, understand, and practice). 2. But if you are truly willing to understand, and practice, and use his means, you have no cause to imagine that he will thus forsake you; undoubtedly he appoints you no means in vain. If you do not attain sufficient resolution to guide you in your duty, it is either because your hearts are false in the inquiry, and biased, or unwilling to know the truth, or do it; or because you do not use the true appointed means for resolution, but in partiality or laziness neglect it.

*Question 13.* Suppose still my ignorance is my sin; which is the greater sin, to neglect the good work, or to venture on the feared evil that is attached? I am not conscious of any unfaithfulness, but human frailty, that keeps me from certainty. And no man is so perfect as to have no culpable ignorance, and to be certain in every point of duty. Therefore I must with greatest caution avoid the greatest sin, when I am out of hope of avoiding all. On one side, it is a common rule that I must do nothing against conscience (no, not a doubting conscience), though I must not always do what it commands. "For the man who doubts is condemned if he eats, because he does not do so from faith, and whatever is not from faith is sin" (Romans 14:23). On the other side, if all duty is omitted which conscience doubts of, I may be kept from almost every duty.

*Answer.* The heart is so deceitful that you have great cause to watch, lest human frailty be claimed for that error, which a corrupted, biased, partial mind, or willful laziness, is the cause of. Diligent study, and inquiry, and prayer, with a sincere desire to know the truth, may succeed, at least, to so much satisfaction as may keep your minds in quietness and peace, and give you comfort in your way, and preserve you from all such sin as is inconsistent with this your safety and acceptance with God. But yet it is true that human frailty will occasion in the best uncertainties in some particular cases; and though God does not make it our duty, of two sins, to choose the less, but to refuse both, yet he makes it our duty to avoid the greater more diligently than the less. And often the case is so sudden that no inquiry can be made: and therefore I confess a Christian should know which sins are greatest and to be most avoided. At present I shall lay down these following rules, premising this, that where accidents and circumstances which make sins great or small are to be compared, they are often so numerous and various, that no rules can be laid down beforehand that will meet every need, no more than in law and medicine any law books or medical books will serve all cases without a present experienced, judicious counselor: present prudence and sincerity must do most.

**Rule 1.** In things altogether indifferent, nothing must be done that conscience doubts of, because there is a possibility or fear of sinning on the one side, but none on the other; and in that case it is a certain sin to venture on a feared sin. But then it is supposed that the thing is indifferent as clothed with all its circumstances, and that there is no accident that takes away its indifference.

**Rule 2.** In case the thing is really unlawful, and I think it to be lawful, but with some doubting, but am clear that the forbearing it is no sin; there the sin is only in the doing it; because all is clear and safe on the other side.

**Rule 3.** There are many sins which are always and to all persons in all cases sins, and not doubted of by any without gross unfaithfulness or negligence; and here there is no room for any doubting whether we must do that good which cannot be done without that sin, it being certain that no such good can be a duty. As, to commit idolatry, to blaspheme God, to deny Christ, to deny the Scriptures, to hate, or reproach, or oppose a holy life, to be perjured, to approve or justify the sin of others, etc. It can be no duty which cannot be done without the willful yielding to or committing these or any known sin.

**Rule 4.** There are some duties so great, and clear, and constant to all, that none but a profligate or graceless conscience (or one that is fearfully poisoned with sin) can make a doubt of it deliberately: these therefore do not come within the case before us.

**Rule 5.** If moral evil is compared only with natural good, or moral good with natural evil, there is no doubt to be made of the case: the least sin having more evil in it than the prosperity or lives of millions of men have good (considered in themselves as natural good); and the least duty to God having more good in it than the death of millions of men (as such) has evil. For the good of duty and the evil of sin are increased by their relation to God, and the other is lessened as being good or evil only to men, and with respect to them.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®, fetched from labs.bible.org; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Where this begins:** the range opens at Question 9 of a series that Baxter runs under Direction 27 (the Direction named in the heading of the previous file, "Make not your own judgments or consciences your law"). The numbered questions here (9 to 13) continue earlier ones; the "Rule 1" to "Rule 5" list continues in the next file. Baxter's "Quest." is rendered "Question" and "Answ." "Answer."
- **Proverbs 21:27; 15:8:** Baxter quotes the King James of 21:27 ("The sacrifice of the wicked is an abomination to the Lord: how much more, when he bringeth it with a wicked mind?") and adds 15:8 as a parallel reference. The NET of 21:27 is used: it lacks the King James "to the Lord" and has "evil intent" for "wicked mind." The NET of 15:8 ("The Lord abhors the sacrifice of the wicked, but the prayer of the upright pleases him") carries the same thought, so no sense is lost.
- **Proverbs 28:9:** NET wording; the sense is the same as the King James ("shall be abomination" there; "is an abomination" in the NET).
- **Ecclesiastes 5:1:** Baxter quotes the King James ("Be more ready to hear, than to offer the sacrifice of fools: for they consider not that they do evil"). The NET opens differently ("Be careful what you do when you go to the temple of God"), and numbers the verse 5:1 (4:17 in the Hebrew numbering); I give only the part Baxter's quotation covers, with "..." for the opening. The NET's "draw near to listen rather than to offer a sacrifice like fools" is a close match to his point that obedient listening is better than offering a sacrifice joined with sin. The NET reads "they do not realize that they are doing wrong" for "they consider not that they do evil."
- **Romans 14:23:** Baxter's "he that doubteth is condemned if he eat: for whatsoever is not of faith is sin" is the King James. The NET has "because he does not do so from faith" and "whatever is not from faith." The difference is slight, but Baxter leans on the thought that acting against a doubting conscience is sin; the NET keeps that, and, like Baxter's quotation, speaks of the doubter who eats. Baxter's own reasoning in Question 13 treats the verse as a general rule about acting against conscience, which he then qualifies in the answer.
- **de esse; de apparere** (Question 11, Answer 1): Latin kept as printed. (English: the question of what is, about being, must go before the question of what appears, about how a thing seems. Draft translation prepared for this project; a Latinist should check it. "apparere" is printed as in the source.) Baxter's point is that whether the thing really is a sin comes before how it appears to you.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then mine): wilful, willful; pretence, pretense; hath, has; biassed, biased; practise, practice; ofttimes, often; annexed, attached; shoeing-horn, shoehorn; auditor, hearer (a lay hearer of the teachers); statedly, regularly; impotent, powerless; "under our hands," in writing; physic, medicine; counsellor, counselor; "greatened," increased; "serve all turns," meet every need; "at present I shall lay down," for now I shall lay down; "pretended," claimed; "pretence," pretense; "greatened by their respect to God," increased by their relation to God ("respect" here means relation); "christian," Christian. Baxter's "till" and "lay by" are kept as ordinary English. In Question 10 the sense of "under our hands" is a signed pledge to the devil.
- **Technical terms kept:** *profligate* (Rule 4), meaning a deeply immoral, shameless person; *indifferent* (Rule 1), things that are neither commanded nor forbidden; *natural good / natural evil* (Rule 5), good or evil in a person's welfare or life, as opposed to moral good or evil, which concerns obedience to God; *premising*, stating beforehand; *heinous*, very wicked.
- **"the Trent Articles," "the mass, purgatory"** (Question 11): Baxter's polemic against Roman Catholic teaching and practice, kept as he wrote it. "The Trent Articles" probably refers to a profession of faith or oath tied to the Council of Trent; we have not confirmed which text he means. His examples (a hearer and a priest in Spain or Italy) are illustrations of his argument, not reports of particular persons.
- **"depose the king ... and set up myself"** (Question 11): Baxter's example of a subject who argues from the good of governing well; the point is that it is not the subject's office.
- **Numbering:** Baxter's sub-points "1." and "2." are kept inside the answers. Questions 9 to 13 and Rules 1 to 5 keep his numbering. Rule 5 is the last item in this range.
- **Unmarked allusions:** "to do evil that good may come of it" (cf. Romans 3:8); given without a reference in Baxter's text, and the parallel is a suggestion only. "To run before we are called" is left as his phrase, with no reference proposed.
