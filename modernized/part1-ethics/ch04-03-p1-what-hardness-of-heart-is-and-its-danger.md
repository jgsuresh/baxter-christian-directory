# Chapter 4: Directions against Hardness of Heart (part 1: what it is, Directions 1-5)

**Directions against Hardness of Heart.**

It is necessary that some Christians be better informed what hardness of heart is, who most complain of it. [143] The metaphor is taken from the hardness of any matter which a workman would make an impression on; and it signifies the passive and active resistance of the heart against the word and works of God, when it does not receive the impressions which the word should make, and does not obey God's commands; but after great and powerful means remains as it was before, unmoved, unaffected, and disobedient. So that hardness of heart is not a distinct sin, but the habitual power of every sin, or the deadness, unmovableness, and obstinacy of the heart in any sin. So many duties and sins as there are, so many ways may the heart be hardened against the word, which forbids those sins, and commands those duties. It is therefore an error, that has had very ill consequences on many persons, to think that hardness of heart is nothing but a want of emotional feeling in the matters which concern the soul; especially a want of sorrow and tears. This has made them over-careful for such tears, and grief, and emotions, and dangerously to make light of the many greater instances of the hardness of their hearts. Many beginners in religion (who are taken up in penitential duties) do think that all repentance is nothing but a change of opinion, unless they have those emotional griefs, and tears, which indeed would well become the penitent; and hereupon they take more pains with themselves to affect their hearts with sorrow for sin, and to wring out tears, than they do for many greater duties. But when God calls them to love him, and to praise him, and to be thankful for his mercies; or to love an enemy, or forgive a wrong: when he calls them to mortify their earthly-mindedness, their carnality, their pride, their passion, or their disobedience, they yield but little to his call, and show here much greater hardness of heart, and yet little complain of this or take notice of it. I entreat you therefore to observe that the greater the duty is, the worse it is to harden the heart against it; and the greater the sin is, the worse it is to harden the heart by obstinacy in it. And that the great duties are the love of God and man, with a mortified and heavenly mind and life; and to resist God's word commanding these is the great and dangerous hardening of the heart. The life of grace lies, 1. In the preferring of God, and heaven, and holiness, in the estimation of our minds before all worldly things. 2. In the choosing them, and resolving for them with our wills, before all others. 3. In the seeking of them in the bent and drift of our efforts. These three make up a state of holiness. But for strength of parts, or memory, or expression, and so for emotional affections of sorrow, or joy, or the tears that express them; all these in their time, and place, and measure, are desirable, but not of necessity to salvation, or to the life of grace. They follow much the temperament of the body, and some have much of them that have little or no grace, and some want them that have much grace. The work of repentance consists most in loathing and falling out with ourselves for our sins, and in forsaking them with abhorrence, and turning to God; and he that can do this without tears is truly penitent, and he that has never so many tears, without this, is impenitent still. [144] And that is the hard-hearted sinner, who will not be wrought to a love of holiness, nor let go his sin, when God commands him; but after all exhortations, and mercies, and perhaps afflictions, is still the same as if he had never been admonished, or took no notice what God has been saying or doing to reclaim him. Having thus told you what hardness of heart is, you may see that I have given you directions against it at large before, chapter 3, directions 6 and 8; but shall add these few.

**Direction 1. Remember the majesty and presence of that most holy God, with whom we have to do (Hebrews 4:13).** Nothing will more affect and awe the heart, and overrule it in the matters of religion, than the true knowledge of God. We will not talk sleepily or contemptuously to a king; how much less should we be stupid or contemptuous before the God of heaven! It is that God whom angels worship, who sustains the world, who keeps us in life, who is always present, observing all that we think, or say, or do, whose commands are upon us, and with whom we have to do in all things; and shall we be hardened against his fear? "Who has resisted him and remained safe?" (Job 9:4).

**Direction 2. Think well of the unspeakable greatness and importance of those truths and things which should affect you, and of those duties which are required of you.** Eternity of joy or torment is such an amazing thing, that one would think every thought, and every mention either of it or of anything that concerns it, should go to our very hearts, and deeply affect us, and should command the obedience and service of our souls. It is true, they are things unseen, and therefore less apt in that respect to affect us than things visible; but the greatness of them should compensate for that disadvantage a thousandfold. If our lives lay upon every word we speak, or upon every step we go, how carefully should we speak and go! But oh how deeply should things affect us, in which our everlasting life is concerned! One would think a thing of so great moment, as dying, and passing into an endless life of pain or pleasure, should so take up and transport the mind of man, that we should have much ado to bring ourselves to mind, regard, or talk of the inconsiderable interests of the flesh! How inexcusable a thing is a senseless, careless, negligent heart, when God looks on us, and heaven or hell is a little before us! Yes, when we are so heavily laden with our sins, and surrounded with so many enemies, and in the midst of such great and manifold dangers, to be yet senseless under all, is (so far) to be dead. Will not the wounds of sin, and the threatenings of the law, and the accusations of conscience, make you feel? He that cannot feel the prick of a pin will feel the stab of a dagger, if he is alive.

**Direction 3. Remember how near the time is, when stupidity and senseless neglect of God will be banished from all the world; and what certain and powerful means are before you at death and judgment, to awaken and pierce the hardest heart.** [145] There are but few that are quite insensible at death; there are none past feeling after death, in heaven or hell. No man will stand before the Lord in the day of judgment, with a sleepy or a senseless heart. God will recover your feeling by misery, if you will lose it by sin, and not recover it by grace. He can make you now a terror to yourselves (Jeremiah 20:4); he can make conscience say such things in secret to you, as you will not be able to forget or slight. But if conscience does not awaken you, the approach of death it is likely will awaken you: when you see that God is now in earnest with you, and that die you must, and there is no remedy, will you not begin to think now, Where must I go? and what will become of me forever? Will you then harden your heart against God and his warnings? If you do, the first moment of your entrance upon eternity will cure your stupidity forever. It will grieve a heart that is not stone, to think what a feeling stony-hearted sinners will shortly have, when God will purposely make them feel, with his wrathful streams of fire and brimstone! when Satan who now hinders your feeling, will do his worst to make you feel; and conscience, the never-dying worm, will gnaw your hearts, and make them feel, without ease or hope of remedy! Think what a waking day is coming!

**Direction 4. Think often of the love of God in Christ, and of the bloody sufferings of your Redeemer, for it has a mighty power to melt the heart.** If love, and the love of God, and so great and wonderful a love, will not soften your hardened heart, what will?

**Direction 5. Labor for a full apprehension of the evil and danger of a hardened heart.** It is the death of the soul, so far as it is powerful: at the easiest, it is like the stupidity of a paralyzed limb or a seared part. Observe the names which Scripture gives it: The "hardening of the heart" (Proverbs 28:14). The "hardening of the neck" (Proverbs 29:1), which signifies inflexibility. The "hardening of the face," which signifies shamelessness (Proverbs 21:29). The "searedness of the conscience" (1 Timothy 4:2). The "impenitence of the heart" (Romans 2:5). Sometimes it is called "sottishness," or "stupidity" (Jeremiah 4:22). Sometimes it is called a "not caring," or "not laying things to heart, and not regarding" (Isaiah 42:25; 5:12; 32:9–11). Sometimes it is named metaphorically from inanimate things: "A face harder than a rock" (Jeremiah 5:3). "Hearts of stone" (Ezekiel 11:19; 36:26). "A neck with an iron sinew" (Isaiah 48:4), and "a brow of brass." It is called "sleep," and a "deep slumber," and a "spirit of stupor" (Romans 13:11; 11:8; Matthew 25:5); and "death" itself (1 Timothy 5:6; Ephesians 2:1, 5; Colossians 2:13; Jude 12).

Observe also how dreadful a case it is, if it is predominant, both symptomatically and effectively. It is the forerunner of mischief (Proverbs 28:14). It is a dreadful sign of one that is far more unlikely than others to be converted; when they are "alienated from the life of God because of the ignorance that is in them," and are "callous," they "have given themselves over to indecency for the practice of every kind of impurity with greediness" (Ephesians 4:18–19). Usually God calls those that he will save, before they are past feeling; though such are not hopeless, their hope lies in the recovering of the feeling which they want; and a hardened heart, and iron neck, and brazen forehead, are a sadder sign of God's displeasure, than if he had made the heavens as brass, and the earth as iron to you, or let out the greatest distress upon your bodies. When men have eyes and see not, and ears and hear not, and hearts but understand not, it is a sad prognostic that they are very unlikely to "repent and be forgiven" (Mark 4:12; Acts 28:27). A hardened heart (predominantly) is garrisoned and fortified by Satan against all the means that we can use to help them; and none but the Almighty can cast him out and deliver them. Let husband, or wife, or parents, or the dearest friends entreat a hardened sinner to be converted, and he will not hear them. Let the most learned, or wisest, or holiest man alive, both preach and beseech him, and he will not turn. At a distance he may reverence and honor a great divine, and a learned or a holy man, especially when they are dead; but let the best man on earth be the minister of the place where he lives, and entreat him daily to repent, and he will either hate and persecute him, or neglect and disobey him. What minister was ever so learned, or holy, or powerful a preacher, that had not sad experience of this? when the prophet cries out, "Who would have believed what we just heard?" (Isaiah 53:1), and the apostles were obliged to shake off the dust of their feet against many that rejected them; and were abused, and scorned, and persecuted by those whose souls they would have saved? Nay, Jesus Christ himself was refused by the most that heard him; and no minister dares compare himself with Christ. If our Lord and Master was blasphemed, scorned, and murdered by sinners, what better should his ablest ministers expect? St. Augustine found drunkenness so common in Africa, that he proposed that a council might be called for the suppression of it; but if a general council of all the learned bishops and pastors in the world were called, they could not convert one hardened sinner, by all their authority, wit, or diligence, without the power of the Almighty God. For will they be converted by man, that are hardened against God? What can we devise to say to them that can reach their hearts, and get within them, and do them good? Shall we tell them of the law and judgments of the Lord, and of his wrath against them? why all these things they have heard so often until they sleep under it, or laugh at them. Shall we tell them of death, and judgment, and eternity? why we speak to the posts, or men asleep; they hear us as if they heard us not. Shall we tell them of endless joy and torments? they feel not, and therefore fear not, nor regard not; they have heard of all these, until they are weary of hearing them, and our words seem to them but as the noise of the wind or water, which has no meaning. If miracles were performed among them by a preacher, that healed the sick, and raised the dead, they would wonder at him, but would not be converted. For Christ did thus, and yet prevailed but with few (John 11:48, 53); and the apostles performed miracles, and yet were rejected by the most (Acts 7:57; 22:22). Nay, if one of their old companions should be sent from the dead to give them warning, he might frighten them, but not convert them, for Christ has told us so himself (Luke 16:31); or if an angel from heaven should preach to them, they would be hardened still, as Balaam and others have been. Christ rose from the dead, and yet was after that rejected. We do not read of the conversion of the soldiers that watched his tomb, though they were frightened with the sight of the angels: but they were after that hired for a little money to lie, and say that Christ's disciples stole him away. If magistrates that have power over their bodies should endeavor to bring them to godliness, they would not obey them, nor be persuaded. King Hezekiah's messengers were but mocked by the people. David and Solomon could not convert their hardened subjects. Punish them, and hang them, and they will be wicked to the death: witness the impenitent thief that died with Christ, and died reproaching him. Though God afflicts them with rod after rod, yet still they sin and are the same (Psalm 78; Hosea 7:14; Amos 4:9; Jeremiah 5:3; Isaiah 1:5). Let death come near and look them in the face, and let them see that they must at once go to judgment, it will frighten them, but not convert them. Let them know and confess that sin is bad, that holiness is best, that death and eternity are at hand, yet are they the same, and all will not win their hearts to God; till grace takes away their stony hearts, and gives them tender, fleshy hearts (Ezekiel 36:26).

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). Every quoted verse below was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Heading and structure:** "Directions against Hardness of Heart" is Baxter's section heading, rendered in bold. His "Direct. I." to "V." become Directions 1 to 5. In the source the Direction's lead sentence is not marked off from the rest; we bold the first sentence as a heading, as in the finished files. This range ends with Direction 5; the next file continues with the next Direction.
- **Hardness of heart / hardening:** Baxter's definition (a resistance of the heart "passive and active") is kept as written.
- **Chapter reference** ("chap. iii. direct. vi. and viii."): kept as Baxter's cross-reference to his own earlier directions, rendered "chapter 3, directions 6 and 8". We have not checked it.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): *Christians* (printed lowercase); *passionate* to *emotional* (feeling, griefs, affections), per the glossary; *passions* to *emotions* (in "grief, and emotions"; where "passion" is listed as a sin, "their passion," it is kept); *endeavours* to *efforts*; *temperature of the body* to *temperament of the body* (his sense is the bodily constitution; we have not confirmed the older technical sense); *compass* ("compassed about") to *surrounded*; *recompense* to *compensate for*; *Labour* to *Labor*; *member* ("paralytic member") to *limb*; *paralytic* to *paralyzed*; *impudency* to *shamelessness*; *impenitency* to *impenitence*; *denominated metaphorically from inanimates* to *named metaphorically from inanimate things*; *presently* to *at once*; *fain* ("were fain to shake off") to *obliged*; *motioned* to *proposed*; *signification* to *meaning*; *wrought* ("wrought miracles") to *performed*; *sepulchre* to *tomb*; *affrighted / affright* to *frightened / frighten*; *prevailed* kept, as the sense (to succeed) is clear. *Want* ("want of", "some want them", meaning lack) and *wrought* ("be wrought to a love," meaning brought) are kept; the sense is clear. *Whither* to *Where*; *except* ("except they have") to *unless*; *that* to *who* where it refers to a person ("that sustaineth", "that is always present", "that will not be wrought"); *for ever* to *forever*; *the learnedest* to *the most learned*.
- **Words kept, with their sense:** *strength of parts* (natural abilities); *wit* (intelligence, cleverness); *stupidity* (dullness, being unfeeling); *insensible* (unfeeling); *predominant, predominantly* (ruling, in control); *symptomatically and effectively* (as a sign of other things, and in what it does); *sottishness* (a dull, foolish stupor); *searedness* (a seared, burned-out condition); *a full apprehension* (understanding); *unspeakable* (inexpressible); *much ado* (much trouble); *divine* (Baxter's word for a theologian); *religion; mortify; carnality; earthly-mindedness* (concern for earthly things). *Direct.* is rendered "Direction."
- **Job 9:4:** NET reads "Who has resisted him and remained safe?" The King James (which Baxter echoes) has "Who hath hardened himself against him, and hath prospered?" Baxter's argument in Direction 1 turns on the word "hardened," which the NET does not have; the NET's "resisted" gives the same general point (nobody who stands against God comes out safe) but not the link to "hardened" that the sentence before relies on.
- **Jeremiah 20:4:** not in quotation marks in Baxter ("a terror to yourselves"); we keep his words. The NET reads "I will make both you and your friends terrified of what will happen to you." The sense (terror) is much the same.
- **The list of names in Direction 5** is Baxter's own set of labels, mostly nouns made from the verbs and phrases in the verses, so we keep his wording in his quotation marks and give the NET wording here. Proverbs 28:14: "whoever hardens his heart will fall into evil." Proverbs 29:1: "The one who stiffens his neck after numerous rebukes." Proverbs 21:29: "A wicked person has put on a bold face." 1 Timothy 4:2: "consciences are seared." Romans 2:5: "your unrepentant heart" (the NET also has "stubbornness"). Jeremiah 4:22: "foolish"; "no sense." Isaiah 42:25: "they did not take it to heart." Jeremiah 5:3: "hardheaded as a rock" (the NET speaks of the head, not the face; Baxter's "face" follows the King James). Isaiah 48:4: "neck muscles are like iron and your forehead like bronze" (Baxter's "iron sinew" and "brass" are the King James words). Ezekiel 11:19 and 36:26: "hearts of stone" / "heart of stone"; we use the NET wording "Hearts of stone" in the quotation.
- **Sleep, slumber, death:** for the Scripture names of hardness, "sleep," "deep slumber," and "death" are Baxter's single words. "spirit of slumber" in Baxter corresponds to Romans 11:8 where the NET reads "a spirit of stupor," so we use "spirit of stupor." This matters a little, because "stupor" is close to Baxter's own word "stupidity," while "slumber" suggests sleep only. Romans 13:11 (NET): "awake from sleep." 1 Timothy 5:6 (NET): "is dead even while she lives." We have not fetched or checked Matthew 25:5, Ephesians 2:1, 5, Colossians 2:13 or Jude 12 beyond keeping Baxter's references.
- **Proverbs 29:10 corrected to Proverbs 29:1:** Baxter prints "Prov. xxix. 10" in Direction 5, but the words about the hardened neck are at Proverbs 29:1 (and Baxter's own note [143] lists "xxix. 1"). We use 29:1; the printed "10" may be a misprint.
- **Ephesians 4:14 corrected to Ephesians 4:18–19:** Baxter's printed "Eph. iv. 14" does not contain the words he quotes; they are at Ephesians 4:18–19. In the NET: "alienated from the life of God because of the ignorance that is in them"; "callous"; "they have given themselves over to indecency for the practice of every kind of impurity with greediness." Two shifts matter. First, Baxter's "past feeling" is the King James word for the NET's "callous"; Baxter uses "past feeling" again in his own prose in the next sentence and in Direction 3, so the connection to the quotation is easier to see in the King James than here. Second, Baxter's "given up to work uncleanness" (the King James has "have given themselves over unto lasciviousness, to work all uncleanness with greediness") and the NET "have given themselves over" are both active; Baxter's "they are 'given up'" reads as a passive in his sentence, as if God gave them up, which the NET does not say. We keep his sentence with the NET wording and drop his "are" for the NET's "have."
- **Mark 4:12; Acts 28:27:** Baxter's "converted and forgiven" is the King James sense ("be converted, and their sins should be forgiven"). The NET of Mark 4:12 reads "repent and be forgiven"; we use that, and "repent" and "be converted" are close, but Baxter's word is about conversion as a change of the whole life, and the NET's "repent" is narrower. Acts 28:27 in the NET ends "turn, and I would heal them." Baxter's "hearts but understand not" and "eyes ... see not" are his own wording from these verses and are not in quotation marks.
- **Isaiah 53:1:** Baxter's "Who hath believed our report?" is the King James. The NET reads "Who would have believed what we just heard?" The NET reads the "report" as what the speakers have just heard (a message they find hard to believe), which keeps Baxter's point that few believe it.
- **Ezekiel 36:26 (last sentence):** not in quotation marks, so Baxter's words ("stony hearts," "tender, fleshy hearts") stay. The NET of Ezekiel 36:26 reads "heart of stone" and "heart of flesh"; "tender hearts" is from Ezekiel 11:19. Baxter blends the two.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "make the heavens as brass, and the earth as iron" (probably Leviticus 26:19 or Deuteronomy 28:23; not confirmed); "eyes and see not, and ears and hear not" (a scriptural phrase whose source we have not identified); "shake off the dust of their feet" (Matthew 10:14); "the never-dying worm" (Mark 9:48; Isaiah 66:24); "fire and brimstone." The identifications are ours, not Baxter's.
- **Historical claims:** Baxter's statements about Augustine and drunkenness in Africa, the soldiers at the tomb, the impenitent thief, Balaam, and the messengers of King Hezekiah are his own and are kept as he wrote them. We have not checked them. For the soldiers and the "little money," compare Matthew 28:11–15; for the messengers, 2 Chronicles 30:10; these two references are ours and are not in Baxter's text.
- **Augustine, *City of God*** (note 144): the sense is that being patiently in misery is not the same as being blessed. **Valerius Maximus** (note 145): the reference is as printed ("1. 1. c. 2.", where the first "1." is probably "l.", for *liber*, book); we have not checked the book and chapter.
