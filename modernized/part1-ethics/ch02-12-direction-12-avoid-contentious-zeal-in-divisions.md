# Chapter 2: Direction 12

**Direction 12. If controversies cause any divisions where you live, be sure to look first to the interest of common truth and good, and to the exercise of charity.**

And do not become emotional contenders for any party in the division, or harsh critics of the peaceable, or of your teachers, who will not overrun their own understandings to gain with you the esteem of being orthodox or zealous men; but suspect your own unripe understandings, and silence your opinions until you are clear and certain; and join rather with the moderate and the peacemakers than with the contenders and dividers.

You can easily be sure that division tends to the ruin of the church, and the hindrance of the gospel, and the injury of the common interest of religion. [66] You know it is greatly condemned in the Scriptures. You can know that it is usually the exercise and the increase of pride, uncharitableness, and passion; and that the devil is best pleased with it, as being the greatest gainer by it. But, on the other side, you are not easily certain which party is in the right: and if you were, you are not sure that the matter will be worth the cost of the contention: or if it is, it is to be considered whether the truth is not likely to gain more advantage by handling it in a more peaceable way, that has no contention, and does not stir up other people so much against it as the way of controversy does. And whatever it turns out to be, you can and should know that young Christians, who lack abilities, and helps, and time, and experience to be thoroughly versed in controversies, are very unfit to make themselves parties; and that they are even more unfit to be the hottest leaders of these parties, and to spur on their teachers, who know more than they. If the work is fit for another to do, who knows on what ground he goes, and can foresee the end, yet certainly it is not fit for you. And therefore hold back from it until you are more fit.

I know those who would draw you into such a contentious zeal will tell you that their cause is the cause of God, and that you desert him and betray it if you are not zealous in it: and that it is only the counsel of flesh and blood that makes you claim moderation and peace: and that it is a sign that you are hypocrites, who are so lukewarm, and carnally comply with error: and that the cause of God is to be followed with the greatest zeal and self-denial. And all this is true, if you are only sure that it is indeed the cause of God; and that the greater works of God are not neglected on such pretexts; and that your zeal is much greater for faith, and charity, and unity than for your opinions. But from great experience, I must tell you that of the zealous contenders [67] in the world who proclaim "The cause of God, and truth," there is not one in very many who understands what he talks of; but some of them proclaim the cause of God when it is a brat of a proud and ignorant brain, and such as a judicious person would be ashamed of. And some of them are rashly zealous before they have the abilities or the time to come to any judicious trial. And some of them are misguided by some person or party that captivates their minds. And some of them are hurried away by passion and discontent. And many of the ambitious and worldly are blinded by their carnal interests. And many of them, in mere pride, think highly of an opinion in which they are somewhat distinctive, and which they can, with some glorying, call their own, as either invented by them or as one in which they think they know more than ordinary people do. And abundance, after long experience, confess that to have been their own erroneous cause, which they before labeled the cause of God. Now when this is the case, and one cries, Here is Christ, and another, There is Christ; one says, This is the cause of God, and another says, That is it; no one who has any care for his conscience, or for the honor of God and his profession, will leap before he looks where he will land; or run after everyone who will whistle him with the name or claim of truth or a good cause. It is a sad thing to go on many years together in condemning, opposing, and abusing those who are against you, and in seducing others, and misusing your zeal, and abilities, and time, and poisoning all your prayers and conversations, and in the end to see what mischief you have done for want of knowledge, and with Paul to confess that you were mad in opposing the truth and servants of God, though you did it in a zeal of God through ignorance. Would it not be much better to wait until you have tried the ground, and prevent so many years' grievous sin, than to escape by a sad repentance, and leave behind you stinking and venomous fruit of your mistake? and worse, if you never repent. Your own and your fellow believers' souls are not so lightly to be risked on dangerous, untried ways. It will not make amends to the truth and the church to say at last, I had thought I had done well. Let those go to the wars of disputing, and contending, and condemning, and siding with a sect, who are riper, and understand the cause better: wars are not for children. Suspend your judgment until you can inform it solidly and certainly, and serve God in charity, quietness, and peace; and it is two to one that you will live to see the day when the contenders who would have led you into their wars will come off with so much loss themselves as will teach them to approve your peaceable course, or teach you to bless God who kept you in your place and duty.

In all this I do not deny that every truth of God is to be valued at a very high rate; and that he who carries himself in a neutrality when faith or godliness is the matter in controversy, or does it merely for his worldly ends, to save his stake by temporizing, is a false-hearted hypocrite, with no religion at heart. But along with this I tell you that not everything is a matter of faith or godliness that the autonomian-papist, the antinomian-libertine, or other emotional parties will call so: and that as we must avoid contempt of the smallest truth, so we must much more avoid the most heinous sins that we may commit in defending an error: and that some truths must be silenced for a time, though not denied, when contending for them is untimely, and tends to the injury of the church. If you were teachers in the church, you must not teach your pupils to their hurt, though it is truth you teach them. And if you were physicians, you must not cram them, or medicate them to their hurt. Your power and duty is not to destruction, but to edification. The good of the patient is the end of your medicine. All truth is not to be spoken, nor all good to be done, by all people, nor at all times. He who will do the contrary, and take this for a carnal principle, only calls folly and sin by the name of zeal and duty, and sets the house on fire to roast his egg, and, with the Pharisees, prefers the outward rest of their Sabbath before his brother's life or health. Be careful what you do when God's honor, and people's souls, and the church's peace are concerned in it.

And let me tell you my own observation. As far as my judgment has been able to reach, the men who have stood for pacification and moderation have been the most judicious, and those who have best understood themselves, in most controversies that I ever heard debated among good Christians: and those who furiously condemned them as lukewarm or corrupted have been men who had the least judgment, and the most passion, pride, and foul mistakes in the points in question.

No, I will tell you more of my observation, of which these times have given us too much proof. Profane and formal enemies on the one hand, and ignorant, self-conceited wranglers on the other hand, who think they are champions for the truth when they are venting their passions and foolish opinions, are the two thieves between whom the church has suffered from the beginning to this day. The first are the persecutors, and the other the dividers and disturbers of the church. Mark what the Holy Spirit says in this case, 2 Timothy 2:23-24, "But reject foolish and ignorant controversies because you know they breed infighting. And the Lord's slave must not engage in heated disputes but be kind toward all." Philippians 2:14-15, "Do everything without grumbling or arguing, so that you may be blameless and pure, children of God without blemish though you live in a crooked and perverse society, in which you shine as lights in the world." 1 Timothy 6:3-6, "If someone spreads false teachings and does not agree with sound words (that is, those of our Lord Jesus Christ) and with the teaching that accords with godliness, he is conceited and understands nothing, but has an unhealthy interest in controversies and verbal disputes. This gives rise to envy, dissension, slanders, evil suspicions, and constant bickering by people corrupted in their minds," etc. So 1 Timothy 1:4-5, "...nor to occupy themselves with myths and interminable genealogies. Such things promote useless speculations rather than God's redemptive plan that operates by faith. But the aim of our instruction is love that comes from a pure heart, a good conscience, and a sincere faith."

Yet I must declare here that if any false-hearted, worldly hypocrite, who resolves to be on the saving side, and to hold all to be lawful that seems necessary to his safety or promotions, takes any encouragement from what I have said here to corrupt his conscience, and sell his soul, and then call all those furious zealots who will not be as false to God as he, let that man know that I have given him no cloak for so odious a sin, nor will he find a cover for it at the bar of God, though he may deceive his conscience, and carry it off by his carnal advantages before the world.

## Baxter's notes

The Latin is reproduced as printed in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; these translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so a Latinist should check them before anyone relies on fine points.

[66] Stoici dicunt cum nemine stultorum esse litigandum: omnesque stultos insanire. Laert. in Zenone. (English: The Stoics say that one should not quarrel with any of the foolish, and that all fools are mad. Laertius, in [the life of] Zeno.)

[67] Consuming zeal is accustomed at last to burn up the owners of it. Whatever they say or do against others in their intemperate violence, they teach others at last to say and do against them, when they have opportunity. How the orthodox taught the Arians to use severity against them may be seen in Victor. Utic. p. 447-449, in the edict of Hunnerychus: Legem quam dudum Christiani Imperatores nostri contra eos et alios hæreticos pro honorificentia ecclesiæ catholicæ dederunt, adversus nos illi proponere non erubuerunt, v. g. Rex Hun. &c. Triumphalis et majestatis regiæ probatur esse virtutis, mala in autores consilia retorquere: quisquis enim pravitatis aliquid invenerit, sibi imputet quod incurret.--Nullos conventus homousion sacerdotes assumant, nec aliquid mysteriorum, quæ magis polluunt, sibi vendicent. Nullam habeant ordinandi licentiam.--Quod ipsarum legum continentia demonstratur quas induxisse imperatoribus, &c. viz. Ut nulla exceptis superstitionis suæ antistibus ecclesia pateret; nullis liceret aliis aut convictus agere, aut exercere conventus nec ecclesias, aut in urbibus, aut in quibusdam minimis locis. (English, rendered loosely: The law which our Christian emperors long ago gave against them and other heretics for the honor of the catholic church, they have not been ashamed to set against us, e.g. King Hun[neric], etc. It is judged a mark of triumphal and royal majesty to turn evil counsels back upon their authors; for whoever has found some depravity, let him blame himself for what he will incur.--Let the homoousian priests hold no assemblies, nor claim for themselves any of the mysteries, which rather pollute. Let them have no license to ordain.--Which is shown by the content of the laws themselves, which they [had brought upon] the emperors, etc., namely, that no church should stand open except to the leaders of their own superstition; that it should be lawful for no others either to [hold gatherings; "convictus agere" is obscure] or to hold assemblies or churches, whether in cities or in some very small places.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). All four quotations were fetched from the NET text, not quoted from memory. Baxter's references are converted to modern form.
- **Opening paragraph:** the whole first paragraph is Baxter's statement of the Direction. We set its first sentence in bold as the heading and kept the rest of the paragraph as the first body paragraph; no sentence was split or moved.
- **2 Timothy 2:23-24 (NET flags):** Baxter's King James reads "foolish and unlearned questions avoid, knowing that they do gender strifes. And the servant of the Lord must not strive; but be gentle unto all men." The NET has "controversies" for "questions," "infighting" for "strifes," "heated disputes" for "strive," and "the Lord's slave" for "servant of the Lord." "Controversies" fits his theme well (his chapter is about controversy). The NET also has "ignorant" for "unlearned" and "kind" for "gentle." Baxter's quotation stops at "all men"; the NET continues "an apt teacher, patient," which we left out to keep his extent.
- **Philippians 2:14-15 (NET flags):** the King James has "murmurings and disputings," "harmless," "sons of God," and "without rebuke." The NET has "grumbling or arguing," "pure," "children of God," and "without blemish"; for "generation" it has "society." "Disputings" becoming "arguing" keeps his point; the other changes do not affect it.
- **1 Timothy 6:3-6 (NET flags):** the King James has "If any man teach otherwise," "wholesome words," "proud," "doting about questions and strifes of words," "railings," "evil surmisings," and "perverse disputings of men of corrupt minds." The NET has "spreads false teachings" (sharper than "teach otherwise"), "sound words," "conceited," "an unhealthy interest in controversies and verbal disputes," "slanders," "evil suspicions," and "constant bickering by people corrupted in their minds." The change from "proud" to "conceited" and from "questions" to "controversies" keeps Baxter's sense. His "etc." ("&c.") marks that he stops mid-sentence; the NET continues "and deprived of the truth..."
- **1 Timothy 1:4-5 (NET flags):** the NET text begins "nor to occupy themselves with" because it continues a sentence that began in verse 3 and speaks of "them" in the third person; Baxter's King James is an imperative ("Neither give heed to"). More importantly, the King James has "which minister questions, rather than godly edifying, which is in faith"; the NET has "useless speculations rather than God's redemptive plan that operates by faith." Baxter's argument turns on edification (he says later that a teacher's power is "to edification"), and the NET's "God's redemptive plan" loses that word. The King James "charity" is "love" in the NET; Baxter's own prose keeps "charity," as he uses it throughout.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording; no references added in the text): "Here is Christ, and... There is Christ" (compare Matthew 24:23, we have not checked the NET wording); Paul's confession that he was "mad" in opposing the faith, "in a zeal of God through ignorance" (compare Acts 26:11 and Romans 10:2); "the two thieves" between whom Christ was crucified (compare Luke 23:32-33); the Pharisees preferring their Sabbath to a person's health (compare Luke 13:14 and Matthew 12:9-14).
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): passionate (parties, contenders) to emotional; censurers to harsh critics; censured/censuring to condemned/condemning; parts to abilities; want (lack) to lack; seen in (skilled in) to versed in; forbear to hold back from; pretend to claim; pretences to pretexts; cry up to proclaim; pretence (of truth) to claim; singular to distinctive; entitled to labeled; misemploying to misusing; discourses to conversations; brethren's to fellow believers'; managing to handling; delude to deceive; bear it out to carry it off; masters (teachers) to teachers, and scholars to pupils; physic to medicine; fond (opinions) to foolish; preferments to promotions; debauch to corrupt; Holy Ghost to Holy Spirit; honour to honor; Take heed to Be careful; hath/doth/-eth forms to modern forms. We also adjusted "Nay" to "No" and kept every sentence boundary.
- **Technical or period terms kept:** *orthodox*; *zeal, zealous* (here a loaded word, since Baxter attacks "zeal" that serves party and pride; the NET Scripture quotations do not use the word); *carnal* (see GLOSSARY.md); *uncharitableness* (lack of charity, love toward others); *judicious*; *temporizing* (adapting one's views to the times for personal gain); *neutrality*; *edification* (building up in faith); *libertine*.
- **"overrun their own understandings":** left as written; Baxter appears to mean teachers who will not go beyond what they actually understand to win a name for being orthodox or zealous.
- **"saving side"** (last paragraph): left as written; it means the side that will save him, that is, the safe side.
- **"autonomian-papist" and "antinomian-libertine":** Baxter's labels for opposite kinds of extremists. *Antinomian* means one who holds that believers are freed from the moral law; *papist* is Baxter's word for a Roman Catholic. We have not confirmed what he means by "autonomian" and leave the term as written. This is his polemic, kept as he wrote it.
- **"profane and formal enemies":** "formal" means given to outward forms without inner life (compare *formalists* in GLOSSARY and earlier files); kept.
- **"a brat of a proud and ignorant brain":** Baxter's blunt phrase, kept.
- **"two to one":** an odds expression (likely, more probable than not); kept.
- **Note 66:** "Laert." is presumably Diogenes Laertius (Lives of the Philosophers), the life of Zeno; we have not checked the passage. The Latin "litigandum" literally concerns going to law or quarreling.
- **Note 67:** Baxter's own claims about history (the orthodox teaching the Arians to use severity, and the edict of "Hunnerychus") are kept as his and not confirmed here. "Victor. Utic." is probably a work of Victor of Vita (a historian of the Vandal persecution in North Africa); "Hunnerychus" is probably the Vandal king Huneric; both identifications are unconfirmed. The Latin "antistibus" is printed in the source; it is probably a misprint for "antistitibus" (leaders, bishops). "homousion" is as printed (apparently the Greek *homoousios*, "of the same substance"). The final clause is obscure; "convictus agere" is rendered loosely, as the source may be damaged. In the English of this note, "doth use at last to burn up" is rendered "is accustomed at last to burn up."
- **Margin notes:** none in this range.
