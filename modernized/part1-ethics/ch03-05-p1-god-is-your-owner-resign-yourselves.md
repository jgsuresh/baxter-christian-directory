# Chapter 3: Grand Direction 5 (God is your Lord and Owner)

**Grand Direction 5. Remember that God is your Lord or Owner: and see that you make an absolute resignation of yourselves, and all that you have, to him as his own; and use yourselves and all accordingly; trust him with his own; and rest in his disposals.**

Of this I have already spoken in my "Sermon of Christ's Dominion," and in my *Directions for a Sound Conversion*; and therefore must only touch it here. It is easy, in theory, to know and say that God is our Owner, and we are not our own; but if the habitual, practical knowledge of it were as easy, or as common, the happy effects of it would be the sanctification and reformation of the world. I shall first tell you what this duty is, and how it is to be performed; and then, what fruits and benefits it will produce, and what should move us to it.

I. The duty lies in these acts: 1. That you consider the ground of God's ownership of you; (1.) In making you out of nothing, and preserving you. (2.) In redeeming you by purchase. (3.) In regenerating you, and renewing you for himself. [94] The first is the ground of his common natural ownership of you and all things. The second is the ground of his common gracious ownership of you and all men, as purchased by Christ (Romans 14:9; John 13:3). The third is the ground of his special gracious ownership of you, and all his sanctified, special people. Understand and acknowledge what a full dominion God has over you, and how absolutely and wholly you are his. 2. Let it exceedingly please you to think that you are wholly his: it being much better for you, as to your safety, honor, and happiness, than to be your own, or anyone else's. 3. As God requires it in his covenant of grace, that he have his right by your consent, and not by constraint; so you must thankfully accept the offer, and with hearty and full consent of will, resign yourselves to him, as his own, even as his creatures, his ransomed ones, and his regenerate children, by a covenant never to be violated. 4. You must carefully watch against the claims and reservations of carnal selfishness; lest while you confess you are God's, and not your own, you should secretly still keep possession of yourselves against him, or take back the possession which you surrendered. 5. You must use yourselves ever after as God's, and not your own.

II. In this using yourselves as wholly God's lies both your further duty, and your benefits. 1. When God's ownership is discerned and consented to, it will make you aware of how you are obliged to employ all your powers of soul and body in his service; and to perceive that nothing should be taken away from him, no creature having any equal claim to a thought of your hearts, or a glance of your affection, or a word of your mouths, or a minute of your time. The sense of God's ownership must cause you to keep constant accounts between God and you; and to call yourselves to a frequent reckoning, whether God has his own, and you do not defraud him; whether it is his work that you are doing, and for him that you think, and speak, and live? And all that you have will be used as his, as well as yourselves; for no man can have any good thing that is more his own than he is his own himself.

2. Ownership, once discerned, endears us in affection to our owner. As we love our own children, so they love their own fathers. Even our dogs love their own master better than another. When we can say with Thomas, "My Lord and my God!" it will certainly be the voice of love. God's common ownership of us, as his created and ransomed ones, obliges us to love him with all our heart; but the knowledge of his special ownership, by regeneration, will more effectively command our love.

3. God's ownership, once perceived, will help to satisfy us of his love and care for us: and will help us to trust him in every danger; and so take away our excessive fear, and anxieties, and caring for ourselves. [95] The apostle proves Christ's love for his church from his ownership: "For no one has ever hated his own body" (Ephesians 5:29). God is not careless of his own. As we take care of our livestock, to preserve them, and provide for them, more than they do for themselves, for they are more ours than their own; so God is more concerned in the welfare of his children, than they are themselves, they being more his than their own. Why are we afraid of the wrath and cruelty of man? Will God be mindless and negligent of his own? Why are we over-careful and distrustful of his providence? Will he not take care of his own, and make provision for them? "May God, our God, bless us" (Psalm 67:6). God's interest in his church, and cause, and servants, is an argument which we may plead with him in prayer (1 Chronicles 17:21-22), and with which we may greatly encourage our confidence: "For the sake of my reputation I hold back my anger; for the sake of my prestige I restrain myself from destroying you. ... For my sake alone I will act, for how can I allow my name to be defiled? I will not share my glory with anyone else!" (Isaiah 48:9, 11). "Now, this is what the Lord says, the one who created you, O Jacob, and formed you, O Israel: 'Don't be afraid, for I will protect you. I call you by name, you are mine. When you pass through the waters, I am with you; ...'" (Isaiah 43:1-2). If God should neglect our interest, he will not neglect his own.

4. God's ownership of us, once discerned, so greatly increases the guilt of our sin against him that it should greatly restrain us, and further our humbling and recovery when we have fallen: "You must be holy to me because I, the Lord, am holy, and I have set you apart from the other peoples to be mine" (Leviticus 20:26). "I swore a solemn oath to you and entered into a marriage covenant with you, declares the Sovereign Lord, and you became mine" (Ezekiel 16:8), when he is aggravating Jerusalem's sin. "...and you are not your own? For you were bought at a price. Therefore glorify God with your body" (1 Corinthians 6:19-20). Justice requires that everyone have his own.

5. It should silence all murmuring and complaining against the providence of God, to consider that we are his own. Does he afflict you? and are you not his own? Does he kill you? are you not his own? As a Ruler, he will show you reason enough for it in your sins; but as your absolute Lord and Owner, he need not give you any other reason than that he may do with his own as he wishes. It is not possible that he can do any wrong to that which is absolutely his own. If he deny you health, or wealth, or friends, or take them from you; he denies you, or takes from you, nothing but his own. Indeed, as a Governor and a Father, he has assured the faithful of eternal life: otherwise, as their Owner, he could not have wronged them, if he had made the most innocent as miserable as he is capable of being. Do you work hard, and beat, and kill your livestock, because they are your own (by an imperfect ownership)? and do you dare resent God for afflicting his own, when their consciences tell them that they have deserved it and much more?

And so that you may not think that you have resigned yourselves to God entirely, when you only hypocritically profess it, observe: 1. That man is not thus resigned to God who thinks any service too much for God that he can do. 2. Nor he who thinks any cost too great for God that he is called to undergo. 3. Nor he who thinks that all is won (of his time, or wealth, or pleasure, or anything) which he can save or steal from God: for all is lost that God has not. 4. Nor he who must needs be the one to dispose of himself, and his condition and affairs, and God must indulge him, and accommodate his providence to his carnal interest and will, or else he cannot bear it, or think well of it. 5. Remember that all that is bestowed in sin upon God's enemies is used against him, and not as his own. 6. And that he who hides his talent, or does not use it at all, cannot be said to use it for God. Both idleness and withdrawing the gifts of God are a robbing him of his own.

III. To help you in this work of self-resignation, consider often: 1. That if you were your own, you would be most miserable. You could not support, preserve, or provide for yourselves: who should save you in the hour of temptation and distress? Alas! if you are humbled Christians, you know so much of your own insufficiency, and feel yourselves such a daily burden to yourselves, that you have had quite enough of yourselves by now: and beg of God, above all your enemies, to save you from yourselves; and of all judgments, to save you from being forsaken by God, and given up to yourselves. 2. Remember that none in the world has sufficient power, wisdom, and goodness to take the full care and charge of you, except God; none else can save you, or sanctify you, or keep you alive one hour: and therefore it is your happiness and honor that you are his. 3. His right is absolute, and none has any right to you but he; none else created you, redeemed you, or regenerated you. 4. He will use you only in safe and honorable services, and for no worse an end than your endless happiness. 5. What you deny him, or steal from him, you give to the devil, the world, and the flesh; and do they deserve it better? 6. You are his own in title, whether you will or not; and he will fulfill his will upon you. Your consent and resignation is necessary to your good, to ease you of your cares, and secure you from present and eternal misery.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source. The English translations of Latin that follow in parentheses are drafts prepared for this project, not from a published edition, and need a Latinist's check.

[94] Persuasum hoc sit à principio hominibus, dominos esse omnium rerum ac moderatores Deos: eaque quæ gerantur eorum geri ditione atque numine--Et qualis quisque fit, quid agat, quid in se admittat, qua mente, qua pietate colat religionem, intueri, piorumque et impiorum habere rationem. Cicero 2. de Leg. (English: Let people be persuaded from the beginning that the gods are the lords and governors of all things, and that what is done is done by their authority and divine will--and that the gods look at what sort of person each one is, what he does, what he admits into himself, with what mind and with what piety he practices religion, and keep an account of the pious and the impious. Cicero, book 2 of *On the Laws*.)

[95] Deorum providentia mundus administratur, iidemque consulunt rebus humanis neque solum universis, verum etiam singulis. Cicero 1. de Divin. (English: The world is governed by the providence of the gods, and the same gods look after human affairs, not only for all together but also for individuals. Cicero, book 1 of *On Divination*.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®, fetched from labs.bible.org; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). Baxter's references are converted to modern form.
- **Heading.** The source heading is "_Grand Direct._ V."; this file begins at the top of Grand Direction 5 and ends with the margin note that opens the next section (on subjection to God as Governor), which belongs to text after line 10155.
- **Ephesians 5:29:** Baxter (King James) has "No man ever yet hated his own flesh"; the NET has "his own body." His point (that people look after what is their own) is the same, but he is leaning on "own" and on the likeness to Christ's care of the church. The NET renders the Greek word usually translated "flesh" as "body" here, by sense.
- **Psalm 67:6:** Baxter's "God, even our own God, shall bless us" is a statement; the NET reads as a wish or prayer ("May God, our God, bless us"). He uses it as an assurance of care for what is God's own, so the NET is weaker as a promise. The NET also runs the previous clause ("The earth yields its crops") into the verse, which Baxter does not quote.
- **Isaiah 48:9, 11:** Baxter's text is King James and condenses. He has "defer mine anger ... refrain for thee, that I cut thee not off" where the NET has "hold back my anger ... restrain myself from destroying you." His "for my praise" is the NET's "for the sake of my prestige." The sense he draws (God acts for his own name) is the same. I use "..." where Baxter's quotation moves from verse 9 to verse 11 (he runs "for mine own sake, even for mine own sake" from verse 11, while the NET has "For my sake alone").
- **Isaiah 43:1-2:** Baxter's "for I have redeemed thee" is the King James. The NET has "for I will protect you." This matters, because Baxter's whole section builds God's ownership on redemption by purchase; in this verse the NET removes the word "redeemed." His "&c." after "I will be with thee" is rendered as "...".
- **Leviticus 20:26:** King James "have severed you from other people" is the NET's "have set you apart from the other peoples."
- **1 Corinthians 6:19-20:** Baxter quotes through "and in your spirit, which are God's." The NET text ends at "glorify God with your body" and has no "and in your spirit, which are God's" (the longer reading is the one the King James follows; we have not checked the manuscript evidence). Baxter leans on it to say that soul as well as body is God's; his sense is kept in the surrounding text ("all your powers of soul and body"), but the quoted words are not in the NET here.
- **Ezekiel 16:8:** the NET adds "marriage" before "covenant" (Baxter has just "a covenant").
- **Romans 14:9; John 13:3; 1 Chronicles 17:21-22:** Baxter gives these as references only, with no quotation, so none is quoted. **"My Lord and my God!"** (Thomas) is given without a reference by Baxter; it is John 20:28 (not in Baxter's text), NET wording.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "no man can have any good thing that is more his own" (no clear source); "he that hideth his talent" (the parable of the talents, Matthew 25:14-30); "the devil, the world, and the flesh" (the traditional trio of temptations).
- **ownership / propriety:** Baxter's "propriety" means ownership, property right, and I use "ownership" throughout. **full dominion:** his "plenary." **aware:** his "sensible." **equal claim:** his "co-ordinate title." **excessive:** his "inordinate." **complaining:** his "repinings." **as he wishes:** his "as he list." **indulge:** his "humour." **resent:** his "grudge at." **withdrawing:** his "alienating." **livestock:** his "cattle." **special people:** his "peculiar people" (with the older sense of "belonging to him alone"). **careless of:** his "regardless of." **assured:** his "secured." **offer:** his "motion." **take back:** his "re-assume." **humbling:** his "humiliation." **sanctified:** kept.
- **Kept as Baxter's terms:** carnal; regenerate; sanctify; providence; talent (the sum of money in the parable, used for gifts or abilities).
- **Word choices kept close to Baxter:** "man/men" is kept where he means people generally; "must only touch it here" keeps his omitted "I."
- **Sentence-level notes.** The sentence "Do you work hard, and beat, and kill your livestock..." reads as a rhetorical question; kept as asked. In III.6 "whether you will or not" means "whether you are willing or not." In item 3 the sentence "God's ownership, once perceived, will help..." was "God's propriety perceived, will help..." in the source; I have added "once," with commas, to carry the participle, and changed no other structure. The same applies to items 2 and 4.
- **"Sermon of Christ's Dominion":** Baxter's own earlier work, not checked here. ***Directions for a Sound Conversion*:** his title, in italics as in other files.
- **Cicero** (notes 94 and 95): Baxter cites Cicero's *De Legibus* (book 2) and *De Divinatione* (book 1). The translations are drafts. In [94] the Latin "Persuasum hoc sit" is a command ("let this be accepted"); "numine" is "divine will."
- **"he deny you"** (II.5): Baxter's subjunctive; kept as "he deny you" in a conditional clause, though modern English would say "denies."
