# Chapter 8: Against drunkenness, questions 4–15

*This file begins partway through section V of Part 4, Title 2 (against drunkenness), Baxter's series of questions put to the drunkard before his directions, at Question 4; Questions 1–3 are in the previous file, and the series continues in the next file.*

*Question 4.* Tell me, do you believe that the holy Scripture is true? If you do not, no wonder if you are a drunkard. [445] But if you do, remember that then it is true, that "drunkards ... will not inherit the kingdom of God" (1 Corinthians 6:10). And then mark what the Scripture says, "The splendid crown of Ephraim's drunkards is doomed" (Isaiah 28:1). "Woe to you who force your neighbor to drink wine—you who make others intoxicated by forcing them to drink from the bowl of your furious anger" (Habakkuk 2:15). "Beware, those who get up early to drink beer, those who keep drinking long after dark until they are intoxicated with wine. They have stringed instruments, tambourines, flutes, and wine at their parties. So they do not recognize what the Lord is doing, they do not perceive what he is bringing about" (Isaiah 5:11–12). "Beware, those who are champions at drinking, who display great courage when mixing strong drinks" (Isaiah 5:22). "It is not for kings, O Lemuel, it is not for kings to drink wine or for rulers to crave strong drink, lest they drink and forget what is decreed and remove from all the poor their legal rights. Give strong drink to the one who is perishing and wine to those who are bitterly distressed" (Proverbs 31:4–6). See Amos 6:6. "But be on your guard so that your hearts are not weighed down with dissipation and drunkenness and the worries of this life, and that day close down upon you suddenly like a trap" (Luke 21:34). "Not in carousing and drunkenness, not in sexual immorality and sensuality, not in discord and jealousy. Instead, put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh to arouse its desires" (Romans 13:13–14). "Wine is a mocker and strong drink is a brawler; whoever goes astray by them is not wise" (Proverbs 20:1). "Who has woe? Who has sorrow? Who has contentions? Who has complaints? Who has wounds without cause? Who has dullness of the eyes? Those who linger over wine, those who go looking for mixed wine. Do not look on the wine when it is red, when it sparkles in the cup, when it goes down smoothly. Afterward it bites like a snake and stings like a viper. Your eyes will see strange things, and your mind will speak perverse things. And you will be like one who lies down in the midst of the sea, and like one who lies down on the top of the rigging" (Proverbs 23:29–34). "Old and new wine take away the understanding of my people" (Hosea 4:11). "Wake up, you drunkards, and weep! Wail, all you wine drinkers," etc. (Joel 1:5). If you do indeed believe the word of God, why do such passages not make you tremble?

*Question 5.* Do you consider into how dangerous a case you put yourself when you are drunk, or join yourself with drunkards? What abundance of other sins you are liable to? And in what peril you are of some present judgment of God? Even those examples in Scripture which encourage you should make you tremble. To think that even a Noah that was drunk but once is recorded to his shame for a warning to others. How horrid a crime even Lot fell into by the temptations of drunkenness! How Uriah was made drunk by a David to have hidden his sin! (2 Samuel 11:13). How David's son Amnon, in God's just revenge, was murdered by his brother Absalom's command, when "his heart was merry with wine" (2 Samuel 13:28). How Nabal was struck dead by God after his drunkenness (1 Samuel 25:36–38). How king Elah was murdered as he was drinking himself drunk (1 Kings 16:9). And how the terrible hand appeared writing upon the wall to king Belshazzar in his carousing, to signify the loss of his kingdoms, and that very night he was also slain (Daniel 5:1, 30). You see God does not spare kings themselves, that one would think might be allowed more pleasure: and will he spare you? "It is not for kings, O Lemuel, it is not for kings to drink wine or for rulers to crave strong drink" (Proverbs 31:4–5); and is it then for you? Mark the dreadful fruits of it even to the greatest. "The royal advisers delight the king with their evil schemes; the princes make him glad with their lies. They are all like bakers; they are like a smoldering oven ... At the celebration of their king, his princes become inflamed with wine; they conspire with evildoers" (Hosea 7:3–5). You see that be they great or small, both soul and body are cast by tippling and drunkenness into greater danger than you are in at sea in a raging tempest. You put yourself in the way of the vengeance of God, and are not likely to escape it long.

*Question 6.* Did you ever measure your sin by that strange kind of punishment commanded by God against incorrigible gluttons and drunkards? "If a person has a stubborn, rebellious son who pays no attention to his father or mother, and they discipline him to no avail, his father and mother must seize him and bring him to the elders at the gate of his city. They must declare to the elders of his city, 'Our son is stubborn and rebellious and pays no attention to what we say—he is a glutton and drunkard.' Then all the men of his city must stone him to death. In this way you will purge wickedness from among you, and all Israel will hear about it and be afraid" (Deuteronomy 21:18–21). Surely gluttony and drunkenness are heinous crimes, when a man's own father and mother were bound to bring him to the magistrate to be put to death, if he will not be reformed by their own correction. And you see here that youth is no excuse for it, though now it is thought excusable in them.

*Question 7.* Do you think your drink is too good to leave at God's command? Or do you think that God grudges you the sweetness of it? or rather that he forbids it to you for your good, that you may escape the hurt. And tell me, do you love God better than your drink and pleasure, or do you not? If not, your own conscience must tell you (if you have a conscience not quite seared) that there is no hope of your salvation in that state: but if you say you do, will God, or any wise man, believe you, that you love him better, and will not be so far ruled by him, nor leave so small a matter for his sake? "For this is the love of God: that we keep his commandments. And his commandments do not weigh us down" (1 John 5:3). So 2 John 6.

*Question 8.* Do you remember that your carcass must lie rotting in the grave, and how loathsome a thing it must shortly be? And can you make so great a matter of the present satisfying of so vile a body, and dung the earth at so dear a rate?

*Question 9.* Would you have all your friends and children do as you do? If so, what would become of your estate? It would be a mad world if all were drunkards. Would you have your wife a drunkard? If she were, you would scarcely be confident of her chastity. Would you have your servants drunkards? If they were, they might set your house on fire: and they would do you little work, or do it so that it would be better undone. Your house would be a bedlam if all were drunkards; and much worse than bedlam; for there are some wise men to govern and correct the mad ones. But if you do not like it in wife, and children, and servants, why do you continue it yourself? Are you not nearest to yourself? Do you love any others better than yourself? Would you rather your own soul were damned than theirs? or can you more easily endure it? I have wondered sometimes to observe some drunkards very severe against the same sin in their children, and very desirous to have them sober! But the reason is, because the sobriety of their children is no trouble to them, nor puts them to deny the pleasure of their appetites, as their own sobriety must do.

*Question 10.* Would you have your physician drunk when he should cure you of your sickness? or your lawyer drunk when he should plead your cause? or the judge when he should judge it? If not, why will you be drunk when you should serve your God and mind the business of your soul? If you would not have your servant be tippling in an alehouse when he should be about your work, will you sit tippling and chattering there, when you have a thousandfold greater work to do for your everlasting happiness?

*Question 11.* If anyone only lames or spoils your beast, and makes him unfit for your service, would you be pleased with it? And will you unfit yourself for the service of God, as if your work were of less concern than your beast's?

*Question 12.* Would it please you if your servants poured all that drink in the gutter? If not, I have before proved to you that it should displease you more to pour it into your belly: for you will find at last that it will hurt you more.

*Question 13.* What relish does your pleasant liquor have the next day? Will it then be any sweeter than wholesome abstinence? All the delight is suddenly gone: there is nothing left but the slime in your guts, and the ulcer in your conscience, which cannot be cured by all your treasure, nor palliated long by all your pleasure. And can you value much so short delights? As all your sweet and merry cups are now no sweeter than if they had been wormwood; so all the rest will quickly come to the same end and relish. As Plato said of his slender supper, compared to a rich man's feast, Yours seems better tonight, but mine will be better tomorrow; so your conscience tells you that temperance and holy obedience will be better tomorrow, and better to eternity, though gluttony and drunkenness seem better now.

*Question 14.* Do you consider how dearly you pay for hell? and buy damnation at a harder rate than salvation might be attained at? What shame it costs you! What sickness it is likely to cost you! What painful vomitings or worse you undergo! How much you suffer in your estate! And is hell worth all this ado?

*Question 15.* Do you not think in your heart that sober, temperate, godly men live a more quiet and comfortable life than you, as well as a more honest and safer life? If you do think so, why will you not imitate them? It is as free for you to choose as them. If you think they do not, consider that as they have none of your forbidden cups, so they have none of your thirst or desire after them. Abstinence is much sweeter to them. [446] They have none of your sour belchings, or vomitings, nor shame, nor danger, nor your bill to pay. They have none of your gripes of conscience, and terrors under the guilt of such a sin. They live in the love of God and the forethoughts of heaven, while you are in the alehouse. And do you not think in your conscience that to a heart that is suited and sanctified to it, it is not a sweeter thing to live in the love of God than in the love of your indulgence in physical pleasure? Dare you say (whatever you think) that God, and heaven, and holiness are not so lovely and fit to be delighted in as a cup of wine or ale? Surely you dare not say so! If it were for no more than the different aspects of death and eternity to them and to you, I account your life in the midst of your pleasures incomparably more sad than theirs. They look at death as at the time of hope, and the day of their deliverance, as the assizes are to the innocent or pardoned man: but you look on death with terror, as the end of all your mirth, as the guilty criminal thinks on the assizes; or else with senselessness or presumption, which is worse. They look to eternity as their endless, unspeakable happiness; and you scarcely dare seriously think of it, without the deluding ease of unbelief or of false hopes: you dare not seriously look beyond death, unless through the devil's cheating spectacles. I tell you, a sober, godly man would not have your merry life (as you account it) one day, for all your wealth, or for any worldly gain: he would rather lie in jail, or sit in the stocks that while, than drink and swagger with you. Keep your merriment to yourself, for no wise man or good man will be your partner. If you were their enemy, they would not wish you so much misery as you choose. As the story goes of a confessor, that hearing many confess the sin of drunkenness, insisted on trying for himself what pleasure was in it: and having vomited and slept it out, the next drunkard that came to him in confession, he appointed him for penance to be drunk again, and told him he needed no sharper penance.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows the Latin in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[445] Id sane magno Christianis opprobio est, Ingam Regem barbarum et idolis deditum ab ebrietate subditos sibi populos cohibuisse; nostros vero quos oportebat mores quoque perditos emendare, temulentiæ incrementa tanta fecisse. Acosta l. 3. c. 21. (It is truly a great reproach to Christians that the Inca, a barbarous king given over to idols, restrained the peoples subject to him from drunkenness, while our own people, who ought to have amended even corrupted morals, have brought about such increases of drunkenness. Acosta, book 3, chapter 21.)

[446] He is happiest who needs least of any creature, and not he who has most. Socrates said it was proper to God only to need nothing, but those that came nearest to God in this were the happiest men.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). All NET wording was fetched from labs.bible.org. Spaces lost between poetic lines in the fetched text were restored.
- **Labels:** this file sits in Part 4, Title 2 (against drunkenness). "Tit." in the source is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," a heading or division within a Part. "V." is Baxter's section number within Title 2: questions he puts to the drunkard "before the directions"; the questions keep his numbers.
- **Isaiah 28:1 (corrected reference):** the source prints "Isa. xxi. 1," but Isaiah 21:1 is an oracle about "the wilderness by the Sea"; the words Baxter quotes are Isaiah 28:1. The King James begins "Woe to the crown of pride"; the NET reads "The splendid crown of Ephraim's drunkards is doomed," so the "woe" that links this verse to the next ones Baxter quotes becomes "is doomed."
- **Habakkuk 2:15:** NET wording, to the point where Baxter's quotation stops (he omits the clause about looking on their nakedness). The King James "puttest thy bottle to him" is in the NET "forcing them to drink from the bowl of your furious anger," which reads the drinking as a figure of God's wrath poured on others. Baxter uses the verse of literally making a neighbor drunk; the NET's opening ("force your neighbor to drink wine ... make others intoxicated") still carries that sense, but the added "furious anger" shifts it. Flagged.
- **Isaiah 5:11–12, 22:** Baxter cites "Isa. v. 11" but quotes verses 11–12; the reference is given as 11–12. His "Ver. 22" is given as Isaiah 5:22. The NET has "Beware" for the King James "Woe," "beer" for "strong drink," "do not recognize what the Lord is doing" for "regard not the work of the Lord," and "champions at drinking ... great courage when mixing strong drinks" for "mighty to drink wine ... men of strength to mingle strong drink." The sense Baxter uses is unchanged.
- **Proverbs 31:4–6:** NET wording. The NET adds "O Lemuel," has "rulers to crave strong drink" for "princes strong drink," "forget what is decreed" for "forget the law," and "remove from all the poor their legal rights" for "pervert the judgment of any of the afflicted." In Question 5 Baxter cites 31:4–5 but quotes only verse 4; the reference is kept as he gives it, and verse 4 is quoted in the NET wording. His point ("is it then for you?") rests on "not for kings," which the NET keeps.
- **Luke 21:34:** NET wording. "Dissipation" stands for the King James "surfeiting" (overindulgence in food and drink), and "weighed down" for "overcharged"; the NET's "dissipation" is less clearly about eating to excess.
- **Romans 13:13–14:** Baxter's quotation begins "Not in gluttony and drunkenness"; the King James has "rioting and drunkenness," so "gluttony" is his own word or a slip. The NET has "carousing and drunkenness," "sexual immorality and sensuality" for "chambering and wantonness," and "discord and jealousy" for "strife and envying." We quote the NET from "not in carousing," keeping the extent Baxter quotes. Baxter's "gluttony" is lost. Flagged.
- **Proverbs 20:1:** NET wording. "Brawler" for "raging," and "whoever goes astray by them" for "whosoever is deceived thereby." Baxter's word "deceived" (the drunkard as one fooled) becomes "goes astray."
- **Proverbs 23:29–34:** Baxter cites "29-32," but his quotation runs through verse 34; the reference is given as 29–34. NET differences: "dullness of the eyes" for "redness of eyes," "linger over wine" for "tarry long at the wine," "goes down smoothly" for "moveth itself aright," "snake ... viper" for "serpent ... adder," "top of the rigging" for "top of a mast." Most important: the King James "Thine eyes shall behold strange women" is in the NET "Your eyes will see strange things," so the link between drink and lust that the King James makes here is gone. Flagged.
- **Hosea 4:11:** the King James reads "Whoredom, and wine, and new wine take away the heart." The NET verse 11 reads "Old and new wine take away the understanding of my people," without "whoredom"; the NET appears to take that word with the end of verse 10 ("by pursuing other gods"). Baxter's coupling of whoredom and wine is lost. Flagged.
- **Joel 1:5:** NET wording; Baxter's "&c." is rendered "etc."
- **2 Samuel 13:28:** Baxter quotes the King James "his heart was merry with wine." The NET has "When Amnon is drunk," which says the same thing but does not contain Baxter's words and would not fit his sentence, so Baxter's wording is kept in his quotation marks.
- **1 Kings 16:9 (corrected reference):** the source prints "1 Sam. xvi. 9," which is about Jesse's son Shammah; the account of king Elah murdered while "drinking heavily" (NET) is 1 Kings 16:9.
- **Hosea 7:3–5:** NET wording, with "..." where Baxter's "--" skips the rest of verse 4. Two differences matter. The King James "they are all adulterers" is "They are all like bakers" in the fetched NET text. And the King James "the princes have made him sick with bottles of wine" (the king made sick by drink, which is Baxter's "dreadful fruits of it even to the greatest") is "his princes become inflamed with wine," so in the NET it is the princes, not the king, who are drunk. "He stretched out his hand with scorners" is "they conspire with evildoers." Flagged.
- **Deuteronomy 21:18–21:** NET wording. The NET has "person" and "son"; Baxter's remark that "youth is no excuse" rests on "son," which the NET keeps. Baxter's "magistrate" is his word for the King James "elders"; kept.
- **1 John 5:3:** NET wording. The NET sentence continues into verse 4 ("because everyone who has been fathered by God conquers the world"), so the quotation is closed with a period. "Do not weigh us down" for "are not grievous." **2 John 6** and **Amos 6:6, 1 Samuel 25:36–38, 2 Samuel 11:13, Daniel 5:1, 30** are cited only.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): Noah's drunkenness (Genesis 9:20–23); Lot (Genesis 19:30–36); "a conscience ... seared" (compare 1 Timothy 4:2). Baxter's statement that Noah "was drunk but once" is his own reading.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): drunken → drunk; stricken → struck; like (likely) → likely; must needs → must; potting → tippling (drinking from pots, sitting over drink); prating → chattering; ale-house → alehouse; channel → gutter (the street gutter); one (do but lame) → anyone; but (only) → only; concernment → concern; reckoning → bill (the drinking bill at the alehouse); honester → more honest; thereto → to it; sensuality → indulgence in physical pleasure; malefactor → criminal; delusory → deluding; felicity → happiness; would needs try → insisted on trying; to have hid → to have hidden.
- **Words kept:** present judgment (a judgment of God in this life, now, rather than at the last day); dung the earth (to become manure); at so dear a rate (at so high a price); bedlam (a madhouse; the word comes from the name of a London hospital for the insane); relish (taste, savor); palliated (eased without being cured); wormwood (a bitter herb, a byword for bitterness); gripes of conscience (pangs); forethoughts (thinking ahead); assizes (periodic sessions of the courts, where criminals were tried; Baxter's term, kept); that while (for that time); appointed him for penance (assigned him as a penance); ado (fuss); tippling (habitual small drinking).
- **"it is not a sweeter thing"** (Question 15): "do you not think ... that ... it is not a sweeter thing to live in the love of God" has a negative that seems to work against Baxter's point (he means that it *is* sweeter). Kept as printed; it may be a slip or a loose rhetorical double negative.
- **"or rather that he forbids it to you for your good, that you may escape the hurt."** (Question 7): printed with a period, though it continues a question; kept.
- **"as the guilty criminal thinks on the assizes":** Baxter's "thinketh on" means thinks about; kept.
- **Plato's supper; the confessor's story:** the anecdotes are Baxter's; we have not traced their sources. "Confessor" here is a priest who hears confessions.
- **Note 445:** "opprobio" is printed for *opprobrio* (reproach), probably a misprint; kept as printed. "Ingam" is the Latin accusative of "Inga," the Inca king. Acosta is probably José de Acosta, the Jesuit writer on the native peoples of the Americas; we have not checked which of his works "l. 3. c. 21" refers to.
- **Note 446:** the saying attributed to Socrates is Baxter's report; not traced.
