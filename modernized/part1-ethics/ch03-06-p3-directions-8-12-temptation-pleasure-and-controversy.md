# Chapter 3: Grand Direction 6 (part 3 of 3)

*This range begins partway through Grand Direction 6, at its Direction 8, and runs to the end of that Grand Direction. It closes with the margin note for the next Grand Direction, whose heading falls outside this range.*

**Direction 8. If you are unavoidably cast upon strong temptation, take the alarm; and put on all the armor of God, and call up your souls to watchfulness and resolution, remembering that you are now among your enemies, and must resist as for your lives.**

Take every temptation in its naked, proper sense, as coming from the devil, and tending to your damnation by enticing your hearts from your subjection to God: suppose you saw the devil himself in his instruments offering you the bait of preferment, or honor, or riches, or fleshly lust, or amusements, or of delightful foods, or drinks, to tempt you to excess; and suppose you heard him say to you plainly, Take this for your salvation; sell me for this your God, and your soul, and your everlasting hopes; commit this sin, that you may fall under the judgment of God, and be tormented in hell with me forever. Do this to please your flesh, that you may displease your God, and grieve your Savior: I cannot draw you to hell, but by drawing you to sin; and I cannot make you sin against your will; nor undo you, but by your own consent and doing: therefore I beg you to consent and do it yourself, and let me have your company in torments. This is the naked meaning of every temptation: suppose therefore you saw and heard all this, with what detestation then would you reject it! with what horror would you flee from the most enticing bait! If a robber would entice you out of your way and company, with flattering words, that you might fall into the hands of his companions, if you knew all his meaning and design beforehand, would you be enticed after him? Watch therefore, and resolve when you know beforehand the design of the devil, and what he intends in every temptation.

**Direction 9. Be most suspicious, fearful, and watchful about that which your flesh most desires, or finds the greatest pleasure in.**

Not that you should deny your bodies all delight in the mercies of God: if the body has none, the mind will have the less: mercy must be distinguished from punishment; and must be valued and relished as mercy: mere natural pleasing of the senses is in itself no moral good or evil. A holy use of lawful pleasure is a daily duty: excessive pleasure is a sin: all is excessive that tends more to corrupt the soul, by enticing it to sin, and turning it from God, than to fit and dispose it for God and his service, and preserve it from sinning. But still remember, it is not sorrow but delight that draws the soul away from God, and is the flesh's interest which it sets up against him. Many have sinned in sorrows and discontents; but none ever sinned for sorrows and discontents: their discontents and sorrows are not taken up and loved for themselves; but are the effects of their love to some pleasure and content, which is denied them, or taken from them. Therefore, though all your bodily pleasures are not sin, yet, seeing nothing but the pleasures of the flesh and carnal mind is the end of sinners, and the devil's great and chiefest bait, and this alone causes men's ruin, you have great reason to be most afraid of that which is most pleasing to your flesh, and to the mind as it is corrupt and carnal: escape the delusions of fleshly pleasure, and you escape damnation. You have far more cause to be afraid of prosperity, than of adversity; of riches, than of poverty; of honor, than of obscurity and contempt; of men's praises and applause, than of their criticisms, slanders, and reproach; of preferment and greatness, than of a low and humble condition; of a delicious, than of less tempting foods and drinks; of elaborate, costly, than of plain, and cheap, and simple clothing. Let those who have hired out their reason to the service of their fleshly lusts, and have delivered the crown and scepter to their appetites, think otherwise. No wonder if they who have sold the birthright of their intellects to their senses, for a bowl of stew, for a whore, or a high place, or a domineering power over others, or a bellyful of pleasant foods or drinks, do deride all this, and think it but a gloomy notion, more suitable to a hermit or anchorite, than to men of society and business in the world. As heaven is the portion of serious believers and mortified saints alone, so it shall be proper to them alone to understand the doctrine and example of their Savior, and practically to know what it is to deny themselves, and forsake all they have, and take up their cross and follow Christ, and by the Spirit to mortify the deeds of the body (Luke 14:26-29, 33; Romans 8:5-7, 13; Colossians 3:1-4). Such know that millions part with God for pleasures, but none for griefs: and that hell will be stocked with those who preferred wealth, and honor, and amusements, and gluttony, drink and filthy lusts, before the holiness and happiness of believers; but none will be damned for preferring poverty, and disgrace, and abstinence, hunger and thirst, and chastity before them. It must be something that seems good, that must entice men from the chiefest good: apparent evil is no fit bait for the devil's hook. Men will not displease God, to be themselves displeased; nor choose present sorrows instead of everlasting joys; but for the pleasures of sin for a season many will despise the endless pleasures.

**Direction 10. Meet every impulse to disobedience with an army of holy graces; with wisdom, and fear, and hatred, and resolution, with love to God, with zeal and courage; and quench every spark that falls upon your hearts before it breaks out into a flame.**

When sin is little and in its infancy, it is weak and easily resisted; it has not then turned away the mind from God, nor quenched grace, and disabled it to do its office. But when it is grown strong, then grace grows weak, and we want its help, and want the sense of the presence, and attributes, and truths of God, to rebuke it. O do not wait until your hearts are gone out of hearing, and strayed from God beyond the notice of his calls. The habit of obedience will be dangerously weakened, if you do not quickly resist the acts of sin.

**Direction 11. Labor for the clearest understanding of the will of God, so that doubtfulness about your duty does not make you flag in your obedience, and doubtfulness about sin does not weaken your detestation and resistance, and draw you to venture on it.**

When a man is sure what his duty is, it is a great help against all temptations that would take him off: and when he is sure that a thing is sinful, it makes it the easier to resist. And, therefore, it is the devil's method to delude the understanding, and make men believe that duty is no duty, and sin is no sin; and then no wonder if duty is neglected, and sin committed: and therefore he raises up one false prophet or other to say to Ahab, "Go, and prosper;" or to say, There is no harm in this; to argue for sin, and to argue against duty. And it is almost unbelievable how much the devil has gained, when he has once made it a matter of controversy. Then every hypocrite has a cloak for his sin, and a dose of opium for his conscience, when he can but say, It is a controversy; some are of one mind, and some of another, you are of that opinion, and I am of this: especially if there are wise and learned on both sides; and yet more, if there are religious men on both sides; and more yet, if he has an equal number on his side; and most of all, if he has the majority vote (as error and sin commonly have in the world). If Ahab has but four hundred lying, flattering prophets to one Micaiah, he will think he may hate him, reproach him, and persecute him without any scruple of conscience. If it is made a controversy whether bread is bread, and wine is wine, when we see and taste it; some will think they may venture to subscribe or swear that they hold the negative, if their reputation, or livelihoods, or lives depend on it; much more if they can say, It is the judgment of the church. If it is once made a controversy, whether perjury is a sin, or whether a vow that is lawful in itself binds, or whether it is lawful to equivocate, or lie with a mental reservation for the truth, or to do the greatest evil, or speak the falsest thing with a true and good intent and meaning, almost all the hypocrites in the country will be for the sinful side, if their fleshly interest requires it; and will think themselves wronged, if they are called hypocrites, liars, or perjured, as long as it is but a point of controversy among learned men. If it is once made a controversy, whether an excommunicate king becomes a private man, and it is lawful to kill him, and whether the pope may absolve the subjects of temporal lords from their allegiance, (notwithstanding all their oaths,) and if such learned men as Suarez, Bellarmine, Perron, etc. are for it, (to say nothing of Santarellus, Mariana, etc.) you shall have a Clement, a Ravilliac, a Faux, indeed, too great a choice of instruments, that will be satisfied to strike the blow. If many hold it may or must be done, some will be found too ready to do it: especially if an approved general council (Lateran. sub Innoc. III. Can. 3. [English: the Lateran Council under Innocent III, canon 3.]) is for such papal absolution. We have seen at home how many will be emboldened to pull down government, to sit in judgment on their king, and condemn him, and to destroy their brethren, if they can but say that such and such men think it lawful. If it were but a controversy once whether drunkenness, whoredom, swearing, stealing, or any villainy is a sin or not, it would be committed more commonly, and with much less regret of conscience. Indeed, good men will be ready to think that modesty requires them to be less censorious of those who commit it, because in controverted cases they must suspect their own understandings, and allow something to the judgment of dissenters. And so all the rules of love, and peace, and moderation, which are required in controversies that are about small and difficult points, the devil will make use of, and apply them all to the defense of the most odious sins, if he can but get them once to have some learned, wise, religious offenders. And from our tenderness toward the persons we easily slide to an indulgent tenderness in judging the sin itself: and good men themselves, by these means, are dangerously disabled to resist it, and prepared to commit it.

**Direction 12. Be careful that the devil does not cast you either into the sleep of carnal security, or into such doubts, and fears, and perplexing scruples, as shall make holy obedience seem to you an impossible or a tiresome thing.**

When you are asleep in carelessness, he can use you as he pleases; and if obedience is made grievous and unpleasant to you, your heart will go against it, and you will go but like a tired horse, no longer than you feel the spur: you are half conquered already, because you have lost the love and pleasure of obedience; and you are still in danger that difficulties will quite tire you, and weariness make you yield at last. The means by which the tempter accomplishes this must be spoken of afterward, and therefore I shall omit it here.

By the faithful practice of these directions obedience may become, as it were, your nature, a familiar, easy, and delightful thing; and may be like a cheerful servant or child, that waits for your commands, and is glad to be employed by you. Your full subjection of your wills to God will be as the health, and ease, and quietness of your wills: you will feel that it is never well or easy with you, but when you are obedient and pleasing to your Creator's will. Your "delight will be in the law of the Lord" (Psalm 1:2). It will be sweeter than honey to you, and better than thousands of gold and silver; and this not for any side motive, but as it is the "law of God;" a "light unto your feet," and an infallible guide in all your duty. You will say with David (Psalm 119:16, 24, 35, 47, 70, 77, 174), "I find delight in your statutes; I do not forget your instructions. ... Yes, I find delight in your rules; they give me guidance. ... Guide me in the path of your commands, for I delight to walk in it." And as in Psalm 40:8, "I want to do what pleases you, my God. Your law dominates my thoughts." And, O "how blessed is the one who obeys the Lord, who takes great delight in keeping his commands" (Psalm 112:1).

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). All NET wording below was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Where this range begins and ends:** it starts at Direction 8 of Grand Direction 6 and ends at the close of that Grand Direction. The last line is the margin note ("Learning as disciples of Christ our Teacher") belonging to Grand Direction 7, whose heading is in the next range. The row of asterisks in the source is kept as a divider.
- **Psalm 40:8 (important):** Baxter quotes the King James, "I delight to do thy will, O my God, yea, thy law is within my heart." The NET reads "I want to do what pleases you, my God. Your law dominates my thoughts." Baxter's argument in this closing paragraph is about *delight* in obedience, and the NET drops the word "delight" here and puts "dominates my thoughts" where he has "within my heart." We use the NET. Flagged.
- **Psalm 112:1 (reference corrected; important):** Baxter prints "Psal. cxii. 2," but the quoted words are in verse 1 of the NET, so we cite 112:1. The King James has "blessed is the man that feareth the Lord"; the NET has "How blessed is the one who obeys the Lord." The NET changes "fears" to "obeys," so the fear of God, which Baxter's wider argument values, is not in the NET wording. Flagged. The NET text begins verse 1 with "Praise the Lord!"; we left that out because Baxter starts at "blessed."
- **Psalm 119:16, 24, 35 (important):** Baxter's quotation is a blend of the King James of verses 16, 24, and 35 ("I will delight myself in thy statutes; I will not forget thy word. Thy testimonies are my delight and my counselors. Make me to go in the path of thy commandments, for therein do I delight"). We give the NET for those three verses, with "..." between them. The NET of verse 24 reads "they give me guidance" where the King James has "my counselors," so the personal image of the statutes as advisors is lost. The NET has "statutes," "rules," and "commands" where the King James has "statutes," "testimonies," and "commandments." The further references (47, 70, 77, 174) are cited only.
- **Psalm 1:2:** Baxter's wording "delight will be in the law of the Lord" is his adaptation of the King James ("his delight is in the law of the Lord"). We kept his wording, in his quotation marks. The NET reads "Instead he finds pleasure in obeying the Lord's commands; he meditates on his commands day and night." The NET puts the pleasure in *obeying*, where Baxter's phrase says "the law."
- **"Go, and prosper"** (Direction 11): Baxter gives no reference. We take it to be the false prophets of 1 Kings 22 (verse 12 in the NET's numbering; we have not checked the other versions' numbering). The NET there reads "Attack Ramoth Gilead! You will succeed; the Lord will hand it over to the king." We kept Baxter's wording, in his quotation marks. Ahab, Micaiah, and the four hundred prophets (a figure from 1 Kings 22:6, which we did not fetch) are Baxter's allusions, not marked in his text.
- **"law of God;" "light unto your feet"** (last paragraph): quoted by Baxter without a reference. Probably Psalm 119:105 (the NET reads "Your word is a lamp to walk by and a light to illumine my path"). We kept Baxter's words, in his quotation marks. Not in Baxter's text; we have not confirmed his source. "Sweeter than honey" and "better than thousands of gold and silver" are unmarked allusions, probably to Psalm 19:10 and Psalm 119:72.
- **Cited only:** Luke 14:26-29, 33; Romans 8:5-7, 13; Colossians 3:1-4. Baxter's "Col. iii. 1-4" and the others are converted to modern form.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "the armor of God" (Ephesians 6:11); "the birthright ... for a mess of pottage" (Esau; Genesis 25:29-34, Hebrews 12:16); "the pleasures of sin for a season" (Hebrews 11:25); "take up their cross and follow Christ" (compare Matthew 16:24); "the sleep of carnal security" (compare 1 Thessalonians 5:6, probably). These are our suggestions, not Baxter's.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): armour/honour/Saviour, American spelling (armor/honor/Savior); sports, amusements; meats and drinks, foods and drinks ("meat" meant food in general); curious (attire), elaborate; mean (attire, condition), plain, simple, humble; dispraises, criticisms; conceit, notion; mess of pottage, bowl of stew; eremite, hermit; stored with, stocked with; motion (to disobedience), impulse; endeavor and similar words did not occur; by-respect, side motive; list ("as he list"), pleases; hath/doth/-eth forms to modern; imboldened, emboldened; villany, villainy; incredible, unbelievable; ungrateful, unpleasant (of obedience); discourse words did not occur; livings, livelihoods (see below); credit, reputation; censuring, judging; patronage, defense; &c., etc.
- **livings** (Direction 11): the source has "their credit, or livings, or lives." "Livings" could mean church posts (benefices) as well as means of support in general; we used "livelihoods," which covers both.
- **Terms kept:** *preferment* (promotion to high office or position), *carnal*, *mortified saints*, *security* (careless confidence; here "carnal security" is the sleep of careless ease), *the flesh* and *fleshly*, *delight*, *affections* did not occur. *Hypocrite* is kept.
- **Direction 8, "sell me for this your God":** the source reads "sell me for this thy God." It could mean "sell me [your loyalty] for this," or "sell your God for this, for me." We kept the words as printed and made no choice.
- **Direction 8, "take the alarm":** left as written (be alerted, as to an alarm).
- **Direction 9, "remember ... mercy must be distinguished from punishment":** Baxter's "differenced." **"excessive":** his "inordinate." **"mere natural pleasing of the senses":** left as written. **"bellyful," "gloomy notion":** his "belly-full," "melancholy conceit." Here "melancholy" is used in its ordinary sense of gloomy (see GLOSSARY.md); we used "gloomy."
- **Direction 9, "end of sinners":** his word "end" here means the goal sinners aim at (the pleasures of the flesh and carnal mind), not their final destiny. Left as written.
- **Direction 11, polemic:** the long list of controversies (the Mass, the pope's power over rulers, regicide) is Baxter's own polemic against Rome and, near the end, against those who judged the king; kept exactly. We have not checked each claim. The people named (Suarez, Bellarmine, Perron, Santarellus, Mariana; Clement, Ravilliac, Faux) are as Baxter names them; probably Jesuit and other Roman Catholic writers, and the assassins of kings (Henri III and Henri IV of France, and the Gunpowder Plot of 1605), but we have not confirmed each identification, and Baxter's claims about what these writers taught are his own. "Faux" is Baxter's spelling of Fawkes. "Perron" is probably Cardinal Duperron; not confirmed.
- **"Lateran. sub Innoc. III. Can. 3."** (Direction 11): printed as in the source, with a draft English translation in brackets (rendered loosely; the translation is a draft prepared for this project and needs a Latinist's check). Baxter's claim that this council's canon supports papal absolution of subjects from allegiance is his own; we have not checked it.
- **"We have seen at home"** (Direction 11): probably Baxter's reference to the English Civil War and the trial and execution of Charles I in 1649; he does not name them here.
- **"whether bread be bread, and wine be wine"** (Direction 11): Baxter's way of mocking the Roman Catholic doctrine of transubstantiation. His polemic, kept.
- **"vote"** (Direction 11): left as written. **"equivocate ... mental reservation":** the practice of saying words that deceive while privately holding back part of the meaning. Baxter treats it as lying.
- **Direction 12:** "The means by which the tempter accomplishes this must be spoken of afterward" is a cross-reference to a later part of the book. We have not checked where.
- **Numbering:** Baxter's "_Direct._ VIII" through "XII" become Directions 8 through 12. This is Grand Direction 6; the Direction numbers are those printed in the source.
