# Chapter 7: Title 9, against sinful grief and trouble (introduction and Directions 1-7)

**Title 9. Directions against sinful grief and trouble.**

Sorrow is planted in nature to make man a subject capable of government, by making him capable of punishment, that he might be kept from sin by the fear or sense of that which nature has made its punishment: and that the beginnings of pain might help to prevent the sin that would bring more; and might drive the wounded soul to its remedy; or by sympathy might grieve over the misery of others.

Sorrow or grief, in itself considered, is neither morally good nor evil; but it is a natural passion; and evil, that is, hurtful, to him that has it; but good, that is, an apt, conducive means to the universal or higher ends of government to which the Creator and Universal King has planted it in man: the same may be said of all capacity of pain and natural misery.

Mere sorrow, in itself considered, is a thing that God does not command, nor take pleasure in. Sorrow for our natural or penal hurt is in itself no duty, but a necessary thing. God does not command it, but threatens it; therefore there is no moral good in it. God will not command or entreat men to feel when they are hurt, or mourn under their torment; but will make them do it whether they will or not: therefore humble souls must beware of thinking they merit or please God merely by sorrowing for their sufferings. But yet sorrow for misery may incidentally become a duty and a moral good. 1. *Ratione principii*, by respect to the principle it proceeds from. As when it is, (1.) The belief of God's threatenings which causes the sorrow. (2.) When it comes from a love to God. 2. *Ratione materiæ*, for the matter's sake, when it is the absence of God, and his favor, and his Spirit, and image, which is the misery that we lament (which therefore savors of some love to God); and not mere fleshly, sensory suffering. 3. *Ratione finis*, in respect of the end; when we sorrow with intent to drive our hearts to Christ our Savior, and to value mercy and grace, and to recover us to God. 4. *Ratione effecti*, in respect of the effect, when these aforementioned ends become the fruits of it. [358]

Sorrow for sin is a duty and moral good. 1. Formally in itself considered: for to be sorrowful for offending God, and violating his law, essentially contains a will to please God and obey his law. 2. It must be also made good by a good principle; that is, by faith and love. 3. By a right end; that it be to carry us from the sin to God. 4. And by a right guide and matter; that it be sin indeed, and not a mistaken, seeming sin, that is it we sorrow for. But sorrow for sin (materially) may be made sinful, 1. By an ill end and formal reason; when we mourn not for sin as sin, but as one sin hinders another, or as it marred some ill design. 2. And by the effect; when it does but sink men in despair, or torment them, and not at all separate them from the sin. 3. When it comes not at all from any love to God, or care to please him, but only an unwillingness to be damned, and so it is lamented only as a means of damnation; which, though it be a sorrow, positively neither good nor evil, yet it is evil privatively.

But it is the passion of grief as in its excess that I am now to speak against. And it is in excess, 1. When we grieve for that which we ought not at all to grieve for; that is, either for some good, or for a thing indifferent that is neither good nor bad; both which come from the error of the mind. 2. When we grieve too much for that which we may grieve for lawfully in some measure; that is, for our own afflictions or penal suffering. 3. When we grieve too much for that which we are bound to grieve for in some measure: as (1.) For our sin. (2.) For our loss of the favor of God, or of his grace and Spirit. (3.) For other men's sin and suffering. (4.) For the sufferings of the church, and calamities of the world. (5.) For God's dishonor. [359]

Though it is not easy to have too much sorrow for sin, considering it estimatively; that is, we can hardly take sin for a worse evil than it is, and accordingly grieve for it; yet it is often too easy to have too much sorrow for sin, or any other evil intensively as to the greatness of the passion. And thus sorrow for sin is too great, 1. When it distracts the mind, and overturns reason, and it is made unfit for the ends of sorrow. 2. When it so clouds and clothes the soul in grief that it makes us unfit to see and consider the promise, to relish mercy, or believe it; to acknowledge benefits, or own grace received, or be thankful for it; to feel the love of God, or love him for it, to praise him, or to keep him in mind, or to call upon him; when it drives the soul from God, and weakens it to duty, and teaches it to deny mercy, and sinks it towards despair; all this is too much and sinful sorrow: and so is all that does the soul more hurt than good; for sorrow is not good of itself, but as it does good, or shows good.

**Direction 1. Keep your hearts as true and close to God as possible, and make sure of his love, that you may know you do not have an unregenerate, miserable soul to mourn for, and then all other grief is the more curable and more tolerable.**

Be once able to say that God is on your side, that Christ, and the Spirit, and heaven are yours, and then you have the greatest cordial against excessive grief that this world affords. If you say, How should this be done? I answer that it is explained in its proper place. No wonder if sorrow overwhelms that soul that is in the chains of sin, under the curse of God, as soon as awakened conscience comes to feel it. And it is most miserable when it has the smallest sorrow; there being some hope that sorrow may drive it home to Christ. Therefore if you have been a secure, unhumbled, carnal wretch, and God is now beginning to humble you, by showing you your sin and misery, be careful, as you love your soul, that you do not drive away necessary, healing sorrow and repentance, under pretense of driving away melancholy or overmuch sorrow; your pain tends to your hopes of cure.

**Direction 2. Do not renew the wounds of conscience by renewed, willful, gross sin.**

For sin will bring sorrow, especially if you have any life of grace to feel it: even as falls, and breaking the bones, bring pain. Obey carefully if you would have peace.

**Direction 3. Be well acquainted with the general grounds of hope in the mercy of God, the office and death of Christ, and the free, universal offer of pardon, grace, and life in the new covenant.**

Abundance of grief does dwell in many humbled souls, through the ignorance of these general grounds of comfort; which would vanish away if these were known.

**Direction 4. Know well the true nature and use of godly sorrow; how it is but a means to higher grace, and a thing which may exceed, and not a thing that we should stop in, or think we can never have too much of it.**

Desire it but in its place, and to its proper ends.

**Direction 5. Know well the nature and excellence of those higher graces which sorrow tends to; even love, and thankfulness, and delight in God, and fruitful obedience.**

And then you will be carried after these, and will learn to hate the sorrow that hinders them, and to cherish that sorrow which leads you up to them, and to value it but as a means to them.

**Direction 6. Manage all your affairs, especially those of your souls, with prudent foresight; and do not look only on things as they appear at hand. [360]**

Do not judge by sense, but by reason; for sense cannot foresee, but pleases itself at present with that which must be bitterness in the end. Thus, carnal delight is the common way to overwhelming sorrow. He that would not have the pain and sickness of a surfeit tomorrow must not please his appetite against reason today. Poison will cramp and kill none the less for tasting sweet. You must foreknow how that which you take will work, and what will be the effects of it, and not only how it tastes, if you would escape the pain. The drunkard does not think of his vomiting, and poverty, or shame, or sickness, and therefore causes them. There is no sorrow so intolerable as that of a guilty soul that is passing in terror to the bar of God, and thence to everlasting pain. Foresee this sorrow in your most pleasant sin; and remember that when you are tempted to sin, you are tempted to sorrow; and then you may prevent it. And in all your particular actions use a foreseeing judgment, and ask what is likely to be the end, before you enter on the beginning. Most of our sorrows come for lack of this, and express themselves by, Had I known, or had I thought of this, I would have prevented it. Do nothing which you may foresee must be repented of; for repentance is sorrowful; and the weightier the case the deeper the sorrow. How easy and comfortable a life and death might men attain, if they would not buy a little forbidden, poisonous pleasure with the price of future pain and sorrows! And if they did not foolishly and over-tenderly refuse those holy, necessary, medicinal sorrows, by which their greater, overwhelming, and undoing sorrows would have been prevented!

**Direction 7. Look always on your remedy when you look on your misery; and when you find any dangerous sin or sign in you, at once consider what is your duty in order to your recovery and escape.**

It is an ordinary thing with peevish, distempered natures, when they are reproved for any sin, to resist the reproof by excuses as long as they can; and when they can resist no longer, then they fall into despairing lamentations, if they are so bad, what then shall they do! and in the meantime never set themselves against the sin, and cast it off and return to their obedience, that their comforts may return: they will do anything rather than amend. The reason why God convicts them of sin is that they may forsake it, and they are sooner brought to anything than to this: convict them of their pride, or malice, or worldliness, or disobedience, or slothfulness, or passion, and they will sooner sink in sorrow and despair than they will set upon a resolved reformation. This is it indeed which the devil desires; he can allow you grief and desperation, but not to amend. But is this best for you? Or is it pleasing to God? Do not deny your sin, but see at the same time that there is enough in Christ for your pardon and deliverance. He has appointed you means for your present recovery, and he is ready to help you. Ask what is your duty for your cure, and set upon it without delay.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[358] Even sorrow that does not profit may testify a just affection. It is said by Laertius that when Solon was reproved for mourning for his son, with a Nihil proficis; he answered, At propter hoc ipsum illachrymor, quia nihil proficio. (English: "You accomplish nothing"; he answered, "But for this very reason I weep, because I accomplish nothing." Diogenes Laertius, on Solon.)

[359] That very old book of Hermes, called "Pastor," notably shows how much grief and heaviness is an enemy to Christianity and the Spirit of God.

[360] Pittaci sententia fuit, prudentiam virorum esse prius-quam adversa contingant, providere ne veniant: fortium vero, cum illa contigerint, æquo animo ferre. Laert. in Pittac. (English: It was the saying of Pittacus that it belongs to the prudence of men, before adversities happen, to take care that they do not come; but to the brave, when they have happened, to bear them with an even mind. Laertius, on Pittacus.)

## Translator's notes

- **Title:** Chapter 7 is divided into ten "Titles" (Baxter's *Tit.*), meaning headings or divisions; this file opens Title 9. The file ends at the close of Direction 7; Direction 8 continues in the next file.
- **Scripture:** no verse is quoted in this range, so no NET text was needed.
- **passion(s):** Baxter's word for the emotions and affections; kept. In Direction 7, "passion" in the list of sins means outbursts of temper; kept.
- **melancholy** (Direction 1): kept as Baxter's word; see GLOSSARY.md.
- **grieve over:** Baxter's "condole" (to grieve with or over), in the first paragraph.
- **conducive:** Baxter's "conducible."
- **incidentally:** Baxter's "accidentally," in the scholastic sense *per accidens*: not of its own nature, but by some added circumstance (the four respects he then lists).
- **Ratione principii / materiæ / finis / effecti:** scholastic Latin, kept in italics as printed; Baxter glosses each himself ("by respect to the principle," "for the matter's sake," "in respect of the end," "in respect of the effect").
- **formally, materially, positively, privatively; estimatively, intensively:** scholastic terms kept. "Positively ... privatively" means that such sorrow is not evil as a thing in itself, but is evil by what it lacks (love to God). "Estimatively" means in how bad we judge sin to be; "intensively" means in the strength of the feeling.
- **sensory:** Baxter's "sensitive" (of the senses), as decided in CONVENTIONS.md.
- **penal:** suffering as punishment; kept.
- **beware of thinking:** Baxter's "take heed of thinking." **be careful:** his "take heed" (Direction 1).
- **keep him in mind:** Baxter's "mind him."
- **consider the promise:** Baxter's "consider of the promise."
- **explained:** Baxter's "opened" (Direction 1). He does not say where; probably his directions on assurance elsewhere in the *Directory*, not checked.
- **cordial** (Direction 1): kept; a heart-strengthening medicine or drink.
- **secure** (Direction 1): kept as Baxter's term for careless, unwarranted confidence. **carnal:** kept (unspiritual, ruled by the flesh).
- **your pain:** Baxter's "thy smart" (Direction 1).
- **cramp:** Baxter's "gripe" (to cause griping pains in the bowels), Direction 6. **none the less:** his "never the less." **surfeit:** kept (overindulgence in food or drink). **for lack of:** his "for want of."
- **I would have prevented it; would have been prevented:** Baxter's "I had prevented it" and "should have been prevented" (older conditional forms), Direction 6.
- **at once:** Baxter's "presently" (Direction 7).
- **convicts / convict:** Baxter's "convinceth" and "convince," here in the older sense of proving someone guilty, not persuading (Direction 7).
- **at the same time:** Baxter's "withal" (Direction 7).
- **if they are so bad, what then shall they do!** (Direction 7): Baxter reports the lament indirectly without quotation marks; punctuation kept as printed.
- **"Hermes," "Pastor"** (note 359): probably the early Christian work known as *The Shepherd of Hermas*, which includes a commandment against grief; we have not checked the passage Baxter means.
- **Solon, Pittacus** (notes 358, 360): both counted among the Seven Sages of Greece; Baxter cites Diogenes Laertius's *Lives* for both sayings. **illachrymor** (note 358) is kept as printed.
- **Cross-reference:** Baxter's mention just before this Title of his other books on the fear of death belongs to the previous file.
