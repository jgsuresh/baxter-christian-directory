# Chapter 8: Part 4, Title 1: what gluttony is, and its causes (1–6)

**Part 4.**

**Directions for governing the taste and appetite.**

**Title 1. Directions against gluttony.**

The most that is necessary to be said to acquaint you with the nature and evil of this sin is said before in chapter 4, part 7, against flesh-pleasing. But something more particularly must be said, 1. To show you what is and what is not the sin of gluttony. 2. To show you the causes of it. 3. The odiousness of it. And, 4. To acquaint you with the more particular helps and means against it.

I. Gluttony is a voluntary excess in eating, for the pleasing of the appetite, or some other carnal end. [395] Here note, 1. The matter. 2. The end or effect of this excess. (1.) It is sometimes an excess in quantity, when more is eaten than is fitting. (2.) Or else it may be an excess in the delicious quantity, when more regard is had to the delight and sweetness than is fitting. (3.) Or it may be an excess in the frequency and ordinary unseasonableness of eating; when men eat too often, and sit at it too long. (4.) It may be an excess in the costliness or price; when men feed themselves at too high rates. (5.) Or it may be an excess of fastidiousness in the preparing, and saucing, and ordering of all. 2. And it is usually for some carnal end. Whether it may be properly called gluttony, if a man should think that at a sacrifice of thanksgiving he were bound to eat inordinately, and so made the service of God his end, we need not inquire (though I see no reason but it may have that name). For that is a case that is more rare; and it is undoubtedly a sin: and it is gluttony, if it is done for the pleasing of others that are insistent with you. But the common gluttony is when it is done for the pleasing of the appetite, with such a pleasure as is no help to health or duty, but usually a hurt to body or soul; the body being hurt by the excess, the soul is hurt by the inordinate pleasure. [396]

Yes, it is a kind of gluttony and excess when men will not fast or abstain when they are required, from that which at other times they may use with abstinence and without blame. If a man is not accustomed to eat excessively or deliciously, yet if he will not abstain from his temperate diet, either at a public fast, or when his lust requires him to bring down his body, or when his physician would diet him for his health, and his disease else would be increased by what he eats, this is an inordinate eating and excess to that person, at that time. Or if the delight that the appetite has in one sort of food, which is hurtful to the body, prevails against reason and health so with the person that he will not refrain from it, it is a degree of gulosity or gluttony, though for quantity and quality it is in itself but plain and ordinary.

By this you may see, 1. That it is not the same quantity which is an excess in one, which is in another. A laboring man may eat somewhat more than one that does not labor; and a strong and healthy body, more than the weak and sick. It must be an excess in quantity, as to that particular person at that time, which is when to please his appetite he eats more than is profitable to his health or duty. 2. So also the frequency must be considered with the quality of the person; for one person may rationally eat a little and often, for his health, and another may luxuriously eat oftener than is profitable to health. Ecclesiastes 10:16–17: "Woe to you, O land, when your king is childish and your princes feast in the morning. Blessed are you, O land, when your king is the son of nobility, and your princes feast at the proper time—with self-control and not in drunkenness." 3. And in point of costliness, the same measure is not to be set to a prince and to a plowman; that is luxurious excess in one which may be temperance and frugality in another. But yet, unprofitable cost, which, all things considered, would do more good another way, is excess in whomever. 4. And in fastidiousness of diet a difference must be allowed: the happier healthy man need not be so fastidious as the sick; and the happy plowman need not be so fastidious as state and expectation somewhat require the noble and the rich to be. 5. And for length of time, though unnecessary sitting out time at meals is a sin in any, yet the happy poor man is not obliged to spend all out so much this way as the rich may do. 6. And it is not all delight in food, or pleasing the appetite, that is a sin; [397] but only that which is made men's end, and not referred to a higher end; even when the delight itself does not tend to health, nor alacrity in duty, nor is used to that end, but to please the flesh and tempt to excess. 7. And it is not necessary that we measure the profitableness of quantity or quality by the present and immediate benefits; but by the more remote, sometimes: so merciful is God that he allows us that which is truly for our good, and forbids us only that which does us hurt, or at least, no good. 8. All sin in eating is not gluttony; but only such as are here described.

II. The causes of gluttony are these: 1. The chief is an inordinate appetite, together with a fleshly mind and will, which is set upon flesh-pleasing as its happiness. "Those who live according to the flesh have their outlook shaped by the things of the flesh" (Romans 8:5). This gulosity, which Clement of Alexandria calls the throat devil, and the belly devil, is the first cause. [398]

2. The next cause is the lack of strong reason, faith, and a spiritual appetite and mind, which should call off the glutton, and take him up with higher pleasures; even such as are more manly, and in which his real happiness consists. "Those who live according to the Spirit have their outlook shaped by the things of the Spirit" (Romans 8:5). Reason alone may do something to call up a man from this happiness of a beast (as appears by the philosopher's assaults upon the epicures), but faith and love, which feast the soul with sweeter delicacies, must do the cure.

3. Gluttony is much increased by habit: when the appetite is used to being satisfied, it will be the more insistent and impetuous; whereas a custom of temperance makes it easy, and makes excess a matter of no delight, but burden. I remember myself that when I first set upon the use of Cornaro's and Lessius's diet, as it is called (which I did for a time, for some special reasons), it seemed a little hard for two or three days; but within a week it became a pleasure, and another sort, or more, was not desirable. And I think almost all that use one dish only, and a small quantity, do find that more is a trouble and not a temptation to them: so great a matter is habit (unless it is with very strong and laboring persons).

4. Idleness and lack of diligence in a calling is a great cause of luxury and gluttony. Though labor causes a healthy appetite, yet it cures a beastly, pleasure-loving mind. An idle person has leisure to think of his guts, what to eat and what to drink, and to be longing after this and that; whereas a man that is wholly taken up in lawful business, especially such as finds employment for the mind as well as for the body, has no leisure for such thoughts. He that is close at his studies, or other calling, has something else to think on than his appetite.

5. Another incentive of gluttony is the pride of rich men, who, to be accounted good hosts, and to live at such rates as are agreeable to their grandeur, make their houses shops of sin, and as bad as alehouses; making their tables a snare both to themselves and others, by fullness, variety, deliciousness, costliness, and fastidiousness of fare. It is the honor of their houses that a man may drink excessively in their cellars when he pleases: and that their tables have excellent provisions for gluttony, and put all that sit at them upon the trial of their temperance, whether a bait so near them, and so studiously fitted, can tempt them to break the bounds and measure which God has set them. [399] It is a lamentable thing when such as have the rule of others, and influence on the common people, think their honor lies upon their sin; yes, upon such a constant course of sinning; and think it a dishonor to them to live in sweet and wholesome temperance, and to see that those about them do the like. And all this is either because they overvalue the esteem and talk of fleshly epicures, cannot bear the censure of a swine; or else because they are themselves of the same mind, and are such as glory in their shame (Philippians 3:18–19).

6. Another incentive is the custom of urging and pressing others to eat still more and more; as if it were a necessary act of friendship. People have grown so uncharitable and selfish that they suspect one another, and think they are not welcome if they are not urged thus to eat; and those that invite them think they must do it to avoid the suspicion of such a stingy mind. And I do not deny that it is fit to urge any to that which it is fit for them to do; and if we see that modesty makes them eat less than is best for them, we may persuade them to eat more. But now, without any due disrespect to what is best for them, men think it a necessary compliment to provoke others more and more to eat, till they peremptorily refuse it: but among the most familiar friends, there is scarcely any that will admonish one another against excess, and advise them to stop when they have enough, and tell them how easy it is to stop when they have enough, and tell them how easy it is to step beyond our bounds, and how much more prone we are to exceed than to come short: and so custom and compliment are preferred before temperance and honest fidelity. You will say, What will men think of us if we should not persuade them to eat, much more if we should desire them to eat no more? I answer, 1. Regard your duty more than what men think of you. Prefer virtue before the thoughts or breath of men. 2. But yet if you do it wisely, the wise and good will think much the better of you. You may easily let them see that you do it not in stingy sparing, but in love of temperance and of them; if you speak only when there is need either for eating more or less; and if your talk is first in general for temperance, and do not apply it till you see that they need help in the application. 3. It is undeniable that healthy persons are much more prone to excess than to the defect in eating, and that nature is very much bent to luxury and gluttony, I think as much as to any one sin; and it is as sure that it is a beastly, breeding, odious sin. And if this is so, is it not clear that we should do a great deal more to help one another against such luxury than to provoke them to it? Had we not a greater regard to men's favor, and fancies, and reports, than to God and the good of their souls, the case would soon be decided.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Labels:** the source heads this Part "PART IV." with the italic sub-heading "Directions for governing the Taste and Appetite," and this division "_Tit._ 1." "Tit." is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," that is, a heading or division within a Part. This file covers the opening of Title 1 through the sixth cause of gluttony; the seventh cause continues in the next file.
- **Ecclesiastes 10:16–17:** NET wording. Baxter's King James text has "thy king is a child," "thy princes eat in the morning," and "eat in due season, for strength, and not for drunkenness." The NET has "childish," "feast," and "with self-control and not in drunkenness." This changes the phrase Baxter leans on: his point is that eating should serve *strength* (health and duty), not appetite, and the King James "for strength" says that directly; the NET's "with self-control" makes the contrast one of restraint rather than of purpose.
- **Romans 8:5:** Baxter cites "Rom. viii. 6, 7" for the first quotation and "Rom. viii. 6" for the second, but both sentences ("They that are after the flesh do mind the things of the flesh"; "They that are after the Spirit do mind the things of the Spirit") are in Romans 8:5, so we cite 8:5. His references may mean the passage as it continues into verses 6–7. The NET's "have their outlook shaped by" renders the King James "do mind"; the sense Baxter uses (what the mind is set on) is kept.
- **Philippians 3:18–19:** "glory in their shame" is Baxter's King James phrase, worked into his own sentence, so we keep his wording. The NET at 3:19 reads "they exult in their shame," and the same verse says "their god is the belly," which fits his subject.
- **Genesis 27:7** (note 397): Baxter's "pleasant meat"; the NET's "tasty food" is used. **Numbers 11** (note 395): the Israelites' craving for meat instead of manna.
- **"delicious quantity"** (section I, point (2)): so printed. The sense (delight and sweetness) and the later pairing "for quantity and quality" suggest "quality" was meant; we keep the printed word.
- **"without any due disrespect"** (cause 6): so printed. The sense seems to call for "due respect" (that is, men press food on others without regard to what is good for them); kept as printed.
- **Repeated clause** (cause 6): "and tell them how easy it is to stop when they have enough" appears twice in the source; probably a printing repetition. Kept as printed.
- **"spend all out so much this way"** (point 5): kept as printed; the sense seems to be that the poor man is not obliged to spend as much time at meals as the rich may.
- **"and do not apply it"** (answer 2): Baxter's "and apply it not till," with "you" understood.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): meet to fitting; oft to often; curiosity / curious to fastidiousness / fastidious (elaborate, particular care about food and its preparation); dressing to preparing; importunate to insistent; importuning to pressing; use (custom) to habit; meat to food or meals; mean (ordinary) to plain; forbear to refrain from; take down (the body) to bring down; want to lack; felicity to happiness; delicates to delicacies; sensual to pleasure-loving; good housekeepers to good hosts (those who keep a generous, hospitable table); sordid (miserly) to stingy; discourse to talk; scarce to scarcely; "I see not but" to "I see no reason but."
- **Kept words:** **gulosity** (greediness of appetite; Baxter uses it as a synonym for gluttony); **deliciously, deliciousness** (in Baxter, of food chosen for delight and daintiness rather than need); **luxury, luxuriously** (indulgence and excess, especially at table, not wealth or comfort as such); **epicures** (pleasure-lovers; "the philosopher's assaults upon the epicures" refers to philosophical attacks on the followers of Epicurus); **carnal** (unspiritual, ruled by the flesh); **alacrity** (cheerful readiness); **breeding** (probably "that breeds other sins"; kept as printed).
- **"his lust requires him to bring down his body":** that is, when fasting is needed to subdue sexual desire.
- **Clement of Alexandria:** Baxter's attribution of the names "throat devil" and "belly devil" to him; we have not checked the passage.
- **Cornaro's and Lessius's diet:** probably Luigi Cornaro, the Venetian author of a treatise on the sober life, and the Jesuit Leonard Lessius, who wrote in favor of a measured diet; we have not checked the editions Baxter used. His remark about trying the diet himself is his own.
- **Chapter 4, part 7** (first paragraph): Baxter's cross-reference to his earlier treatment of flesh-pleasing; we have not checked the corresponding file.
- **Note 396 (Plutarch):** Baxter's attribution; we have not located the passage.
- **Note 398:** "Ethic. 1. 2. c. 3." is kept as printed; "1." is probably "l." (*liber*, book), that is, Aristotle's *Ethics*, book 2, chapter 3. The Cicero passage is unidentified here.
- **Note 399:** the Socrates sayings are from Diogenes Laertius's *Life of Socrates*, as Baxter says ("Laert."). "Zantippen" (Xanthippe, Socrates' wife) and "vivat" (where "vivere" would match "vivere ut ederent") are kept as printed.
