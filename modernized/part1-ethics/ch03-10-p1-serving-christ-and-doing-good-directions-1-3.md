# Chapter 3: Grand Direction 10 (part 1 of 6)

**Grand Direction 10. Your lives must be spent in doing God service, and doing all the good you can, in works of piety, justice, and charity, with prudence, fidelity, industry, zeal, and delight; remembering that you are engaged to God, as servants to their lord and master; and are entrusted with his talents, of whose profitable use you must give an account.**

The next relation between Christ and us, which we are to speak of (subordinate to that of King and subjects), is this of Master and servants. Though Christ says to the apostles, "I no longer call you slaves... But I have called you friends" (John 15:15); the meaning is not that he does not call them servants at all, but not mere servants, they being more than servants, having such acquaintance with his counsels as his friends. For he at once, in verse 20, tells them, "Remember what I told you, 'A slave is not greater than his master.'" And "You call me 'Teacher' and 'Lord,' and do so correctly, for that is what I am" (John 13:13). And "you have one Teacher and you are all brothers" (Matthew 23:8): so verse 10. And the apostles called themselves the "slaves of Christ Jesus" (Romans 1:1; Philippians 1:1; compare 1 Corinthians 4:1, "servants of Christ"): and "of God" (Titus 1:1, "a slave of God"), etc.

*In the margin: What it is to be Christ's servants.*

He is called our Master, and we his servants, because he is our Rector, *ex pleno dominio*, with absolute ownership; and does not give us laws to obey, while we do our own work, but gives us his work to do, and laws for the right doing of it: and it is a service under his eye, and in dependence on him for our daily provisions, as servants on their lord. God has work for us to do in the world; and the performance of it he will require. God tells his sons, "Son, go and work in the vineyard today" (Matthew 21:28); and expects that they do it (verse 31). His "servants" are like "tenant farmers," to whom "he entrusts his vineyard, that he may receive the fruit" (Matthew 21:33, 34, 41, 43). "Faithful servants shall be made rulers over his household" (Matthew 24:45, 46). Christ delivers to his servants his talents to make the most of, and will require an account of the use made of them at his coming (Matthew 25:14). Good works, in the proper, comprehensive sense, are all actions, internal and external, that are morally good; but in the narrower sense, they are works, not only formally good, as acts of obedience in general, but also materially good, such as a servant does for his master, that tend to his advantage, or the profit of some other, whose welfare he cares about. Because the doctrine of good works is disputed in these times, I shall first explain it briefly, and then give you the directions.

1. Nothing is more certain, than that God does not need the service of any creature; and that he receives no addition to his perfection or happiness from it; and, consequently, that on terms of commutative justice, (which gives one thing for another, as in selling and buying,) no creature is capable of meriting at his hands.

2. It is certain, that on the terms of the law of works, (which required perfect obedience as the condition of life,) no sinner can do any work so good as, in terms of distributive, governing justice, will merit at his hands.

3. It is certain, that Christ has so fulfilled the law of works, as to merit for us.

4. The redeemed are not masterless, but have still a Lord, who has now a double right to govern them. And this Governor gives them a law: and this law requires us to do good works, as much as we are able, (though not so frighteningly, yet) as bindingly as the law of works: and by this (of Christ) we must be judged: and thus we must be judged according to our works: and to be judged is nothing else but to be justified or condemned. Such works therefore are rewardable according to the distributive justice of the law of grace, by which we must be judged. And the ancient fathers, who (without any opposition) spoke of good works as meritorious with God, meant no more than that they were such as the righteous Judge of the world will reward according to the law of grace, by which he judges us. And this doctrine being agreed on as certain truth, there is no controversy with them, except whether the word merit was properly or improperly used: and that both Scripture and our common speech allow the fathers' use of the word, I have shown at large in my "Confession."

5. Christ is so far from redeeming us from a necessity of good works, that he died to restore us to a capacity and ability to perform them, and has made us new for that end. "He gave himself for us to set us free from every kind of lawlessness and to purify for himself a people who are truly his, who are eager to do good" (Titus 2:14). "For we are his creative work, having been created in Christ Jesus for good works that God prepared beforehand so we can do them" (Ephesians 2:10).

6. Good works opposed to Christ, or his satisfaction, merit, righteousness, mercy, or free grace in the matter of justification or salvation, are not good works, but proud self-confidence and sin. But good works, in their due subordination to God's mercy, and Christ's merits and grace, are necessary and rewardable.

7. Though God needs none of our works, yet that which is good materially pleases him, as it tends to his glory, and to our own and others' benefit, which he delights in.

8. It is the communicating of his goodness and excellences to the creature, by which God glorifies himself in the world; and in heaven, where is the fullest communication, he is most glorified. Therefore the praise which is given to the creature, who receives all from him, is his own praise. And it is no dishonor to God, that his creature be honored, by being good, and being esteemed good: otherwise God would never have created anything, lest it should detract from himself; or he would have made them bad, lest their goodness were his dishonor; and he would be most pleased with the wicked, and least pleased with the best, as most dishonoring him. But madness itself abhors these notions.

9. Therefore, as an act of mercy to us, and for his own glory, (as at first he made all things very good, so) he will make the new creature according to his image, which is holy, and just, and good, and will use us in good works; and it is our honor, and gain, and happiness to be so used by him. As he will not communicate light to the world without the sun (whose glory detracts not from his honor); so will he not do good works in the world directly by himself alone, but by his servants, whose calling and daily business it must be, as that which they are made for, as the sun is made to give light and heat to lower things (Ephesians 2:10). "Let your light shine before people, so that they can see your good deeds and give honor to your Father in heaven" (Matthew 5:16). Christ was far from the opinion of those who think all good works that are attributed to good men are dishonorable to God. [110]

10. He is most indebted to God, who is most exercised in good works. The more we do, the more we receive from him: and our very doing itself is our receiving; for it is he that "gives us both to will and to do," by his working in us (Philippians 2:13); even "he, without whom we can do nothing" (John 15:5).

11. The obligation to good works, that is, to works of piety, justice, and charity, is essential to us as servants of the Lord. We are practical atheists, if we do not do works of piety to God: we are rebels against God, and enemies to ourselves, and unfit for human society, if we do not do the works which are good for ourselves, and for others, if we have ability and opportunity. This is our fruit which God expects; and if we do not bear it, he will cut us down, and throw us into the fire.

12. Though doing no harm will not be enough, without doing good, yet it is not the same works that are required of all, nor in the same degree, but according to each person's talent and opportunities (Matthew 25:14, 15, etc.).

13. God does not look only or mainly at the outward part of the work, but much more at the heart of the one who does it; nor at the length of time, but at the sincerity and diligence of his servants. And therefore, though he is so just, as not to deny the reward which was promised them, to those who have borne the burden and heat of the day (Matthew 20:12-15); yet he is so gracious and generous, that he will give as much to those whom he finds as willing and diligent, and who would have done more if they had had opportunity. You see in all this, what our doctrine is about good works, and how far those papists are to be believed, who persuade their ignorant disciples, that we regard them as empty and needless things.

*Directions for faithfully serving Christ, and doing good.*

**Direction 1. Be sure that you have that holiness, justice, and charity within, which are the necessary principles of good works.**

For "a good tree will bring forth good fruit, and an evil tree evil fruit. Make the tree good, and the fruit good. A good man out of the good treasure of his heart, brings forth good things, and an evil man out of the evil treasure of his heart, brings forth evil things." As out of the heart come evil works (Matthew 15:19, 20), so out of the heart must good works come (Matthew 7:16-20). Can the dead do the works of the living? or the unholy do the works of holiness? or the unrighteous do the works of justice? or the uncharitable do the works of charity? Will he do good to Christ in his members on earth, who hates them? Or will he not rather imprison them, than visit them in prison; and rather strip them of all they have, than feed and clothe them? Or if a man should do that which materially is good, from pride, or other sinful principles, God does not accept it, but takes all such sacrifice as no better than carrion that is offered to him without the heart.

**Direction 2. Do not be content to do some good as something out of the ordinary, on the side, or when you are urged to it; but study to do good, and make it the trade or business of your lives.**

Having so many obligations, and so great encouragements, do what you do with all your might. If you would know whether you are servants to Christ, or to the flesh, the question must be, which of these has the main care and diligence of our lives; for as every carnal act will not prove you servants to the flesh, so every good action will not prove you the servants of Christ.

**Direction 3. Before you do any work, consider whether you can truly say it is a service of God, and will be accepted by him. See therefore that it is done, 1. To his glory, or to please him. 2. And in obedience to his command.**

Mere natural actions, that have no moral good or evil in them, and so do not belong to morality, these do not belong to our present subject; as not being the matter of rational (or at least of obediential) choice. Such as the winking of the eye, the setting of this foot forward first, the taking of this or that food, or drink, or tool, or company, or action, when they are equal, and it is no matter of rational (or obediential) choice, etc. But every act that is to be done deliberately and rationally, as matter of choice, must be moralized, or made good, by doing it, 1. To a right end; and, 2. According to the rule. "Whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, (that is matter of rational choice,) do everything for the glory of God" (1 Corinthians 10:31). All works do not tend alike to his glory; but some more immediately and directly, and others remotely; but all must ultimately have this end. Even servants who labor in their toilsome work, must "do it as to the Lord, and not (only, or ultimately) to men; not with eye-service as men-pleasers, but as the servants of Christ," from whom they must have their greatest reward or punishment (Ephesians 6:5-8; Colossians 3:22-25). All the comforts of food, or rest, or recreation, or pleasure which we take, should be intended to fit us for our Master's work, or strengthen, cheer, and help us in it. Do nothing, deliberately, that belongs to the government of reason, except God's service in the world; which you can say he set you to.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). All NET wording was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **"Servants" and "slaves" (the main flag for this section).** Baxter's whole argument is built on the King James word "servant" (and "Master"). The NET regularly renders the same Greek word (*doulos*) as "slave," and the NET has "Teacher" where the King James has "Master." The quotations below therefore read "slave" and "Teacher" where Baxter's "servant" and "Master" stand. Baxter's own prose keeps "servants" and "Master," and we have not changed it. Readers should know that his sense is "bond-servant who owes his master full service," which the NET's "slave" actually expresses more sharply, but the English words he plays on (servant, master, Master and Lord) are not all visible in the quoted verses.
- **John 15:15:** Baxter has "Henceforth I call you not servants, but friends." NET: "I no longer call you slaves... But I have called you friends." The NET adds the reason ("the slave does not understand what his master is doing"), which supports Baxter's own gloss ("acquaintance with his counsels"). **John 15:20:** NET has "Remember what I told you, 'A slave is not greater than his master.'" Baxter has "Remember that the servant is not greater than his lord." Baxter's "presently" is rendered "at once" and "bids" as "tells."
- **John 13:13:** KJV "Master and Lord"; NET "Teacher" and "Lord." The sense of Baxter's argument (that Christ claims lordship over them) is carried by "Lord," but "Master" is lost.
- **Matthew 23:8, 10:** Baxter's quotation "One is your Master, Christ; and all ye are brethren" is a blend of verses 8 and 10 (and his "so ver. 10" shows he knew it). NET 23:8: "you have one Teacher and you are all brothers"; NET 23:10: "you have one Teacher, the Christ." We quote verse 8 as he cites it. **brethren** is "brothers" in the NET. The NET's "Teacher" again removes the word "Master" on which Baxter's heading leans.
- **Apostles as "servants":** Baxter's quotation "servants of Jesus Christ" is an approximation. NET Romans 1:1 "a slave of Christ Jesus"; Philippians 1:1 "slaves of Christ Jesus"; Titus 1:1 "a slave of God"; 1 Corinthians 4:1 "servants of Christ and stewards of the mysteries of God." We cite them in that order of sense, not Baxter's order of references.
- **Matthew 21:28, 31:** Baxter has "Go work to day in my vineyard." NET has "Son, go and work in the vineyard today" (no "my"; the NET renders the parable's single son, whereas Baxter speaks of God's "sons" in the plural, applying it to all). **Matthew 21:33, 34, 41, 43:** Baxter's quotations ("husbandmen," "he intrusteth his vineyard, that he may receive the fruit") are his paraphrase of the parable, not exact KJV or NET wording. NET has "tenant farmers" (so "husbandmen" is "tenant farmers" here), "leased it," "to collect his portion of the crop," and "a people who will produce its fruit." We keep his paraphrase in his quotation marks, modernized.
- **Matthew 24:45, 46:** "Faithful servants shall be made rulers over his household" is Baxter's paraphrase. NET 24:45: "Who then is the faithful and wise slave, whom the master has put in charge of his household..."; 24:46: "Blessed is that slave whom the master finds at work when he comes."
- **Matthew 25:14 (talents):** not quoted. NET has "entrusted his property to them" and "five talents... two... one." **improve / improvement** (heading and paragraph 3): in Baxter's usage "to improve" is to make profitable use of, to put to work and increase. We render it "make the most of" and "profitable use" / "the use made of them."
- **Titus 2:14:** Baxter (KJV) has "redeem us from all iniquity, and purify to himself a peculiar people, zealous of good works." NET: "to set us free from every kind of lawlessness and to purify for himself a people who are truly his, who are eager to do good." The NET loses "peculiar" (a special, owned people) in favor of "truly his," and has "eager to do good" for "zealous of good works"; Baxter's phrase "good works" is the heart of this paragraph, so the sense is the same but the wording he quotes is not there. **Ephesians 2:10:** KJV has "his workmanship," "that we should walk in them"; NET has "his creative work" and "so we can do them." The NET drops the image of "walking" in good works.
- **Matthew 5:16:** KJV "men... good works... glorify your Father"; NET "people... good deeds... give honor to your Father in heaven." Baxter's text speaks of "good works" in the same breath as the verse, and the NET's "good deeds" is the same idea. Baxter's own phrase "good men" is kept (not "good people") in his prose.
- **Philippians 2:13** (paragraph 10): Baxter's "giveth us both to will and to do" is the KJV wording ("worketh in you both to will and to do") with the pronoun changed, so we treat it as his paraphrase and keep it, modernized. NET: "the one bringing forth in you both the desire and the effort—for the sake of his good pleasure—is God." "To will" corresponds to the NET's "desire" and "to do" to its "effort"; Baxter's "will" is the faculty of choosing, so his sense is slightly narrower than "desire." **John 15:5:** "he, without whom we can do nothing" is Baxter's paraphrase. NET: "apart from me you can accomplish nothing."
- **Matthew 20:12-15:** Baxter's "the burden and heat of the day" is KJV wording, not in quotation marks. NET 20:12: "the hardship and burning heat of the day." Cited as he gives it.
- **Good tree and good treasure** (Direction 1): Baxter gives no reference to the quotation. It blends Matthew 7:17-18, Matthew 12:33-35, and probably Luke 6:45 in KJV wording. We keep his words, modernized, in his quotation marks. For comparison: NET Matthew 12:33, "Make a tree good and its fruit will be good, or make a tree bad and its fruit will be bad"; NET Matthew 12:35, "The good person brings good things out of his good treasury, and the evil person brings evil things out of his evil treasury." His references that follow are Matthew 15:19, 20 (NET: "out of the heart come evil ideas, murder, adultery..."; verse 20, "These are the things that defile a person") and Matthew 7:16-20.
- **1 Corinthians 10:31:** NET: "So whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God." Baxter's own phrasing ("must be done by us to the glory of God") is closer to the KJV, and his bracketed gloss "(that is matter of rational choice,)" is his own addition inside the quotation. We keep the gloss and use NET wording around it.
- **Ephesians 6:5-8; Colossians 3:22-25:** Baxter's quotation ("do it as to the Lord, and not (only, or ultimately) to men; not with eye-service as men-pleasers, but as the servants of Christ") blends Colossians 3:23 and Ephesians 6:6 in KJV wording, with his own parenthesis. We keep it as his paraphrase. NET Colossians 3:23: "work at it with enthusiasm, as to the Lord and not for people"; NET Ephesians 6:6: "not like those who do their work only when someone is watching—as people-pleasers—but as slaves of Christ doing the will of God from the heart." NET again has "slaves" where Baxter has "servants," and this verse is addressed to slaves, which the modern reader should keep in mind.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording, no reference in his text; the references are our guesses and are not in Baxter): "made all things very good" (probably Genesis 1:31); "he will hew us down, and cast us into the fire" (probably Matthew 3:10 or 7:19); imprisoning rather than visiting, stripping rather than feeding and clothing (compare Matthew 25:35-36). Baxter's "after his image, which is holy, and just, and good" has no reference in the text.
- **Latin:** *ex pleno dominio* (English: from full lordship or ownership; draft translation). **Rector:** Baxter's word for ruler or governor, kept. **propriety:** here ownership; rendered "ownership."
- **formally good / materially good** (paragraph 2): scholastic terms, kept. "Formally good" means good in the doing (done as obedience); "materially good" means good in its content or effect (of real benefit to someone). **commutative justice / distributive justice:** kept; commutative justice is the fairness of exchange (one thing for another), distributive justice the fairness of giving each their due (here, reward or punishment under a law). **law of works / law of grace:** Baxter's terms for the old covenant of perfect obedience and the new covenant of grace; kept. **merit / meritorious:** kept; Baxter's point in item 4 is that he accepts the fathers' word on his own definition and says nothing else is at issue. **papists:** Baxter's word for Roman Catholics; his charge in item 13 is his polemic and is carried over as written. **"Confession":** Baxter's own earlier book; we have not confirmed which of his works he means, so we keep his title in quotation marks.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): felicity, happiness; derogate, detract; conceits, notions; unmeet, unfit; beholden, indebted; vain, empty; hurt, harm; "serve turn," be enough; "narrower acceptation," narrower sense; controverted, disputed; "open it," explain it; "terribly," frighteningly; "obligingly," bindingly; "operation," working; "inferior things," lower things; "meat," food; "instrument," tool; "painful work," toilsome work; "on the by," on the side; "extraordinarily," as something out of the ordinary; "is no matter," left as written; "but" (meaning except), except; "hew down," cut down. **improve:** see above. **industry:** left as written (diligence). **eye-service; men-pleasers:** left as Baxter wrote them inside his quotation.
- **obediential choice** (Direction 3): choice that falls within obedience to God's command; kept. **moralized:** Baxter's word for making an act morally good by its end and rule; kept.
- **immediately** (Direction 3): "more immediately and directly" means more without intermediate steps; kept in Baxter's wording.
- **"our lives"** (Direction 2): Baxter shifts from "your" to "our" in the middle of the sentence; left as written.
- **Footnote 110:** attached at the end of item 9. Carried over in full with draft translations (see above). The first sentence and the Acosta passage run together in the source; they may be two separate notes. Cross-reference to Acosta (probably *De procuranda Indorum salute*) is our guess and we have not confirmed it.
