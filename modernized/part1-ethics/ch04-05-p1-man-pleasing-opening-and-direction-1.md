# Chapter 4: Inordinate man-pleasing, the opening and the start of Direction 1

**Directions against inordinate man-pleasing; or that overvaluing the favor and censure of man, which is the fruit of pride, and a great cause of hypocrisy; or, directions against idolizing man.**

As in other cases, so in this, iniquity consists not simply in the heart's neglect of God, but in the preferring of some competitor, and dominance of some object which stands up for an opposite interest. [163] And so the obeying man before God and against him, and the valuing the favor and approbation of man before or against the approbation of God, and the fearing of man's censure or displeasure more than God's, is an idolizing man, or setting him up in the place of God. It turns our chief observance, and care, and labor, and pleasure, and grief into this human fleshly channel, and makes all that to be but human in our hearts and lives, which (objectively) should be divine. Which is so great and dangerous a sin, partaking of so much impiety, hypocrisy, and pride, as that it deserves a special place in my directions, and in all watchfulness and consideration to escape it.

As all other creatures, so especially man, must be regarded and valued only in a due subordination and subserviency to God. If they are valued otherwise, they are made his enemies, and so are to be hated, [164] and are made the principal engine of the ruin of such as overvalue them. See what the Scripture says of this sin: "Stop trusting in human beings, whose life's breath is in their nostrils. For why should they be given special consideration?" (Isaiah 2:22). "And call no one your 'father' on earth, for you have one Father, who is in heaven" (Matthew 23:9). [165] "But you are not to be called 'Rabbi,' for you have one Teacher and you are all brothers. ... The greatest among you will be your servant" (Matthew 23:8, 11). "I will put a curse on people who trust in mere human beings, who depend on mere flesh and blood for their strength" (Jeremiah 17:5). "The Lord is on my side; I am not afraid. What can people do to me? ... It is better to take shelter in the Lord than to trust in people. It is better to take shelter in the Lord than to trust in princes" (Psalm 118:6, 8–9). "I will not show partiality to any person, nor will I confer a title on anyone. For I do not know how to give honorary titles; if I did, my Creator would quickly do away with me" (Job 32:21–22). "Is my complaint against a man?" (Job 21:4). "Or am I trying to please people? If I were still trying to please people, I would not be a slave of Christ!" (Galatians 1:10). "So for me, it is a minor matter that I am judged by you or by any human court" (1 Corinthians 4:3). "If anyone comes to me and does not hate his own father and mother, and wife and children, and brothers and sisters, and even his own life, he cannot be my disciple" (Luke 14:26). "Blessed are you when people insult you and persecute you and say all kinds of evil things about you falsely on account of me. Rejoice and be glad because your reward is great in heaven" (Matthew 5:11–12). "Not like those who do their work only when someone is watching—as people-pleasers" (Ephesians 6:6; Colossians 3:22). "So we declare it, not to please people but God, who examines our hearts" (1 Thessalonians 2:4). "Enchanting folks for their own gain" (Jude 16). [166] This is enough to show you what Scripture says of this inordinate man-pleasing, or respect to man: and now I shall proceed to direct you to escape it.

**Direction 1. Understand well in what the nature of this sin consists, that you may not run into the contrary extreme, but may know which way to bend your opposition.** I shall therefore first show you, how far we may and must please men, and how far not.

1. Our parents, rulers, and superiors must be honored, obeyed, and pleased in all things which they require of us, in the several places of authority which God has given them over us; and this must be not merely as to man, but as to the officers of God, from whom, and for whom, (and not against him,) they have all their power (Romans 13; Exodus 20:12; Titus 3:1; 1 Peter 2:13; 2 Peter 2:10).

2. We must in charity, and condescension, and meekness of behavior, seek to please all men in order to their salvation. We must so thirst for the conversion of sinners, that we must become all things (lawful) to all men, that we may win them. [167] We must not stand upon our terms, and keep at a distance from them, but condescend to the lowest, and bear the infirmities of the weak; and in things indifferent not take the course that pleases ourselves, but that which, by pleasing him, may edify our weak brother. We must forbear and forgive, and part with our right, and deny ourselves the use of our Christian liberty, were it as long as we live, if it is necessary to the saving of our brethren's souls, by removing the offense which hinders them by prejudice. We must not seek our own carnal ends, but the benefit of others, and do them all the good we can.

3. As our neighbor is commanded to love us as himself, we are bound by all lawful means to render ourselves lovable to him, that we may help and facilitate this his love, as it is more necessary to him than to us: for to help him in obeying so great a command must needs be a great duty. And therefore if his very sin possesses him with prejudice against us, or causes him to dislike us for some indifferent thing, we must, as far as we can lawfully, remove the cause of his prejudice and dislike; though he that hates us for obeying God, must not be cured by our disobeying him. We are so far from being obliged to displease men by surliness and moroseness, that we are bound to pleasing gentleness, and brotherly kindness, and to all that behavior which is necessary to cure their sinful hatred or dislike.

4. We must not be self-conceited, and prefer a weak, unfurnished judgment of our own, before the greater wisdom of another; but in honor must prefer each other: and the ignorant must honor the knowledge and abilities of others that excel them, and not be stiff in their own opinion, nor wise in their own eyes, nor undervalue another man's reasons or judgment; but be glad to learn of any that can teach them, in the humble acknowledgment of their own insufficiency.

5. Especially we must reverence the judgment of our able, faithful teachers, and not by pride set up our weaker judgment against them, and resist the truth which they deliver to us from God. Neither must we make light of the censures or admonitions of the lawful pastors of the church: [168] when they are agreeable to the word and judgment of God, they are very dreadful. As Tertullian says, If any so offend as to be banished from communion of prayer, and assembly, and all holy commerce, it is a judgment preceding the great judgment to come. Yes, if the officers of Christ should wrong you in their censures by passion or mistake, while they act in their own charge about matters belonging to their cognizance and judgment, you must respectfully and patiently bear the wrong, so as not to dishonor and despise the authority and office so abused.

6. If sober, godly persons, that are well acquainted with us, do strongly suspect us to be faulty where we discern it not ourselves, it should make us the more suspicious and fearful: and if judicious persons fear you to be hypocrites, and no sound Christians, by observing your temper and course of life, it should make you search with the greater fear, and not to disregard their judgment. And if judicious persons, especially ministers, shall tell a poor, fearful, doubting Christian, that they truly think their state is safe, it may be a great support to them, and must not be slighted as nothing, though it cannot give them a certainty of their case. Thus far man's judgment must be valued.

7. A good name among men, which is the reputation of our integrity, is not to be neglected as a thing of no account; for it is a mercy from God for which we must be thankful, and it is a useful means to our successful serving and honoring God. And the more eminent we are, and the more the honor of God and religion is joined with ours, or the good of men's souls depends on our reputation, the more careful we should be of it; and it may be a duty sometimes to vindicate it by the magistrate's justice, against a slander. Especially preachers (whose success for the saving of their hearers depends much on their good name) must not despise it. [169]

8. The censures of the most petulant, and the scorns of enemies, are not to be made light of, as they are their sins, which we must lament; nor as they may provoke us to a more diligent search, and careful watchfulness over our ways. Thus far man's judgment is worth regarding.

But, 1. We must know how frail, and erroneous, and inconstant a thing man is; and therefore not be too high in our expectations from man. We must suppose that men will mistake us, and wrong us, and slander us, through ignorance, passion, prejudice, or self-interest. And when this befalls us, we must not account it strange and unexpected.

2. We must consider how far the enmity that is in lapsed man to holiness, and the ignorance, prejudice, and passion of the ungodly, will carry them to despise, and scorn, and slander all such as seriously and zealously serve God, and cross them in their carnal interest. And therefore, if for the sake of Christ and righteousness, we are accounted as the scorn and offscouring of all things, and as pestilent fellows, and movers of sedition among the people, and such as are unworthy to live, and have all manner of evil spoken of us falsely, it must not seem strange or unexpected to us, nor cast us down, but we must bear it patiently, yes, and exceedingly rejoice in hope of our reward in heaven. [170]

3. Considering what remnants of pride and self-conceitedness remain in many that have true grace, and how many hypocrites are in the church, whose religion consists in opinions and their several modes of worship; we must expect to be reproached and abused by such, as in opinions, and modes, and circumstances do differ from us, and take us therefore as their adversaries. A great deal of injustice, sometimes by slanders or reproach, and sometimes by greater violence, must be expected, from contentious professors of the same religion with ourselves: especially when the interest of their faction or cause requires it: and especially if we bring any truth among them, which seems new to them, or crosses the opinions which are there in credit, or would be reformers of them in anything that is amiss.

4. No men must be pleased by sin, nor their favor preferred before the pleasing of God. Man's favor as against God, is to be despised, and their displeasure made light of. If doing our duty will displease them, let them be displeased; we can but pity them.

5. We must place none of our happiness in the favor or approbation of men, but account it as to ourselves to be a matter of no great importance; neither worth any great care or effort to obtain it, or grief for losing it. We must not only despise it as compared to the approbation and favor of God, but we must value it but as other transitory things, in itself considered; estimating it as a means to some higher end, the service of God, and our own or other men's greater good: and further than it contributes to some of these, it must be almost indifferent to us what men think or say of us: and the displeasure of all men, if unjust, must be reckoned with our light afflictions.

6. One truth of God, and the smallest duty, must be preferred before the pleasing and favor of all the men in the world. Though yet as a means to the promoting of a greater truth or duty, the favor and pleasing of men must be preferred before the uttering of a lesser truth, or doing a lesser good at that time: because it is no duty then to do it.

7. Our hearts are so selfish and deceitful, naturally, that when we are very solicitous about our reputation, we must carefully watch them lest self be intended, while God is claimed. And we must take special care, that we be sure it is the honor of God, and religion, and the good of souls, or some greater benefit than honor itself, that we value our honor and reputation for.

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows the Latin passages in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[165] Magna animi sublimitate carpentes se atque objurgantes Socrates contemnebat. Laert. in Socrat. (Socrates despised, with great loftiness of mind, those who carped at and rebuked him. Laertius, in the *Life of Socrates*.)

Tacitus, book 17; rendered loosely.) Secure conscience first, Qua semel amissa, postea nullus eris. (When it has once been lost, afterward you will be nobody; rendered loosely.)

[168] Galatians 5:10; 1 Corinthians 5.

[169] Quicquid de te probabiliter fingi potest, ne fingatur ante devita. Hieron. ad Nepot. (Beforehand, avoid whatever can plausibly be made up about you, so that it is not made up. Non solum veritas in hac parte sed etiam opinio studiose quærenda est, ut te hypocritam agere interdum minime pœniteat, said one harshly enough to Acosta, ut lib. 4. c. 17. p. 413. [170] 1 Peter 4:12, 13, etc.; 1 Corinthians 4:12, 13; Acts 22:22; 24:5, 6; Matthew 5:10-12.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). All the quotations were fetched from the NET text. Baxter's quotations are King James wording, and several are shortened or run together.
- **Section heading:** the heading is Baxter's, in his italics; capitalization is modernized. This is the opening of a new section of Chapter 4.
- **Jeremiah 17:5, a corrected reference:** Baxter prints "Jer. xx. 15," but the words he quotes ("Cursed be the man that trusteth in man, and maketh flesh his arm") are at Jeremiah 17:5; Jeremiah 20:15 is a different curse (on the man who brought news of Jeremiah's birth). We use 17:5. NET: "I will put a curse on people who trust in mere human beings, who depend on mere flesh and blood for their strength." The NET lacks the picture of making "flesh his arm" but says the same in plainer terms.
- **Isaiah 2:22, a flag:** King James "Cease ye from man ... wherein is he to be accounted of?"; NET "Stop trusting in human beings ... why should they be given special consideration?" The NET names the activity (trusting) that the King James leaves general ("cease from"), and "given special consideration" for "accounted of." Baxter's point (do not overvalue man) is kept, but he is quoting the verse as a warning against overvaluing, which is wider than trusting.
- **Matthew 23:8, 11:** Baxter prints "Ver. 8" but his quotation joins verses 8 and 11, so we cite both; the ellipsis marks the skipped verses 9-10. His "even Christ" is not in the NET at verse 8 (the NET has "the Christ" at verse 10), and the King James "Master" is the NET's "Teacher." We use the NET wording only.
- **Psalm 118:6, 8-9:** Baxter's "yea, in princes" is a shortening of verse 9; we give the full NET verse 9. The ellipsis marks verse 7. Baxter's "I will not fear what man can do unto me" is the King James; NET "I am not afraid. What can people do to me?" The King James has "trust in the Lord" and "put confidence in man"; the NET has "take shelter in the Lord" and "trust in people," so the NET does not use Baxter's matching pair "trust"/"confidence" for the two objects.
- **Job 32:21-22:** King James "flattering titles" and "accept any man's person"; NET "honorary titles" and "show partiality". The NET loses the word "flattering," which Baxter's argument about man-pleasing leans on.
- **Job 21:4:** King James "is my complaint to man?" (meaning, my complaint is to God, not to man); NET "Is my complaint against a man?" The NET changes the sense Baxter uses: he reads it as "I appeal to God, not to man." The NET asks whether Job's quarrel is with a human being.
- **Galatians 1:10:** King James "servant of Christ"; NET "slave of Christ." Baxter's "yet" is the NET's "still." We use the NET's second sentence, which carries his point.
- **1 Corinthians 4:3:** King James "man's judgment"; NET "any human court". The extent is Baxter's, "a very small thing" becoming "a minor matter."
- **Luke 14:26:** NET has "does not hate his own father and mother ... even his own life"; Baxter's "yea, and his own life also" matches in sense.
- **Matthew 5:11-12:** Baxter's "exceeding glad" is the King James; NET "be glad." The NET continues with a reason about the prophets, which we leave out because Baxter stops at "in heaven."
- **Ephesians 6:6; Colossians 3:22:** Baxter's "eye-service, as men-pleasers" is the King James; the NET spells out "do their work only when someone is watching." The quotation is from Ephesians; Colossians 3:22 is cited by Baxter alongside it and the NET wording there is similar ("not only when they are watching—like those who are strictly people-pleasers").
- **1 Thessalonians 2:4:** the NET begins "but just as we have been approved by God to be entrusted with the gospel, so we declare it, not to please people but God, who examines our hearts." We quote from "so we declare it"; Baxter's "trieth" is "examines."
- **Jude 16, a flag:** Baxter quotes the King James, "Having men's persons in admiration because of advantage." The NET reads "enchanting folks for their own gain," and says it of the grumblers and fault-finders in the verse ("they give bombastic speeches, enchanting folks for their own gain"). The King James sense (admiring people's persons for advantage) is the one Baxter uses; the NET describes the speakers' own manner of winning people over, not the admiring of men. We keep the NET and note that it does not carry Baxter's sense.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "become all things (lawful) to all men, that we may win them" (1 Corinthians 9:19-22); "bear the infirmities of the weak" (Romans 15:1); "in honor must prefer each other" (Romans 12:10); "wise in their own eyes" (Proverbs 3:7; Isaiah 5:21); "the scorn and offscouring of all things" (1 Corinthians 4:13); "pestilent fellows, and movers of sedition" and "unworthy to live" (Acts 24:5; 22:22); "all manner of evil spoken of us falsely" (Matthew 5:11); "our light afflictions" (2 Corinthians 4:17). The footnotes [167] and [170] give some of these.
- **Tertullian** (paragraph 5): Baxter gives no reference. We have not confirmed where in Tertullian this is found, so it is kept as Baxter's own report of his words.
- **Word swaps:** "dominance" for Baxter's "prevalence" (the object gaining the upper hand); "chief" for "chiefest"; "conduceth" to "contributes"; "contemn" to "despise"; "set light by" to "make light of"; "foregoing" to "preceding"; "stay" to "support"; "a thing of nought" to "a thing of no account"; "carriage" (bearing) to "behavior"; "morosity" to "moroseness"; "unconstant" to "inconstant"; "moment" (importance) to "importance"; "endeavor" to "effort"; "parts" (natural abilities) to "abilities"; "cognisance" to "cognizance"; "distaste" (verb) to "dislike"; "pretended" to "claimed"; "amiable" to "lovable"; "regardable" to "worth regarding"; "verily" to "truly"; "yea" to "yes" where it is a plain affirmation.
- **Words kept:** "inordinate" (excessive, not kept within right limits); "man-pleasing" (the pleasing of men, as in Galatians 1:10); "objectively" (as regards the object loved); "engine" (a contrivance or instrument); "condescension" (stooping graciously to others, without the modern sense of looking down); "things indifferent" (matters neither commanded nor forbidden); "petulant" (insolent, quarrelsome); "lapsed man" (fallen humanity); "professors" (those who profess a religion); "dreadful" (awe-inspiring); "holy commerce" (dealings in holy things); "carnal" (as in the glossary); "judicious" (sound in judgment); "pestilent" (Acts 24:5 in the King James; the NET has "a troublemaker").
- **Numbering:** Baxter's second list begins "But, 1." and runs through numbered items; this file ends at item 7, and item 8 begins the next file. A first list (1-8) precedes it, ending "Thus far man's judgment is [worth regarding]."
- **Footnote 163:** "Martin. Dumiens." is Baxter's abbreviation; we have not confirmed the author or work beyond the source's wording. **Footnotes 165 and 166:** "Laert." is Baxter's abbreviation (probably Diogenes Laertius, which we have not checked). "lib. 17" for Tacitus is as printed; we have not checked it. **Footnote 169:** "Hieron. ad Nepot." is probably Jerome's letter to Nepotian; the Acosta reference is as Baxter gives it.
- **Footnote 164** shows the verses Baxter relies on for "hated"; it is Luke 14:26-27 (hating father, mother, and one's own life).
- **Footnote 166, "the most":** Baxter's "the most" means "the many," that is, the majority of people.
