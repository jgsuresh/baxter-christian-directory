# Chapter 10: Part 3, Directions 1–5 about apparel, and pride in dress

**Part 3.**

**Directions about apparel, and against the sin committed in it.**

**Direction 1. Fitness is the first thing to be regarded in your apparel, to make it a means to the end to which it is appointed.**

The ends of apparel are, 1. To keep the body warm. 2. To keep it from being hurt. 3. To adorn it soberly so far as befits the common dignity of human nature, and the special dignity of your places. 4. To hide those parts which nature has made your shame, and modesty commands you to cover.

The fitness of apparel consists in these things: 1. That it be fitted to your bodies (as your shoe to your foot, your hat to your head, etc.). 2. That it be suited to your sex; that men do not wear apparel proper to women, nor women that which is proper to men. 3. That it be suited to your age; the young and the old being usually by this somewhat distinguished. 4. That it be suited to your estate, or not above it. 5. That it be suited to your place or office. 6. That it be suited to your use and service. As, 1. To cover your nakedness so far as health, or modesty, or decency require. 2. To keep you from cold. 3. And from hurt in your labor (as the shoe does the foot, the glove the hand, etc.). 4. For sober ornament, as said before.

**Direction 2. Among the ends and uses of apparel the greatest is to be preferred: the ornament being the least is not to be pleaded against any of the rest.**

Therefore they that for ornament, 1. Will go naked in any part which should be covered; or, 2. Will go coldly to the hurt or hazard of their health (as our semi-Evites, or half-naked gallants do); 3. Or will either hurt our bodies (as our tight-laced fashion-followers), or disable themselves from their labor, or travel, or fit exercise, lest they should be hurt by their clothes, which are fitted more to sight than use; all these cross the ends of clothing.

**Direction 3. Do not aim at singularity in your apparel; that is, to be odd and noticeably distinct from all those of your own rank and quality; unless their fashions are evil and intolerable (in pride, immodesty, levity, etc.), and then your singularity is your duty.**

An unnecessary affectation of singularity shows, 1. A weakness of judgment. 2. A pride of that which you aim at. 3. And a placing of duty in things indifferent. And on the contrary, an imitating of proud or immodest fashions, 1. Encourages others in the sin. 2. Shows a carnal, proud, or temporizing mind, that will displease God himself to humor men, and avoid their contempt and low esteem.

**Direction 4. Do not run into sordid vileness, or filthiness, or ridiculous, whimsical, squalid fashions, under pretense of avoiding pride.**

For, 1. This will betray a great weakness of judgment. 2. It will make your judgment, to men that discern it, the more contemptible and useless to them in other things. 3. It will harden them in their excess, while they think nothing but whim, folly, or superstition reproves them. 4. You sin by dishonoring human nature. God has put a special honor upon man, and would have us do so ourselves; and therefore has appointed clothing since the fall: as nakedness, so overly sordid or ridiculous clothing wrongs God in his creature.

**Direction 5. Be much more suspicious of pride and excess in apparel, as the more common and dangerous extreme.**

For nature is incomparably more prone to this than the other; and many hundreds, if not thousands, sin in excess, for one that sins in the defect; and this way of sinning is more perilous. Here I shall show you, 1. How pride in apparel appears. 2. What is the sinfulness of it.

1. Pride appears in apparel when the material of it is too costly. 2. When in the fashion you are desirous to be imitating those that are above your estate or rank; and when you so fit your apparel as to make you seem some higher or richer person than you are. 3. When you are overly fastidious in the material, shape, or dress, and make a greater matter of it than you ought: as if your comeliness were a more desirable thing than it is, or as if some lowliness or disliked fashion were intolerable. 4. When your fastidiousness takes up more time in dressing you than is due to so small a matter, while far greater matters are neglected. 5. When you make too great a difference between your private and your public dress; going plain when no strangers see you, and being excessively careful when you go out, or when strangers visit you. These show that pride which consists in a desire to appear either richer or comelier than you are.

Besides these, there is a pride which makes men desirous to seem more learned than they are; which shows itself in affecting, as the titles, so the dress of the learned: which has some aggravations above the former.

And there is a pride which consists in a desire to seem more grave and reverend than you are: thus Christ blames the Pharisees' affectation of long garments, Mark 12:38. When you wear a dress of more gravity than you have, it is hypocrisy.

And there is a pride which consists in a desire to seem more mortified than you are, and more holy. [609] And so to affect those distinguishing garments which signify more of these than you have is proud hypocrisy: and thus lowly clothing is often the effect of pride; and if men fall into that sort of pride, as to desire to be noted as most mortified persons, this is as suitable a badge for them as finery is for those that are proud of their comeliness, and grave clothing of those that are proud of their gravity.

*In the margin: How pride of gravity and holiness appears about apparel.*

*Question 1.* But may we as easily discern this sort of pride in clothing as the other? *Answer.* No, because the humble, and plain, and cheap clothing is commonly worn by persons really mortified and sober, and necessarily by the poor, and grave clothing by persons that are really grave. And therefore we are bound to judge them to be that which they seem by their apparel to be, unless by some other evidences than their apparel their pride and hypocrisy appear; but when we judge a person frivolous that wears frivolous clothing, and proud of their comeliness that are inordinately careful in setting it out, we judge only according to the first and proper signification of their clothing. Hypocrisy is a thing unseen to man: it is the visible signs according to their proper signification that we must judge by; and therefore when we see persons wear frivolous and fastidious attire, we may judge by it that they are frivolous and fastidious; and if we are mistaken, it is owing to them that signified it; and when we see persons wear grave or humble clothing, we must judge by it that they are grave and humble, till the contrary appears.

*Question 2.* But how else will pride of gravity or mortifiedness appear? *Answer.* When they boast of these themselves, and are insolent in censuring and reproaching those that differ from them; when their talk is more against those fashions which they avoid than against any faults of their own; when they aim to be singular in their apparel, even from the grave and humble persons of their rank; but especially when they make a noise and stir in the world with their fashions, to be taken notice of, and to become eminent, and persons talked of and admired for their mortified garb. Thus many sects among the popish friars go by agreement or vow in clothes so differing from all other persons in seeming humility and gravity, which must be the badge of their order in the eye of the world, that the boast and affectation is visible and professed. And thus the Quakers, that by the notoriety of their difference from other sober persons, and by their impudent bawling in the streets and churches, and railing against the holiest and humblest ministers and people that are not of their sect, and this in the face of markets and congregations, make a plain profession or exposure of their pride. But where it is not openly revealed, we cannot judge it.

*In the margin: May not a deformity be hidden by apparel or painting.*

*Question 3.* Is it not lawful for a person that is deformed to hide their deformity by their clothing? And for any persons to make themselves (by clothing, or spots, or painting) to seem to others as comely and beautiful as they can? *Answer.* The person, and the material, and the end and reasons, the principle and the probable consequences must all be considered for the right answering of this question. It is lawful to some persons, by some means, for some good ends and reasons, when a greater evil is not likely to follow it, to hide their deformities, and to adorn themselves so as to seem more comely than they are: but for other persons, by evil means, for evil ends and reasons, or when it tends to evil consequences, it is unlawful. 1. A person that is naturally very deformed may do more to hide it by their ornaments than one that has no such deformity may do to seem more comely; because one aspires no higher than to seem somewhat like other persons; but the other aspires to seem excellent above others. And a person that is under government may do more in obedience to their governors than another may do that is at their own choice. 2. If the material of their ornament is only modest, decent clothing, and not immodest, insolent, luxurious, frivolous, or against nature, or the law of God or man, it is in that respect allowable. But so is no cover of deformity by unlawful means. 3. It may be lawful, if also it is to a lawful end, as to obey a governor, or only to cover a deformity, so as not unnecessarily to reveal it; but it is always sinful when the end is sinful. As, (1.) If it is to seem extraordinarily beautiful or comely when you are not so; or if it is to be observed and admired by beholders. (2.) If it is to tempt the beholders' minds to lustful or undue affections. (3.) If it is to deceive the mind of someone that you desire in marriage: for in that case, to seem by such dissembling to be what you are not is the most injurious kind of cheat, much worse than to sell a horse that is blind or lame for a sound one. (4.) If it is to follow the fashions of proud gallants, that you may not be scorned by them as not neat enough; all these are unlawful ends and reasons. 4. So also the principle or mind that it comes from may make it sinful: as, (1.) If it comes from a lustful, wanton mind. (2.) Or if it comes from an over-great regard for the opinion of spectators; which is the proper temper of pride. [610] A person that does not do it in pride is not very anxious about it: nor makes any great matter of it whether men take him to be comely or uncomely; and therefore he is at no great cost or care to seem comely to them. If such persons are deformed, they know it is God's work, and not their sin; and it is sin that is the true cause of shame: and all God's works are good, and for our good if we are his children. They know that God does it to keep them humble, and prevent that pride, and lust, and wantonness which is the undoing of many; and therefore they will rather be careful to make good use of it, and get the benefit, than to hide it and seem comelier than they are. 5. Also the consequences concur much to make the action good or bad: though that is not your end, yet if you may foresee that greater hurt than good will follow, or is likely to follow, it will be your sin. As, (1.) If it tends to the ensnaring of the minds of the beholders in brazen, lustful, wanton passions, though you say you do not intend it, it is your sin that you do that which probably will bring it about, yes, that you did not do your best to avoid it. And though it is their sin and frivolity that is the cause, it is nevertheless your sin to be the unnecessary occasion: for you must consider that you live among diseased souls! And you must not lay a stumbling block in their way, nor blow up the fire of their lust, nor make your ornaments their snares; but you must walk among sinful persons as you would do with a candle among straw or gunpowder; or else you may see the flame which you would not foresee, when it is too late to quench it. But a proud and brazen, lustful mind is so very willing to be loved, and thought highly of, and admired and desired, that no fear of God, or of the sin and misery of themselves or others, will satisfy them, or draw them off. (2.) Also it is sinful to adorn yourselves in such fashions as probably will encourage pride or frivolity in others, or seem to approve of it. When any fashion is the common badge of the proud and frivolous sort of persons of that time and place, it is sinful unnecessarily to conform yourselves to them; because you will harden them in their sin, and you join yourselves to them, as one of them, by a kind of profession. As when spotted faces (a name that former ages did not understand) or naked breasts, or such other fashions, are used ordinarily by the frivolous, and brain-sick, and heart-sick, proud and wanton party, it is a sin unnecessarily to use them. For, (1.) You will hinder their repentance. (2.) And you will hinder the great benefit which the world may get by their frivolous attire: for (though it is no thanks to them that do not intend it) yet it is a very great advantage that comes to mankind by these people's sin: that fools should go about in fools' coats, and that empty brains, and proud and wanton hearts, should be so openly exposed in the streets and churches; that sober people may avoid them; and that wise, and chaste, and civil people may not be deceived by such in marriage to their undoing. As the different clothing of the different sexes is necessary to chastity and order; so it is a matter of great convenience in a commonwealth that drunkards, and swaggerers, and madmen, and idiots, and proud, and wanton, lustful persons should be openly distinguished from others; as in a plague-time the doors of infected houses are marked with a "Lord, have mercy on us." And the wisest magistrate did not know how to have accomplished this himself by a law, as the wretches themselves do by their voluntary choice; for if it were not voluntary, it would be no distinguishing badge of their profession. Now for any honest, civil people to join with them, and take up their livery, and the dress of their order, is to profess themselves such as they, and so to encourage and approve them, or else to confuse the proud and humble, the frivolous and sober, the wanton and the chaste, and destroy the benefit of distinction.

By this you may see that it is not so much the bare fashion itself that is to be regarded as the signification and the consequences of it. The same fashion, when used by sober persons, to better signification and consequences, may be lawful, which otherwise is unlawful. Therefore those fashions that can hardly ever be supposed to have a good signification and consequences are hardly ever to be supposed lawful.

Note also that any one of the aforesaid evils makes a fashion evil, but it must be all the requisites together that must prove your fashions good or lawful.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows the Latin passage in parentheses; the translation was prepared for this project and is not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check it before relying on fine points.

[609] Matthew 23:5; Malachi 3:17.

[610] Laertius says that when Croesus sat in all his ornaments and glory on his throne, he asked Solon, An pulchrius unquam spectaculum viderit? Illumque dixisse: Gallos, gallinaceos, phasianos, et pavones: naturali enim eos nitore et speciositate eximia vestiri. (English: [he asked Solon] whether he had ever seen a more beautiful sight? And [Laertius says] that he answered: cocks, barnyard fowl, pheasants, and peacocks; for they are clothed by nature with brilliance and exceptional beauty.)

## Translator's notes

- **Headings:** this file begins Part 3 of Chapter 10 ("Directions for the Government of the Body"); Baxter's heading is "PART III." with the sub-heading "Directions about Apparel, and against the Sin therein committed." Part 3 has no "Title" divisions in this range. Direction numbering restarts at 1 for this Part, as in Baxter. This file covers Directions 1–5; the next file continues after the closing paragraphs of Direction 5.
- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). No verse is quoted in this range. **Mark 12:38** is cited, not quoted. Baxter says Christ blames "the Pharisees'" love of long garments; in the verse (in the King James as in the NET, which we fetched) the people blamed are the scribes ("experts in the law," in the NET), who "like walking around in long robes." Matthew 23:5, cited in note 609, is said of the scribes and Pharisees together (Matthew 23:2); there the King James has "enlarge the borders of their garments," which suits Baxter's point about garments, while the NET (fetched) has "they make their phylacteries wide and their tassels long." Neither verse is quoted by Baxter. We keep Baxter's wording.
- **Note 609:** the relevance of Malachi 3:17 to the point is not obvious to us; we keep Baxter's reference as printed and have not checked whether it is a misprint.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): men not wearing women's apparel nor women men's (Deuteronomy 22:5); "lay a stumbling block in their way" (compare Romans 14:13). The "Lord, have mercy on us" on the doors of infected houses refers to the practice, in plague-time England, of marking shut-up houses with that inscription; it is a prayer, not a citation.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): respected (regarded); beseemeth (befits); aforesaid (said before, in Direction 1; kept as "aforesaid" in the final paragraph); pretended against (pleaded against, i.e. put forward as a reason against); strait-laced fashionists (tight-laced fashion-followers, i.e. people who lace their bodices or stays tight); affect singularity (aim at singularity); observably (noticeably); disesteem (low esteem); humorous (whimsical); humour, as a noun (whim); nastiness (filthiness); over (overly); matter, meaning the stuff of a garment (material); over-curious and curiosity (overly fastidious, fastidiousness); curious attire (fastidious attire); meanness and mean clothing (lowliness, humble clothing); habit (dress); abroad (out); discriminating vestments (distinguishing garments); vile clothing (lowly clothing: Baxter means base, poor clothing, not disgusting); bravery (finery); long of them (owing to them); discourse (talk); detection and detected (exposure, exposed); consequents (consequences); solicitous (anxious); complexion (temper, i.e. characteristic disposition); improve (make good use of); procacious (brazen; from Latin *procax*, forward, insolent, wanton); procure (bring about); take them off (draw them off); commodity (advantage); sots (drunkards; the word also meant fools); phrenetics (madmen); confound (confuse); concurrent (together).
- **vain, vanity:** in this section Baxter uses "vain" of showy, frivolous dress and the people who wear it, so we use *frivolous* and *frivolity* rather than the usual *empty*. "Empty brains" is his own phrase.
- **nor makes no great matter:** Baxter's double negative; we give "nor makes any great matter." **When you shall wear:** "When you wear."
- **semi-Evites:** kept as Baxter's coinage, apparently "half-Eves," people dressed so scantily that they recall Eve before clothing; with "half-naked gallants" (fashionable men of display).
- **hurt our bodies** (Direction 2, item 3): the source has "our," where the sentence's subject would lead one to expect "their." We keep "our."
- **estate:** one's financial standing and rank in life. **comeliness, comely:** good looks, attractiveness; kept. **mortified, mortifiedness:** having put to death sinful desires; here, appearing self-denying; kept. **carnal:** unspiritual, ruled by the flesh; kept. **temporizing:** time-serving, bending to whoever is in favor. **things indifferent:** matters neither commanded nor forbidden by God. **affectation, affecting:** putting on, assuming for show. **aggravations:** features that make a sin worse.
- **spots, spotted faces:** beauty patches, small pieces of black silk or the like stuck on the face, a fashion of Baxter's day. We keep "spots" because Baxter remarks on the name itself ("a name that former ages did not understand"). **painting:** the use of cosmetics on the face.
- **under government; governors:** under the authority of parents, masters, husbands, or others; Baxter allows such a person more latitude when obeying them.
- **popish friars; the Quakers:** Baxter's polemic against Roman Catholic religious orders and against the Quakers, kept as he wrote it. "Papist" and "popish" are his words for Roman Catholic.
- **livery; order:** the uniform of a servant or company, and a religious order; Baxter uses both figuratively of the fashionable proud.
- **Note 610:** "Laertius" is probably Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of the Philosophers*, in his life of Solon; we have not checked the passage. Croesus was the wealthy king of Lydia.
