# Chapter 9: Title 6, the last consideration against scorning, Directions 1–8 to prevent it, and closing requests to the godly

*This file begins partway through Direction 1 of Title 6 (for the cure of those already infected with scorning), at the twentieth and last of its numbered considerations.*

20. Lastly, consider what you will think of yourself for this at death and judgment. [560] Will it comfort you when you are going to be judged by God, to think that you are now going into the presence of that God whom you used to scorn? When you see Christ come with thousands of his holy angels to judge the world, will it comfort you to think, this is he whose holy life, and precepts, and servants I mocked or persecuted on earth? Now I must be judged by him that I derided. Oh dreadful case! for a scorner or persecutor of godliness, to go to be judged by that holy God whose ways he scorned and persecuted! [561] If you say, It was not Christ but a man that you derided; see Matthew 25:40, 45; Luke 19:27; Acts 9:4, "Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?" If you scorn a child for that in which he resembles, imitates, or obeys his Father, you will find in the day of judgment to your woe, that it was the Father himself that was the utmost and principal object of your scorn. Then I would rather be the vilest toad than such a man. Then will you stand by what you said? Will you then maintain your slanders and reproach? Will you then condemn or scorn the godly, when you see them justified at Christ's right hand, or glorified with him in heaven? No! as Malachi 3:18, when God makes up his jewels, "Then once more you will see that I make a distinction between the righteous and the wicked, between the one who serves God and the one who does not." Then how gladly would you eat all the words of reproach and scorn, that ever you uttered against a saint; and wish that you had never spoken them! I tell you it is an unseemly thing for the same man now to scorn at godliness, who will so speedily tremble before the righteous God in the remembrance of it!

I have thought these disclosures of the horribleness of this sin to be the best directions against it; for as it is a sin you gain nothing by, so it is a sin that you may easily leave if you are willing. But for those that are yet only on the way to it, or in danger of it, I shall add these further directions to keep them from so desperate a wickedness.

**Direction 1. Avoid the company of those mad men that dare revile the servants and ways of God.**

There is that in your corrupted natures which will incline you to imitate the most horrid blasphemies if you often hear them. We have seen it in our days, that in imitation of others, men have been drawn to sins not to be named: to drink healths to the devil, to make "God damn me" an ordinary byword. Therefore do not be companions of them.

**Direction 2. Take heed of sinning yourselves into blindness of mind and hardness of heart.**

Do not forsake God, lest you be forsaken by him. It is men forsaken by God that ordinarily come to this desperate degree of sin: insomuch that the book of Homilies, describing them thus, says, "The third sort he calls scorners, that is, a sort of men whose hearts are so stuffed with malice, that they are not contented to dwell in sin, and to lead their lives in all kind of wickedness, but also they despise and scorn in others all godliness, true religion, all honesty and virtue. Of the two first sorts of men, I will not say but they may take repentance and be converted to God: of the third sort, I think I may without danger of God's judgment pronounce, that never any yet were converted to God by repentance, but continued still in their abominable wickedness, heaping up to themselves damnation against the day of God's inevitable judgment." [562] Though I take this to be too severe, yet it is the judgment of the church of England, and terrible to scorners that profess their assent to it.

**Direction 3. Take heed of scorning at the very circumstances or modes of worship which you dislike;**

for such scorns come so near to the worship itself, that the minds of the hearers may easily be drawn from this to dishonor the substance for the sake of the derided mode or circumstance; and it plainly smacks of a bold profaneness, which grave and sober Christians abhor. In the case of idolatry, or where the very substance of the worship is impious and forbidden, I do not deny but Elijah may (sometimes, and with wariness) be imitated, who derided Baal's priests: but to do thus over smaller differences in the manner or circumstances of worship is the way to teach men to turn all religion into matter of derision and contempt. If you see about the king some circumstance of clothing, ornament, or attendance of his followers, which you dislike or judge ridiculous, if you look toward him with a scornful laughter, it will not excuse you to say, I did not laugh at the king, but at such or such a thing about him; for his presence should have restrained you from that which seems to be a deriding of him. So here, I know you will say, It is not at God's worship, but at such words or gestures of the minister that I scorn: but take heed of trifling with holy things; do not play so near the consuming fire; do not give others occasion to deride the thing itself by your deriding the circumstances, though they were unfit. Have we not seen, while factious Christians raise jests, and nicknames, and scorns against each other, how the profane and common enemies of religion take them up, and turn them against all serious godliness, to the trouble of others and their own damnation? [563] And we have had experience in these contentious times, that it is the sectarians and the profane that are apt to use these scoffs and scorns against the things and persons that they dislike; and that sober, peaceable, judicious men of all sides abhor it. How distasteful and profane have all sober men thought it, when they heard some young and hot-brained persons mocking at the Common Prayer by the name of Pottage, and at the surplice by the name of The whore of Babylon's smock! And from this the same spirit led them as proudly and bitterly to deride ministers, universities, learning, temples, tithes, and all the appurtenances of worship; yes, the Lord's day, and singing psalms, and preaching, and almost all the duties of religion: for when once men will claim to strive for God with the spirit and weapons of Satan, and the world, and flesh, there is no stop till they come to the bottom of impiety, and do Satan's work in Satan's way: and so on the other side, while some have too reproachfully scorned such as Precisians or Puritans, who differed from them about the form of church government and ceremonies, the rabble of the profane soon got advantage by it, and turned these words to such common and bitter reproaches of the godly, sober, peaceable people of the land, that Mr. Robert Bolton says, "I am persuaded there was never a poor persecuted word, since malice against God first seized on the damned angels, and the graces of heaven dwelt in the heart of man, that passed through the mouths of all sorts of unregenerate men with more distastefulness and gnashing of teeth than the name of Puritan does at this day; which, notwithstanding, as it is now commonly meant, and ordinarily proceeds from the spleen and spirit of profaneness and good fellowship, is an honorable nickname, if I may so speak, of Christianity and grace." [564] See more cited out of him, and Bishop Downam, Bishop Abbot, etc. in my *Formal Hypocrite*, p. 210, 212, etc.

**Direction 4. Be very fearful of making the persons of the godly contemptible, though for their real faults, lest the ungodly easily step from there to the contempt of godliness itself.**

For it is easy to observe how commonly the common people judge of the doctrine and religion by the person that professes it. If a papist or a sectarian lives a holy life, take heed of making a scorn at their persons, notwithstanding that you take the occasion of your derision from their mistakes; for even a mistaken saint is dearly beloved and honored by God; and wherever holiness is, it is the most great, resplendent, and predominant thing in him that has it; [565] and therefore puts a greater honor on him than any mistake or infirmity can dishonor him: as the person of a king must not be dishonored by a reproachful mention of his infirmities, lest it reflect upon his office; so neither must the person of a holy man, lest it reflect on his religion. Not that any man's person should credit or secure his faults, nor that we should judge of the faults or manners by the men, instead of judging of the men by their manners; but you must judge of them by that which is predominant; and so blame their faults as to preserve the honor of their virtues and religion, and of their persons for their virtues' sake. So blame the falls of Noah, and Lot, and David, and Peter, as may make the sin more odious, but not so as may make their persons contemptible, lest it make their religion next to be despised. Mark here the difference between the mentioning of good men's falls by the godly and by the ungodly. The godly mention them to make sin appear a thing more to be feared and watched against, and holiness to appear more excellent and necessary; but the ungodly mention them (and read them in Scripture) to make themselves believe that sin is not so bad and dangerous a thing as preachers tell them; and that holiness differs only a little from a fleshly life.

**Direction 5. Do not judge of God's servants merely by report, without some considerable acquaintance with them.**

I cannot remember one of a multitude of the enemies, scorners, and persecutors of godliness, great or small, high or low, but such as never had the happiness to be well acquainted with them, by any familiarity, or observation of the private passages of their lives; but usually they are such as know them only by report, or by sight, or small acquaintance. And if they did but live with them in the same houses, or were among their familiars, it would be the likeliest way to change their minds and speeches; unless their acquaintance were only with some of the more ignorant, emotional, or disordered sort of Christians.

**Direction 6. Take heed of uncharitableness and malice against any; but especially the servants of Christ.**

For this blinds the judgment, and maddens men with a venomous kind of passion, and will make them scorn and rage against the most holy servants of the Lord. The least true love to a Christian, as a Christian, would do much to the cure of all this sin.

**Direction 7. Take heed of being engaged in a sect or faction, and take heed of the carnal zeal of schism, and of the spirit of faction, which ordinarily makes men think it lawful, if not necessary, to scorn the persons that seem against them, so that they may disable them from hindering the interest of their cause or party.**

Thus papists, and thus—the factious ones of every party, think that their revilings are only the necessary disarming of the enemies of God (for such all must seem that differ from them); and a stripping them of that honor by which they might do hurt. Thus good is claimed for the most odious evil, and God is set up against that love which is the fulfilling of his law; and made the patron of the scorners of his children; but surely he scorns the scorners, Proverbs 3:34.

**Direction 8. Take heed of error and unbelief:**

for if the understanding is once deluded, and takes religion itself to be only a deceit or fancy, and godliness to be only notions and hypocrisy, no wonder if it is made a scorn by such. And such scorners will justify themselves in it, and think they do no harm; so great a plague is a blinded mind.

I have said less against this devilish sin than the nature of it requires, because I have already said so much, especially in three treatises, namely, *The Vain Religion of the Formal Hypocrite*; that called *Now or Never*; and *A Saint or a Brute*.

I conclude with these earnest requests to the godly: 1. Give men no occasion of scorn by your imprudence, scandal, selfishness, or passions, as you value the honor of God and men's salvation. As Chrysostom says, "As he that carries the king's standard in fight needs to be well guarded, so he that carries the name and profession of God and godliness." [566] 2. Do not be discouraged by scorners: these are only easy in comparison with what Christ suffered for you, and what the scorners themselves must suffer.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows the Latin passage in parentheses; the translation was prepared for this project and is not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check it before relying on fine points.

[560] Read well Jude 14–15; Psalm 1.

[561] Proverbs 9:12; 29:8; Isaiah 28:14.

[562] Homily 10, part 2, tom. 9, p. 150, cited before in my *Now or Never*, p. 125.

[563] Nicknames themselves are the great engines of the devil, and to be avoided; it was well with the church when there was no other name but Christians put by Christ's disciples on each other; though by the enemies they were scornfully called Nazarenes, and a sect, and heresy.

[564] *Discourse of Happiness*, p. 193.

[565] Pliny says that as pearls, though they lie in the bottom of the sea, are yet much nearer akin to heaven, as their splendor and excellence shows; so a godly and generous soul has more dependence on heaven, where it comes from, than on earth where it abides. A good saying for a heathen.

[566] Socrates cum fuisset a quodam calce percussus, admirantibus illius tolerantiam dixit, Quid si me asinus calce impetisset? Num illi diem dixissem? (English: When Socrates had been kicked by someone, he said to those who marveled at his patience, What if an ass had kicked me? Would I have taken it to court?)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Title:** "Tit." in the source is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," his word for a heading or division of the chapter. Chapter 9 ("Directions for the Government of the Tongue") has six Titles; this file finishes Title 6, against profane deriding and scorning, which is also the end of the chapter.
- **Where this file starts:** the range opens with consideration 20 of Direction 1 of Title 6 ("for the cure of those that are already infected"). After it Baxter begins a second series of Directions, numbered again from 1, for those "in danger of" the sin. We keep his numbering.
- **Acts 9:4:** NET wording ("why are you persecuting me?"), same sense as the King James "why persecutest thou me?"
- **Malachi 3:18:** NET wording. The King James, which Baxter quotes, reads "then shall ye return, and discern between the righteous and the wicked, between him that serveth God and him that serveth him not." The NET has "once more you will see that I make a distinction": in the NET God makes the distinction and the hearers see it, while in the King James the hearers themselves discern it. Baxter's point (that scorners will then see the difference between the godly and the ungodly) survives in either reading, but his stress on the scorners' own discerning is softer in the NET.
- **"when God makes up his jewels":** Baxter's own words, alluding to Malachi 3:17 in the King James ("in that day when I make up my jewels"). The NET has "in the day when I prepare my own special property," so the "jewels" image is not in the NET.
- **"surely he scorns the scorners" (Proverbs 3:34):** Baxter gives the King James wording without quotation marks, so we keep it, modernized. The NET reads "With arrogant scoffers he is scornful, yet he shows favor to the humble."
- **Matthew 25:40, 45; Luke 19:27:** cited, not quoted. Matthew 25 is the passage where what is done or not done to "the least of these" is counted as done to Christ, which is Baxter's point.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording; references not in his text): Christ coming "with thousands of his holy angels" to judge (Jude 14, which he cites in note 560); "the consuming fire" (Hebrews 12:29; Deuteronomy 4:24); Elijah deriding Baal's priests (1 Kings 18:27); love as "the fulfilling of his law" (Romans 13:10); "heaping up ... damnation against the day" (compare Romans 2:5). In note 563, the disciples first called Christians (Acts 11:26), and called "the sect of the Nazarenes," a "sect," and "heresy" by their enemies (Acts 24:5, 14; 28:22).
- **Word swaps (Baxter's word, then ours):** distracted (mad) to mad; were wont to to used to; discoveries to disclosures; horridness to horribleness; gettest to gain; contemn to despise; savoureth of to smacks of; Elias to Elijah; dallying to trifling; unapt to unfit; sectaries to sectarians; mislike to dislike; unsavoury to distasteful; pretend to claim; vulgar to common people; takest the rise of to take the occasion of; contemned to despised; barely to merely; secret passages to private passages; of their familiarity to among their familiars; passionate to emotional; distempered to disordered; mads to maddens; infidelity to unbelief; conceit to notions; viz. to namely; tender (hold dear) to value; had need to be to needs to be; "that I may so speak" (in Bolton) to "if I may so speak."
- **drink healths to the devil:** to drink a toast to the devil.
- **the Common Prayer ... Pottage ... the surplice ... The whore of Babylon's smock:** the Book of Common Prayer and the white linen vestment worn by Church of England clergy; "Pottage" (a thick soup) and "the whore of Babylon's smock" were mocking names for them. Baxter quotes these as examples of the scorning he condemns; we keep them as he wrote them.
- **Precisians or Puritans:** both were (in Baxter's account) names of reproach for strict Protestants; he says they began as scorn between parties over church government and ceremonies and were then turned against the godly in general.
- **good fellowship** (in the Bolton quotation): convivial companionship, here with the sense of tavern company and carousing. **the spleen:** spite, ill temper.
- **papist; sectarian:** papist is Baxter's word for a Roman Catholic; sectaries were members of the separatist sects of his day. His remarks on them are his own polemic, kept as written (here, notably, he defends holy members of both from scorn).
- **"Thus papists, and thus—the factious ones":** the source has a dash here ("thus--the factious ones"); we keep it.
- **The book of Homilies (Direction 2 and note 562):** the Church of England's official books of sermons. We have not identified which homily Baxter's reference "Homil. 10 part 2" means, and "tom. 9" (tome or volume 9) is reproduced as printed. The quotation's "third sort ... scorners" seems to refer to the three kinds of sinner in Psalm 1:1 (the "he" who "calls" them is presumably the psalmist, or an author the homily cites); we have not confirmed this.
- **Robert Bolton; *Discourse of Happiness* (note 564):** Robert Bolton was probably the Puritan preacher of that name of the early seventeenth century (we have not confirmed this); the work is probably his *Discourse about the State of True Happiness*. We have not checked the quotation against it. **Bishop Downam, Bishop Abbot:** the source gives only surnames; we have not identified them with certainty.
- **Baxter's own books:** *The Vain Religion of the Formal Hypocrite* (also cited as *Formal Hypocrite*), *Now or Never*, and *A Saint or a Brute*, as Baxter names them. The page numbers are his.
- **Chrysostom's saying:** we have not located the source; Baxter gives none.
- **Note 566 does not match its marker:** the marker follows the Chrysostom saying, but the note tells a story about Socrates bearing a kick with patience. It fits better with request 2 ("Do not be discouraged by scorners"). We keep the marker where the source has it. *Diem dicere* is a Roman legal phrase for summoning someone to trial, rendered loosely as "taken it to court."
- **Pliny (note 565):** presumably Pliny the Elder's *Natural History*, which discusses pearls; Baxter gives no reference and we have not located the passage.
- **"Not that any man's person should credit or secure his faults"** (Direction 4): that is, a good man's standing should not lend credit to his faults or shield them.
- **"if it is made a scorn"; "notions"** (Direction 8): Baxter's "conceit" here means a mere notion or fancy, not pride.
