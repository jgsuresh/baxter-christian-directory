# Chapter 10: Part 2, Direction 1 (what lawful recreation is, and its qualifications)

**Part 2.**

**Directions about amusements and recreations, and against excess and sin in them.**

**Direction 1. If you would escape the sin and danger which men commonly run into by unlawful amusement, under pretense of lawful recreations, you must understand what lawful recreation is, and what is its proper end and use.**

No wonder otherwise if you sin, when you do not know what you do!

*In the margin: What lawful recreation is.*

No doubt but some amusement and recreation is lawful, indeed needful, and therefore a duty to some men. Lawful amusement or recreation is the use of some natural thing or action, not forbidden us, for the exhilarating of the natural spirits by the imagination, and due exercise of the natural parts, thereby to fit the body and mind for ordinary duty to God. It is some delightful exercise.

1. We do not call unpleasant labor by the name of amusement or recreation; though it may be better and more necessary. 2. We do not call every delight by the name of amusement or recreation; for eating and drinking may be delightful; and holy things and duties may be delightful; and yet not properly amusements or recreations. But it is the imagination that is chiefly delighted by amusements.

*Qualification 1.* All these things following are necessary to the lawfulness of an amusement or recreation, and the lack of any one of them will make and prove it to be unlawful. 1. The end which you really intend in using it must be to fit you for your service to God; that is, either for your callings, or for his worship, or some work of obedience in which you may please and glorify him: 1 Corinthians 10:31, "whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God." It is just to your duty, as the mower's sharpening to his scythe, to make it fit to do his work.

*Qualification 2.* 2. Therefore the person that uses it must be one that is heartily devoted to God, and his service, and really lives to do his work, and please and glorify him in the world: which none but the godly truly do. And therefore no carnal, ungodly person, that has no such holy end, can use any recreation lawfully; because he does not use it to a due end. For the end is essential to the moral good of any action; and an evil end must necessarily make it evil. Titus 1:15, "All is pure to those who are pure (that is, all things not forbidden). But to those who are corrupt and unbelieving, nothing is pure, but both their minds and consciences are corrupted."

*Question.* But must all wicked men therefore give up recreation? *Answer.* 1. Wicked men are such as will not obey God's law if they know it; and therefore they do not inquire what they should do, with any purpose sincerely to obey. But if they would obey, that which God commands them is immediately to forsake their wickedness, and to become the servants of God, and then there will be no room for the question. 2. But if they will continue in a sinful, ungodly state, it is useless to devise how they may amuse themselves without sin. But yet we may tell them that if the amusement is materially lawful, it is not the matter that they are bound to forsake, but it is the sinful end and manner. And until this is reformed they cannot but sin.

*Qualification 3.* 3. A lawful recreation must be a means fitly chosen and used to this end. If it has no aptitude to fit us for God's service in our ordinary callings and duty, it can be to us no lawful recreation. Though it is lawful to another that it is a real help to, it is unlawful to us.

*Qualification 4.* 4. Therefore all recreations are unlawful which are themselves preferred before our callings, or which are used by a man that lives idly, or in no calling, and has no ordinary work to make him need them. For these are no fit means, which exclude our end, instead of furthering it.

*Qualification 5.* 5. Therefore all those are unlawful amusements which are used only to delight a carnal imagination, and have no higher end than to please the sickly mind that loves them.

*Qualification 6.* 6. And therefore all those are unlawful amusements which really unfit us for the duties of our callings, and the service of God; which, laying the benefit and hurt together, hinder us as much or more than they help us! which is the case of all voluptuous pleasure-seekers.

*Qualification 7.* 7. All amusements are unlawful which take up any part of the time which we should spend in greater works: such are all those that are unseasonable (as on the Lord's day without necessity, or when we should be at prayer, or any other duty); and all those that take up more time than the end of a recreation necessarily requires (which is too common).

*Qualification 8.* 8. If a recreation is profane, as making fun of holy things, it is a mocking of God, and a villainy unbefitting any of his creatures, and laying them open to his heaviest vengeance. The children that made fun by calling the prophet "baldy" were slain by bears (2 Kings 2:23).

*Qualification 9.* 9. They are unlawful amusements which are used to the wrong of others: as actors, that defame and reproach other men; and hunters and falconers, that trample poor men's grain and hedges.

*Qualification 10.* 10. It is sinful to make fun of other men's sinning, or to act it ourselves so as to become partakers of it; which is too common with comedians, and other profane wits.

*Qualification 11.* 11. Unclean, obscene recreations are unlawful; when filthiness or lewdness is represented without a due expression of its odiousness, or with obscene words or actions. Ephesians 5:3, 4, "But among you there must not be either sexual immorality, impurity of any kind, or greed, as these are not fitting for the saints. Neither should there be vulgar speech, foolish talk, or coarse jesting."

*Qualification 12.* 12. Those amusements are unlawful which occasion the multiplying of idle words about them; and engage the players in foolish, needless, unprofitable chatter.

*Qualification 13.* 13. And those amusements are sinful which plainly tend to provoke ourselves or others to sin: as to lust, to swearing, and cursing, and reviling, and fighting, or the like.

*Qualification 14.* 14. Those also are sinful which are the exercise of greed, to win other men's money from them; or that tend to stir up greed in those you play with.

*Qualification 15.* 15. Cruel recreations also are unlawful: as taking pleasure in the beholding of duelists, fighters, or any that abuse each other; or any other creatures that needlessly torment each other.

*Qualification 16.* 16. Too costly recreation also is unlawful: when you are only God's stewards, and must be accountable to him for all you have, it is sinful to spend it needlessly on amusements.

*Qualification 17.* 17. Unnecessary recreations forbidden by our lawful governors are unlawful. If they were before lawful to you, yet now they are not; because your king, your pastor, your parents, your masters, have power to rule and restrain you in such things; and you must obey them.

*Qualification 18.* 18. Lastly, if you have the choice of various recreations before you, you must choose the fittest: and if you choose one that is less fit and profitable, when a fitter might be chosen, it is your sin; though that which you choose would be lawful, if you had no other.

*In the margin: What to think of common stage plays, gaming, cards, dice, etc.*

By all this it is easy to judge of our common stage plays, gaming, cards, dice, and various other such kinds of amusements. If they have but any one of these evil qualifications they are sinful. And when they are used without very many of them, 1. They are too commonly used by men that never intended to fit themselves for their work and duty by them; indeed, by men that do not live at all to the pleasing and glorifying of God, and do not know what it is to be obediently devoted to his service; indeed, by men that do not live in any constant, honest labor, but make a real trade of their recreations, and use them as the chief business of the day.

2. They are amusements unfit for the ends of lawful recreation, as will easily appear to the impartial. [604] For it is either your bodies or your minds that most need the recreations: either you are sedentary persons, or have a calling of bodily labor: if you are sedentary persons (as students, clerks, and various others), then it is your bodies that have most need of exercise and recreation, and labor is fitter for you than amusement; or at least an active, laboring amusement. And in this case to sit at cards, or dice, or a stage play, is, instead of exercising your bodies, to increase the need of exercising them: it does not stir your parts; it does not warm your blood; it does not help digestion, attraction, assimilation, etc. It does you much more harm than good, as to your very health. But if you are hard laborers, and need rest for your bodies and recreation for your minds; or are lame or sickly, so that you cannot use bodily exercise; then surely a hundred profitable exercises are at hand which are more suitable to your case. You have books of necessity to read (as the word of God); and books of profit to your souls; and books that tend to increase your knowledge in common things, as history, geography, and arts and sciences. And should not these, any of them, be more pleasant than your dice, and cards, and plays?

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). Every NET quotation below was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Where we are:** this file begins Part 2 of Chapter 10 (Directions for the Government of the Body). Part 1 (labor and callings, in three Titles) ends with the previous file. **calling:** Baxter's word for one's vocation or occupation, kept throughout.
- **amusement(s):** Baxter's "sport(s)," following the Glossary; "sporting" is rendered "amusement," and "sport themselves" "amuse themselves." Where "sport" means mockery ("making sport of holy things," "made sport with calling," "make sport of other men's sinning") we use "making fun of" / "made fun by" / "make fun of." Baxter's "sports" covers hunting, hawking, cards, dice, and plays, not only athletic games.
- **Qualification:** the source labels these points "*Qual.* I." to "*Qual.* XVIII." We expand the abbreviation to "Qualification," matching Baxter's own phrase "these evil qualifications" in the paragraph after the list. Baxter numbers each one twice (for example "*Qual.* II. 2."); we keep both numbers as he has them. Qualification 1 has no second number before its opening sentence; its "1." comes at the second sentence, as in the source.
- **Question / Answer:** Baxter's "*Quest.*" and "*Answ.*"
- **natural spirits:** kept. In the physiology of Baxter's day the "spirits" were fine vapors in the body thought to carry life and sensation; "natural spirits" were one kind of them. **imagination:** Baxter's "fantasy" (in his day the faculty of imagination, not a daydream). **natural parts** and "it does not stir your parts": kept; "parts" here means the bodily members and faculties.
- **digestion:** Baxter's "concoction," the old physiological word for digestion. **attraction, assimilation:** kept; in the older physiology, the body's drawing in of nourishment and turning it into its own substance.
- **carnal:** kept; unspiritual, ruled by the flesh (see the Glossary).
- **materially lawful:** kept; lawful in its matter (the thing done), as opposed to its end and manner, which Baxter goes on to distinguish.
- **"truly do.'"** (Qualification 2): the source has a stray closing quotation mark after "truly do"; there is no opening mark, so we dropped it.
- **"to make it for to do his work"** (Qualification 1): the source's wording is awkward; we read it as "to make it fit to do his work" and render it so. **sharpening:** Baxter's "whetting."
- **"you most obey them"** (Qualification 17): the source prints "most"; we read it as a misprint for "must."
- **Word swaps:** **indeed:** "yea." **lack:** "want." **give up:** "forbear." **useless to devise:** "in vain to contrive." **unbefitting:** "unbeseeming." **pleasure-seekers:** "wantons" (Qualification 6); **lewdness:** "wantonness" (Qualification 11). **actors:** "players" (Qualification 9; in Qualification 12 "players" means those playing the game and is kept). **falconers:** "hawkers." **trample:** "tread down." **grain:** "corn" (in British usage any grain crop). **chatter:** "prating." **reviling:** "railing." **duelists:** "duellers." **various:** "divers." **clerks:** "scribes" (people who write for a living). **devoted:** "addicted" (in Baxter's day, given over to something, without the modern sense of dependence). **a real trade:** "a very trade." **an active, laboring amusement:** "a stirring, labouring sport." **would be lawful:** "were lawful."
- **comedians:** kept; in Baxter's day, actors in or writers of comedies. **profane wits:** kept; clever, irreverent talkers or writers.
- **Lord's day:** Sunday, kept as Baxter's term.
- **1 Corinthians 10:31** (Qualification 1): NET wording, beginning at "whether" (the NET's "So" is left off, as Baxter's quotation starts there). The sense is unchanged.
- **Titus 1:15** (Qualification 2): NET wording, with Baxter's own parenthesis "(that is, all things not forbidden)" kept inside the quotation where he placed it, after the first clause. The King James has "defiled" where the NET has "corrupt" and "corrupted"; the sense is unchanged.
- **2 Kings 2:23** (Qualification 8): Baxter's "bald-head" is the King James "Go up, thou bald head." We give the NET's word, "baldy." Baxter's "children" follows the King James "little children"; the NET has "young boys." The bears are in verse 24, which Baxter does not cite.
- **Ephesians 5:3, 4** (Qualification 11, important): NET wording of the part Baxter quotes. The King James has "let it not be once named among you," so that the sins are not even to be spoken of; Baxter leans on that when he condemns plays that represent filthiness with obscene words. The NET's "among you there must not be" says only that the sins must not exist among them, which weakens that point. The NET's "vulgar speech" and "coarse jesting" (King James "filthiness" and "jesting") narrow the sense to obscene talk, which fits his argument about obscene recreations. Flagged.
- **Note 604:** the line is probably from Horace's *Epistles*, Book 2, Letter 1, where Horace complains of noisy shows at Rome; we have not checked the exact line number.
- **Range ends** partway through Baxter's second reason against stage plays, cards, and dice; the next file continues it.
