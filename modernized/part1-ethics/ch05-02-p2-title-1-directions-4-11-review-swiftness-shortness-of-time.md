# Chapter 5: Title 1, Directions 4-11 (the review of time, the dying, the judgment, lost time, swift time, unrecoverable time, short time, uncertain time)

**Direction 4. Consider on the contrary how sad the review of ill-spent time is, and how you will wish you had spent it when it is gone.**

Do you now have any comfort in looking back on your despised hours? I will not so far wrong your understanding, as to question whether you know that you must die. But your sin allows me to ask you, whether at your dying hour it will be any comfort to you to remember your pastimes? And whether it will then better please you, to find upon your account, so many hours spent in doing good to others, and so many in prayer, and studying the Scriptures and your heart, and in preparing for death and the life to come; so many in your calling obediently managed with a view to eternity? or to hear, so many hours spent in idleness, and so many in needless amusements and plays, hawking and hunting, courting and wantonness; and so many in gathering and providing for the flesh, and so many in satisfying its greedy lusts? Which reckoning does your conscience think would be most comfortable to you at the last? I put it to your own conscience, if you were to die tomorrow, how you would spend this present day? Would you spend it in idleness and empty pastimes? Or if you were to die this day, where would you be found, and about what activities? Would you rather death found you in a playhouse, a gaming house, an alehouse, in your fleshly jollity and pleasure? or in a holy walking with your God, and serious preparing for the life to come? Perhaps you will say, that, if you had but a day to live, you would lay aside the labors of your calling, and yet that does not prove them sinful. But, I answer, there is a great difference between an evil, and a small, unseasonable good. If death found you in your honest calling, holily managed, conscience would not trouble you for it as a sin: and if you rather choose to die in prayer, it is but to choose a greater duty in its season: but surely you would be loath on another account to be found in your time-wasting pleasures! And conscience, if you have a conscience, would make you dread it as a sin. You will not wish at death that you had never labored in your lawful calling, though you would be found in a more seasonable work; but you will wish then, if you understand yourself, that you had never lost one minute's time, and never known those sinful empty pursuits and temptations which occasioned it. O spend your time as you would review it!

**Direction 5. Go hear and note how other men at death value time, and how they wish then that they had spent it.**

It is hardly possible for men in health, especially in prosperity and security, to imagine how precious time appears to an awakened, dying man! Ask them then whether life is too long, and men have any time to spare? Ask them then whether lazing or working, playing or praying, is the better spending of our time? Both good and bad, saints and pleasure-seekers, are usually then high esteemers of time. Oh! then what would an ungodly, unprepared sinner give for some of the time which he used before as worth nothing! Then the most holy servants of Christ are aware how they sinned, in losing any of their time! Oh! then how earnestly do they wish, that they had made much of every minute! and they that did most for God and their souls, that they had done much more! Now if they were to pray over their prayers again, how earnestly would they beg! and how much more good would they do, if time and talents were restored! I knew closely a most holy, grave, and reverend minister, who was so affected by the words of a godly woman, who at her death often and vehemently cried out, O call time back! O call time back! that the sense of it seemed to remain on his heart, and appear in his praying, preaching, and conduct to his death. Now you have time to cast away upon every nothing; but then you will say with David, "Take note of my brief lifespan" (Psalm 89:47). And as "Hagar sat down and wept when her water was spent" (Genesis 21:15-16); so then you will lament when time is gone, or just at an end, that you valued it no more while you had it! O sleepy sinner! your heart cannot now conceive how you will value time, when you hear the physicians say, You are a dead man! and the minister say, You must prepare now for another world! When your heart says, All my days are gone: I must live on earth no more! All my preparing time is at an end! Now what is undone must be undone forever! Oh that you had now but the esteem of time, which you will have then, or immediately after! Then, O pray for me, that God will recover me and try me once again! Oh then how I would spend my time! And is it not a most incongruous thing to see the same persons now idle and toy away their time, and perhaps think that they do no harm, who know that shortly they must cry to God, Oh for a little more time, Lord, to do the great work that is yet undone; a little more time to make sure of my salvation! May not God then tell you, you had time till you did not know what to do with it. You had so much time that you had many and many an hour to spare for idleness and empty pursuits, and that which you were not ashamed to call pastime.

**Direction 6. Remember also that when judgment comes, God will call you to account, both for every hour of your misspent time, and for all the good which you should have done in all that time, and did not do it.**

If you must give account for every idle word, then surely for every idle hour (Matthew 12:36). And if we must be judged according to all the talents we have received, and the profitable use of them required of us, then certainly for so precious a talent as our time (Matthew 25). And how should that man spend his time that believes he must give such account of all? Even to the most just and holy God, who will judge all men according to their works; and cause them all to reap as they have sown. O spend your time as you would hear of it in judgment!

**Direction 7. Remember how much time you have lost already: and therefore if you are not impenitent, and unfeeling about your loss, it will stir you to redeem with the greater diligence the remnant which mercy shall grant you.**

How much did you lose in childhood, youth, and riper age! how much have you lost in ignorance! how much in negligence! how much in fleshly pleasure and empty pursuits! how much in worldliness, and many other sins! Oh that you knew but what a loss it was, if it had been but one year, or week, or day! Do you think you have spent your time as you should have done; and as befitted those that had such work to do? If not, do you repent of it, or do you not? If you do not, you have no hope to be forgiven. If you do repent, you will surely not go on to do the same. Who will believe that he repents of gaming, reveling, or other idle loss of time, who does so still while he professes to repent? He that has lost the beginning of the day, must go the faster in the end, if he will complete so great a journey. Can you remember the hours and years that you have misspent, in the follies of childhood, and the empty pursuits of thoughtless youth, and yet still trifle, and not be stirred by penitent shame and fear, to diligence? Have you not yet cast away enough of such a precious treasure, but you will also belittle the little which remains?

**Direction 8. Remember the swift and constant motion of your neglected time.**

What haste it makes! and never stays! That which was here while you spoke the last word, is gone before you can speak the next! Whatever you are doing, or saying, or thinking of, it is passing on without delay! It does not stay while you sleep! Whether you remember, and observe it, and make use of it, or not, it glides away! It does not wait for your leisure! It hastens as fast while you play, as while you work; while you sin, as when you repent! No monarch so potent as to command it a moment to attend his will! We have no more Joshuas to stop the sun. It is above the jurisdiction of the princes of the earth: it will not hear them if they command or request it to delay its haste but the smallest moment! Crowns and kingdoms would be no price, to hire it to loiter but while you draw another breath! Your lives are not like the clothes of the Israelites in the wilderness, that do not wear out; but like the provisions of the Gibeonites, worn and wasted while you are passing but a little way! And is time so swift, and you so slow? Will you stand still and see it pass away, as if you had no use for it; no work to do; nor any account to give?

**Direction 9. Consider also how unrecoverable time is when it is past.**

Take it now, or it is lost forever. All the men on earth, with all their power, and all their intelligence, are not able to recall one minute that is gone! All the riches in this world cannot redeem it, by reversing one of those hours or moments, which you so prodigally cast away for nothing. If you were to cry and call after it till you tear your hearts, it will not return. Many a thousand have tried this by sad experience, and have cried out too late, Oh that we had now that time again which we made so light of! But none of them ever attained their wish! No more will you. Take it therefore while you have it. It is now as liberal to the poorest beggar as to the greatest prince! Time is as much yours as his. Though in your youth and folly you spend as out of the full heap, as if time would never have an end, you will find it is not like the widow's oil, or the loaves and fishes, multiplied by a miracle; but the hour is at hand, when you will wish you had gathered the fragments and the smallest crumbs, that nothing of so precious a commodity had been lost; even the little minutes, which you thought you might neglect and be no losers. Try whether you can stop the present moment, or recall that which is gone by already, before you belittle or loiter away any more; lest you repent too late.

**Direction 10. Think also how exceedingly little time you have, and how near you always stand to eternity.** [281]

"Does not humanity have hard service on earth? Are not their days also like the days of a hired man?" (Job 7:1). "Man, born of woman, lives but a few days, and they are full of trouble. He grows up like a flower and then withers away; he flees like a shadow and does not remain" (Job 14:1-2). "My days are swifter than a runner, they speed by without seeing happiness. They glide by like reed boats, like an eagle that swoops down on its prey" (Job 9:25-26). Oh, what is this inch of hasty time! How quickly will it all be gone! Look back on all the time that is past: if you have lived sixty or eighty years, what is it now? Does it not seem as yesterday since you were a child? Do not days and nights wheel on quickly? O man! how short is your stay on earth! How small a time will leave you in eternity! What a small and hasty moment will bring you to the state in which you must remain forever! Every night is as the death or end of one of the few that are here allotted you. How little a while is it till your mortal sickness!--till you must lie under languishing decays and pain!--till your vital powers will give up their office, and your pulse will cease, and your soul will take its silent, unseen flight, and leave your body to be hidden in darkness, and carried by your friends to the common earth! How short a time is it between this and the digging of your grave!--between your pleasures in the flesh, and your sad farewell, when you must say of all your pleasures, They are gone!--between your cares and affairs for this world, and your entrance into another world, where all these empty pursuits are of no esteem! How short is the time between your sin, and your account in judgment!--between the pleasure and the pain!--and between the patient holiness of the godly, and their full reward of endless joys! And can you spare any part of so short a life? Has God allotted you so little time, and can you spare the devil any of that little? Is it not all little enough for so great a work, as is necessary to your safe and comfortable death? O remember, when sloth or pleasure would have any, how little you have in all!--and out of how small a stock you spend!--how little you have for the one thing necessary!--the providing for eternal life!--and how unseasonable it is to be playing away time, so near the entrance into the endless world!

**Direction 11. Remember also how uncertain that little time is, which you must have.**

As you know it will be short, so you do not know how short. You never yet saw the day or hour, in which you were sure to see another. And is it a thing befitting the reason of a man, to laze or cast away that day or hour, which for all he knows may be his last? You think that though you are not certain, yet you are likely to have more: but nothing that is hazardous should be admitted in a business of such importance. Indeed, when the longest life is short; and when so frail a body, liable to so many hundred illnesses and accidents, and so sinful a soul, make it probable, as well as possible, that the thread of your life should be cut off before long, even much before your natural period; when so many score at younger years come to the grave, for one that arrives at the ripeness of old age; is not then the uncertainty of your time a great aggravation of the sinfulness of your not redeeming it? If you were sure you had but one year to live, it would perhaps make you so wise, as to see that you had no time to spare. And yet do you waste it, when you do not know that you will live another day? Many a one is this week trifling away their time, who will be dead the next week; who yet would have spent it better if they had thought but to have died the next year. O man! what if death comes before you have made your necessary preparation? Where are you then? When time is uncertain as well as short, have you not work enough of weight to spend it on? If Christ had set you to attend and follow him in greatest holiness a thousand years, should you not have gladly done it? And yet can you not hold out for so short a life? Can you not watch with him one hour? He himself was stirred by the nearness of his death, to a speedy dispatch of the works of his life. And should not we? He sends to prepare his last communion feast with his disciples, thus: "My time is near. I will observe the Passover with my disciples at your house" (Matthew 26:18). And "I have earnestly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer" (Luke 22:15). So should you rather say, My time is short; my death is at hand; and therefore it concerns me to live in the knowledge and communion of God, before I go from here into his presence; especially when "no one knows his appointed time" (Ecclesiastes 9:12). Many thousands would have done better in their preparations, if they had known the end of their time. "But understand this: If the owner of the house had known at what time of night the thief was coming, he would have been alert and would not have let his house be broken into. Therefore you also must be ready because the Son of Man will come at an hour when you do not expect him" (Matthew 24:43-44). "Watch out! Stay alert! For you do not know when the time will come" (Mark 13:33).

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source. A draft English translation follows the Latin in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[281] Ex ipsâ vitâ discedimus, tanquam ex hospitio, non tanquam ex domo: commorandi enim nobis natura diversorium non habitandi domum dedit. Cic. in Cat. Maj. (English: We depart from life as from an inn, not as from a home: for nature has given us a lodging to stay in, not a house to dwell in. Cicero, *Cato Maior* [*On Old Age*].)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). Every verse in quotation marks as NET was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Where this file sits:** it continues Title 1 of Chapter 5 ("Tit. 1" in the source, Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," that is, a heading or division of the chapter) and begins at the start of Direction 4; the Title heading itself is in the previous file. It ends at the close of Direction 11; the next file continues.
- **Direction headings:** in the source each Direction's heading sentence runs on into its body in one paragraph. We set the heading sentence in bold on its own line, as the finished files do; no sentence is split or merged.
- **Psalm 89:47 (flag):** Baxter quotes the King James, "Remember how short my time is," and uses it for the dying man's sense of short time. The NET reads "Take note of my brief lifespan," which keeps the sense of shortness but speaks of a lifespan rather than "time," the word Baxter's whole chapter turns on. Baxter attributes the words to David; the NET's heading to Psalm 89 (printed as part of verse 1) names Ethan the Ezrahite. Kept as Baxter wrote it.
- **Genesis 21:15-16 (paraphrase):** "Hagar sat down and wept when her water was spent" is Baxter's own summary of the two verses, so we kept his wording in his quotation marks. The NET reads: "When the water in the skin was gone, she shoved the child under one of the shrubs. Then she went and sat down by herself across from him ... So she sat across from him and wept uncontrollably." We kept "spent" because it echoes his "spending" of time.
- **Matthew 12:36 (flag):** Baxter's "every idle word" (King James) is not quoted but argued from: "every idle word, then surely for every idle hour." The NET has "every worthless word," which loses the link to "idle" hours. We kept Baxter's "idle word."
- **Matthew 25** (cited only): the parable of the talents. **who will judge all men according to their works; ... reap as they have sown** (unmarked allusions; compare Romans 2:6 and Galatians 6:7).
- **Job 7:1 (flag):** Baxter quotes the King James, "Is there not an appointed time to man upon earth?", and uses it for the shortness and fixed limit of life. The NET reads "Does not humanity have hard service on earth?", which speaks of hard service (military or forced labor), not an appointed time, so the first half of the verse no longer supports his point; the second half ("the days of a hired man") still does.
- **Job 14:1-2; Job 9:25-26:** NET wording. Baxter's "he cometh forth like a flower, and is cut down" is "He grows up like a flower and then withers away" in the NET. His "Now my days are swifter than a post" has no "Now" in the NET, and "post" (courier) is "runner"; "the swift ships" are "reed boats." In 9:26 the NET has no space after the line break ("reed boats,like"); we added it.
- **Matthew 26:18; Luke 22:15:** NET wording. Baxter's "My time is at hand" is "My time is near." His next sentence ("My time is short; my death is at hand") echoes the King James "at hand"; we kept "at hand" in his own words. His "communion-feast" is rendered "communion feast."
- **Ecclesiastes 9:12:** Baxter writes "especially when, as Eccles. ix. 12, 'Man knoweth not his time.'" Following the project's reference style we put the reference in parentheses after the quotation, which drops his "as." The NET has "appointed time" where the King James has "his time."
- **Matthew 24:43-44 (reference corrected):** Baxter cites only verse 43 but quotes through verse 44 ("therefore be ye also ready ..."). We cite 24:43-44. The NET's "owner of the house" replaces the King James "good man of the house"; "in such an hour as ye think not" is "at an hour when you do not expect him."
- **Mark 13:33 (flag):** Baxter quotes the King James "Take ye heed, watch and pray." The NET reads "Watch out! Stay alert!" with no "and pray"; we have not checked the reason, but it is probably a difference in the Greek text followed. Baxter does not build on "pray" here.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "Can you not watch with him one hour?" (Matthew 26:40; NET: "So, couldn't you stay awake with me for one hour?"); "Joshuas to stop the sun" (Joshua 10:12-14); "the clothes of the Israelites in the wilderness, that do not wear out" (Deuteronomy 8:4; 29:5); "the provisions of the Gibeonites, worn" (Joshua 9:3-13, where the Gibeonites' old bread and worn wineskins were a ruse); "the widow's oil" (2 Kings 4:1-7, or perhaps 1 Kings 17:8-16); "the loaves and fishes ... gathered the fragments ... that nothing ... had been lost" (John 6:12); "the one thing necessary" (Luke 10:42).
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): in order to eternity / with a view to eternity; sports / amusements; vain pastimes / empty pastimes; exercises / activities; lay by / lay aside; loth / loath; vanities, vanity / empty pursuits; mark / note; set by (time) / value; slugging, slug / lazing, laze; sensualists / pleasure-seekers; sensible / aware; as nothing worth / as worth nothing; knew familiarly / knew closely; divine / minister (a clergyman or theologian); conversation / conduct; call time again / call time back; improvement (of talents) / profitable use; insensible of / unfeeling about; provoke / stir; vouchsafe / grant; beseemed / befitted; perform (a journey) / complete; inconsiderate / thoughtless; vilify / belittle (he means treat as cheap, not slander); stayeth not your leisure / does not wait for your leisure; wax not old / do not wear out; wit / intelligence; threescore or fourscore / sixty or eighty; apace / quickly; abode / stay; undiscerned / unseen; businesses / affairs; for aught he knows / for all he knows; moment (weight) / importance; Yea / Indeed; maladies and casualties / illnesses and accidents; ere long / before long; despatch / dispatch; hence / from here; period / end.
- **Kept words:** *security* (Direction 5) is Baxter's term for careless confidence. *pastime(s)* is kept; Baxter plays on it ("that which you were not ashamed to call pastime," that is, a passing away of time). *wantonness* means lewdness or loose living. *playhouse* is a theater. *upon your account* means in the record God keeps of your use of time. *comfortable* (Directions 4 and 10) means comforting, giving consolation, not merely at ease. *calling* is one's trade or occupation. *talents* (Direction 5) carries both the parable's sense and abilities. *potent* and *prodigally* are kept as ordinary English. *natural period* (Direction 11) means the natural end of life. *redeem*, *redeeming* (time) is the chapter's key term (see Ephesians 5:16).
- **Dashes:** the Gutenberg text's "--" run-in dashes in Direction 10 are Baxter's printed dashes inside his exclamations; we kept them as "--" rather than turn them into headings.
- **Source oddities:** "alloted" (Direction 10) is a misprint for "allotted." "Thou alway standest" (Direction 10 heading) has the older form "alway." Direction 10 shifts from "thou" to "you" partway through, and so does Direction 11; in modern English both are "you," so the shift disappears.
- **Cicero** (note 281): the passage is from Cicero's dialogue on old age (*Cato Maior de Senectute*), as Baxter's "Cat. Maj." indicates; we have not checked the chapter.
- **Direction 5, the woman's cry and the minister:** Baxter's own report of someone he knew; we have not tried to identify the minister.
