# Chapter 8: Part 5, Title 2, directions against inward lusts (6–16)

*This file continues the directions of Part 5, Title 2 (against inward lusts) from Direction 6; Directions 1–5 are in the previous file.*

**Direction 6. Abhor lascivious, immodest speech: as such words come from either empty or filthy hearts, and show the absence of the fear of God, so they tend to make the hearer like the speaker.**

And if your ears merely grow patient and reconcilable to such talk, you have lost much of your innocence already. Christians must abhor the mentioning of such filthy sins, in any other manner, but such as tends to bring the hearers to abhor them. "Do not be deceived; evil words corrupt good morals" (1 Corinthians 15:33). "You must let no unwholesome word come out of your mouth, but only what is beneficial for the building up of the one in need, that it would give grace to those who hear. And do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God." Corrupt communication is rotten, stinking communication; and none but dogs and crows love carrion. But "among you there must not be either sexual immorality, impurity of any kind, or greed" (πλεονεξια, inordinate lust or luxury), "as these are not fitting for the saints. Neither should there be vulgar speech, foolish talk, or coarse jesting," etc.

**Direction 7. Abhor the covering of filthy lust with handsome names to make it the more acceptable.**

Their talk is more dangerous that would thus dress up an ugly lust, than theirs that speak of it in nasty language. Thus among the brutish party, it goes under the names of love, and having a mistress, and courting, and suchlike. But (as one said that is cited in Stobaeus) it is doubled. Lust, that is commonly called love, and doubled love is stark madness. If filthiness will walk about in public, let it go for filthiness, and appear as it is.

**Direction 8. Avoid the reading of romances, and love stories; which are the library of Venus; or the devil's books of the lustful art; to cover over filthiness with clean names, and bewitch the imaginations of fools with fine words; to make men conceive of the ready way to hell, under the notions and images of excellence, beauty, love, gallantry; and by representing strong and amorous passions, to stir up the same passions in the reader.**

As he that insists on reading a conjuring book is well enough served if devils come about his ears; so they that insist on reading such romances and other books of the burning art, it is just of God to allow an unclean devil to possess them, and to allow them to catch the fever of lust, which may not only burn up the heart, but cause that pernicious delirium in the brain, which is the ordinary symptom of it.

**Direction 9. Avoid all wanton stage plays and dances, which either cover the odiousness of lust, or produce temptations to it. [466]**

As God has his preachers, and holy assemblies and exercises, for the communion of saints, and the stirring up of love and holiness; so these are Satan's instruments, and assemblies, and exercises, for the communion of sinners, and for the stirring up of lust and filthiness. They that will go to the devil's church deserve to be possessed with his principles, and numbered with his disciples. The ancient Christians were very severe against the seeing of these *spectacula*, shows or plays; especially in any of the clergy.

**Direction 10. Avoid all tempting, unnecessary ornaments or attire, and the regarding or gazing on them on others.**

It is a brazen, lustful desire to seem attractive and lovable, which is the common cause of this excess. The folly, or lust, or both, of fashion-followers and gaudy gallants is so conspicuous to all in their affected dress, that never did pride more cross itself, than in such displays of such disgraceful folly or lust. [467] They that take it on themselves to be adversaries to lust, and yet are careful when they present themselves to sight to appear in the most adorned manner, and do all that harlots can do to make themselves a snare to fools, do put the charitable hard to it, whether to believe that it is their tongues or their backs that are the liar. As Jerome says, You deserve hell, though none are the worse for you; for you brought the poison, if there had been any to drink it. Let your apparel be suited not only to your rank, but to your disease. If you are inclined to lust, go the more plainly clad yourself, and do not gaze on the ornaments of others. It is folly indeed that will be enamored of the tailor's work: yet this is so common, that it is frequently more the apparel than the person that entices first; and plain rags would have prevented the deceit; as the poet says,

> *Auferimur cultu: gemmis auroque: teguntur*
> *Omnia: pars minima est ipsa puella sui.* [468]

**Direction 11. Think of your tempting object as it is within, and as it shortly will appear without.**

How ordinary is it for that which you call beauty to be the portion of a fool; and a fair skin to cover a silly, childish, peevish mind, and a soul that is enslaved to the devil. And as Solomon says, Proverbs 11:22, "Like a gold ring in a pig's snout is a beautiful woman who rejects discretion." And will you lust after such an adorned thing? Think also what a dunghill of filth is covered with those ornaments; that it would turn your stomach if you saw what is within them. And think what a face that would be, if it were only covered with the pox; and what a face it will be when sickness or age has consumed or wrinkled it; and think what your admired carcass will be, when it has lain a few days in the grave: then you would have little mind for it; and how quickly will that be! O man, there is nothing truly lovable in the creature, but the image of God; the wisdom, and holiness, and righteousness of the soul. Love this then, if you will love with wisdom, with purity and safety; for the love of purity is pure and safe.

**Direction 12. Think of your own death, and how fast you hasten to another world.**

Is a lustful heart a fitting temper for one that is ready to die, and ready to see God, and come into that world, where there is nothing but pure and holy does abide?

**Direction 13. Consider well the tendency and fruits of lust, that it may always appear to your minds as ugly and terrible as it is indeed.**

1. Think what a shame it is to the soul, that can no better rule the body, and that it is so much defiled by its lusts. 2. Think what an unfit companion it is to lodge in the same heart with Jesus Christ and the Holy Spirit. Shall a member of Christ be thus polluted? Shall the temple of the Holy Spirit be thus turned into a pigsty? Is lust fit to dwell with the love of God? Will you entertain your Lord with such odious company? What an unkindness and injury is this to God, that when he that dwells in the highest heavens condescends to take up a dwelling in your heart, you should bring these toads and snakes into the same room with him. Take heed lest he take it unkindly and be gone. He has said he will dwell with the humble and contrite heart; but where did he say, I will dwell in a lustful heart? 3. Think how unfit it makes you for prayer, or any holy address to God. What a shame, and fear, and deadness it casts upon your spirit. 4. And think how it tends to worse. Lust tends to actual filthiness, and that to hell; do not cherish the eggs if you would have none of the brood. It is an easy step, from a lustful heart to a defiled body, and a shorter step from there to everlasting horror than you imagine. As St. James says, "each one is tempted when he is lured and enticed by his own desires. Then when desire conceives, it gives birth to sin, and when sin is full grown, it gives birth to death" (James 1:14–15). Galatians 6:8, "the person who sows to his own flesh will reap corruption from the flesh." Remember that lust is the spawn of sin, and sin is the way to hell.

**Direction 14. Be sure to keep up a holy, constant government over your thoughts.**

Do not allow them to go after tempting, filthy, physical things. As soon as ever a thought of lust comes into your mind, abhor it and cast it out. Much of the cure and of your safety lies upon your thoughts. They that let their thoughts run uncontrolled, and feed on filthiness, are already fornicators in the heart; and are hatching the cockatrice eggs; and no wonder if from thoughts they proceed to deeds. Oh what a great deal of uncleanness is committed by the thoughts, which people are little ashamed of, because they are unseen by men! If the thoughts of many were open to beholders, what wantonness and lust would appear in many adorned sepulchers! Even in the time of holy worship, when once such give the unclean spirit possession of their thoughts, how hard he is to cast out! they can scarcely look an attractive person in the face without some vicious thought. If Jerome confesses that in his wilderness his thoughts were running among the ladies at Rome, what may we think of them that feed such filthy fantasies? Do not say, you cannot rule your thoughts: you can do much if you will, and more than you do. If money and honor can make an ungodly preacher command his thoughts to holy things, in the studies of divinity, through much of his life, you may see that your thoughts are much in your power. But of this before.

**Direction 15. If other means do not serve, open your case to some friend, and shame yourself to him, as I advised under the former title.**

Confession, and shame, and advice, will help you.

**Direction 16. Above all go to Christ for help, and beg his Spirit, and give up your heart to better things.**

Oh, if it were taken up with God, and heaven, and the holy life that is necessary to it, these things are so great, and holy, and sweet, and of such concern to you, that they would leave little room for lust within you, and would make you abhor it as contrary to those things which have your heart. No such cure for any carnal love as the love of God; nor for fleshly lusts, as a spiritual, renewed, heavenly mind. You would then tell Satan that God has taken up all the room, and your narrow heart is too little for him alone; and that there is no room for lust, or the thoughts that serve it. A true conversion which turns the heart to God does turn it from this with other sins, though some sparks may still be unextinguished. It was once noted that many turn from other sects to the Epicureans, but none from the Epicureans to any other sect; the reason was because nature is inclined to indulgence in physical pleasure in all, and when it is confirmed by use and doctrine, philosophy is too weak to master it. But Christ calls and saves epicures, and publicans, and harlots, and has cleansed many such by his grace, which teaches men "to reject godless ways and worldly desires and to live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives in the present age." Philostratus tells us of a sudden change upon one Isaeus, that turned him from luxury to exceeding temperance: so that when one asked him, Is not that a handsome woman over there? he answered, The diseases of my eyes are cured. When they asked him which dish was the pleasantest, he answered, *Desii curare*, I have stopped regarding such things: and told those that marveled at his change the reason, Because he found that he only gathered fruits out of Tantalus' garden. They are "deceitful desires" (Ephesians 4:22), and Satan himself will reproach you forever, if he can deceive you by them. As Alexander when he had taken Darius's finery, and sumptuous houses, and furniture, reproaches him with it, saying, *Hoccine erat imperare?* Was this to rule? So Satan would show you your lusts and say, Was this to be a Christian and seek salvation?

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). Every verse quoted as NET was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Labels:** this file sits in Part 5, Title 2 of Chapter 8. "Tit." in the source is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," a heading or division within a Part. The range begins at the start of Direction 6 and ends with the end of Direction 16. "The former title" (Direction 15) refers to Title 1 of Part 5 (against fornication), whose directions are in earlier files; we have not checked which direction there gives the advice to confess to a friend.
- **1 Corinthians 15:33:** Baxter's "Be not deceived; evil words corrupt good manners" is his own adaptation of the King James "evil communications corrupt good manners," and he leans on it as a verse about *speech*. The NET reads "Do not be deceived: 'Bad company corrupts good morals,'" which is about companions, not words, so it does not support his point in the same way. Following the conventions for a paraphrase, we keep his wording (modernized: "manners" in the old sense of morals or character becomes "morals") and give the NET here.
- **Ephesians 4:29–30:** quoted without a reference in the source; the reference is ours. NET wording, ending where Baxter ends ("And do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God"; the NET continues "by whom you were sealed for the day of redemption"). The King James Baxter quotes has "Let no corrupt communication proceed out of your mouth." His next sentence ("Corrupt communication is rotten, stinking communication") builds on that King James phrase, which the NET renders "unwholesome word"; we keep his sentence as he wrote it. The NET's "unwholesome" (literally rotten) still fits his gloss.
- **Ephesians 5:3–4:** quoted without a reference in the source; the reference is ours. NET wording. Baxter inserts the Greek πλεονεξια (*pleonexia*) with his own gloss "inordinate lust or luxury" in place of the King James "covetousness"; the NET translates that word "greed." We keep his Greek and gloss in parentheses outside the quotation marks. Two shifts matter for his argument: (1) he reads *pleonexia* here as sexual excess, while the NET (like the King James) reads it as greed; (2) the King James "let it not once be *named* among you," which suits a Direction about speech, becomes "there must not be" in the NET. His "&c." is rendered "etc."; "jesting" is "coarse jesting" in the NET.
- **Proverbs 11:22:** NET wording. Baxter's King James has "a fair woman which is without discretion"; the NET's "who rejects discretion" makes it a choice rather than a lack, which does not change his point.
- **James 1:14–15:** Baxter prints "James i. 13, 14," but the words he quotes are verses 14–15; we have corrected the reference. NET wording. The King James "drawn away of his own lust" and "when lust hath conceived" become "his own desires" and "when desire conceives" in the NET. Baxter's whole use of the verse rests on the word "lust"; the NET's "desire" is broader.
- **Galatians 6:8:** NET wording. Baxter's "If ye sow to the flesh, of the flesh ye shall reap corruption" is a loose form of the King James "he that soweth to his flesh shall of the flesh reap corruption"; the sense is unchanged.
- **Titus 2:12:** quoted without a reference in the source; the reference is ours. NET wording. The King James "deny ungodliness and worldly lusts" becomes "reject godless ways and worldly desires," and "soberly" becomes "self-controlled." Again the NET's "desires" is wider than Baxter's "lusts."
- **Ephesians 4:22:** NET wording. King James "deceitful lusts"; NET "deceitful desires."
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording; references ours): a member of Christ and the temple of the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 6:15, 19); God dwelling "with the humble and contrite heart" (Isaiah 57:15, where the King James has "with him also that is of a contrite and humble spirit"; the NET reads "with the discouraged and humiliated," so the NET does not carry Baxter's wording); "fornicators in the heart" (Matthew 5:28); "cockatrice eggs" (Isaiah 59:5); "adorned sepulchers" (Matthew 23:27, whitewashed tombs); "the unclean spirit" (Mark 1:23 and elsewhere); Christ receiving publicans and harlots (Matthew 21:31; the NET has "tax collectors and prostitutes"); "the image of God" (Genesis 1:27).
- **Ovid** (Direction 10, note 468): the two Latin lines are kept as printed. Draft translation: "We are carried away by adornment: with gems and gold everything is hidden; the girl herself is the least part of herself." From Ovid's *Remedia Amoris* (*Remedies for Love*), probably around lines 343–344; we have not checked the line numbers.
- ***Desii curare*** and ***Hoccine erat imperare?*** (Direction 16): Baxter translates both himself ("I have done regarding such things"; "Was this to rule?"). We modernize his first rendering to "I have stopped regarding such things."
- ***spectacula*** (Direction 9): Latin, "shows, spectacles"; Baxter glosses it himself.
- **Stobaeus** (Direction 7): a compiler of extracts from Greek authors. Baxter does not name the person quoted, and we have not identified the saying. The passage is compressed: his point seems to be that lust is commonly called "love," and love "doubled" (that is, intensified) is stark madness.
- **Jerome** (Directions 10 and 14): Baxter's "Hierom." The saying in Direction 10 is given in Baxter's English; we have not located it. The confession in Direction 14, that in the desert his thoughts ran among the dances of Roman girls, is probably from Jerome's letter to Eustochium (Letter 22); we have not checked Baxter's source.
- **Philostratus and Isaeus** (Direction 16): probably the story of the sophist Isaeus in Philostratus' *Lives of the Sophists*; not checked. Tantalus, in Greek myth, was punished with fruit and water that always moved out of reach, so "fruits out of Tantalus' garden" means pleasures that never satisfy.
- **The Epicureans** (Direction 16): the saying that many go over to the Epicureans but none come back is attributed in ancient sources, we believe, to the philosopher Arcesilaus (Diogenes Laertius); not checked. "Epicures" here means people devoted to pleasure.
- **Alexander and Darius** (Direction 16): Baxter's "Darius, his gallantry" is the old possessive ("Darius's"); "gallantry" means splendor or finery. The anecdote of Alexander viewing Darius's captured luxury is told by Plutarch in his *Life of Alexander*, as far as we know; we have not compared Baxter's Latin with it.
- **Lysander and Dionysius** (note 467): Baxter's own English translation of the Latin is kept, modernized. The story is probably from Plutarch; not checked.
- **Petrarch** (note 466): probably the dialogue on spectacles in Petrarch's *De remediis utriusque fortunae*; not checked.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): vain → empty; grow but patient → merely grow patient; discourse → talk; becometh → (NET wording); goeth → goes; such like → suchlike; Stobæus → Stobaeus; walk abroad → walk about in public; cleanly (names) → clean; fantasies (of fools) → imaginations; excellency → excellence; will needs read → insists on reading; it is just with God → it is just of God; suffer → allow; deliriation → delirium; dancings → dances; procacious (impudent, forward) → brazen; comely → attractive; amiable → lovable; fashionists → fashion-followers; publications (of folly) → displays; take on them → take it on themselves; Hierom → Jerome; meanly clad → plainly clad; enamoured → enamored; homely (plain; in modern American English "homely" means ugly) → plain; Think on → Think of; but (merely) covered → only covered; have little mind of it → have little mind for it; seemly → fitting; still (always) → always; swine-sty → pigsty; Holy Ghost → Holy Spirit; where said he → where did he say; thence → from there; Abundance of (the cure) → Much of; what a deal of → what a great deal of; unseen of men → unseen by men; how hardly is he cast out → how hard he is to cast out; scarce → scarcely; honour → honor; If other means serve not → If other means do not serve; thereto → to it; concernment → concern; sensuality → indulgence in physical pleasure; sensual (things) → physical; Isæus → Isaeus; yonder → over there; did but gather → only gathered; for ever → forever.
- **Word order:** in Direction 16, "told them the reason, that marvelled at his change, Because…" is given as "told those that marveled at his change the reason, Because…," since "that marvelled" refers to "them." In Direction 8, "As he that will needs read a conjuring book, is well enough served" loses only the comma between subject and verb.
- **Kept words:** *lascivious*; *brutish party* (the beast-like sort of people); *gallants* (fashionable young men); *cross itself* (thwart itself: pride defeats its own aim by displaying folly); *put the charitable hard to it* (make it hard for charitable observers to decide); *conjuring book* (a book of magic spells); *burning art* (Baxter's phrase for the art of inflaming lust); *the pox* (a disease that scars the face, most likely smallpox here); *temper* (disposition); *cockatrice* (a legendary serpent); *vicious* (sinful, not cruel); *divinity* (theology, a technical term); *carnal*; *publicans* (tax collectors); *luxury* (in Isaeus' story and in Baxter's gloss on *pleonexia*, the older sense of lechery or indulgence); *by use* (by habit).
- **Source oddities:** in Direction 12, "where there is nothing but pure and holy doth abide" is ungrammatical as printed (probably "nothing but what is pure and holy"); we keep the words and only modernize "doth." In Direction 8, the second half of the comparison ("so they that will needs read … it is just with God to suffer …") changes construction partway; kept as printed. "But of this before" (end of Direction 14) means "I have spoken of this before"; kept.
- **Baxter's claims** about the early Christians' severity against plays (Direction 9) and his estimate of romances, plays, and dress are his own, kept as his.
