# Chapter 2

**Directions to Young Christians or Beginners in Religion, for Their Establishment and Safe Proceeding** [46]

Before I come to the common directions for the exercise of grace, and walking with God, containing the common duties of Christianity, I shall lay down some preliminary instructions, proper to those that are only newly entered into religion (presupposing what is said in my book of directions to those that are still under the work of conversion, to keep them from going astray by a false, superficial change).

**Direction 1. Take care lest it be the novelty or reputation of truth and godliness that attracts you more than the solid evidence of their excellence and necessity; lest when the novelty and reputation are gone your religion wither and waste away.**

It is said by Christ of John and the Jews, "He was a lamp that was burning and shining, and you wanted to rejoice greatly for a short time in his light" (John 5:35). All men are affected most by things that seem new and strange to them. It is not only the weakness of children, who are pleased with new clothes, and new toys and games; but even to graver, wiser persons new things are most affecting, and commonness and custom dull delight. Our homes, and possessions, and honors are most pleasing to us at first; and every condition of life affects us most at first: if nature were not much for novelty, the publishing of newsbooks would not have been so profitable a trade for so long, unless the matter had been truer and more desirable. Hence it is that changes are so welcome to the world, though they ordinarily prove to their cost. No wonder then if religion is the more acceptable when it comes with this advantage. When men first hear the doctrine of godliness, and the tidings of another world, laid open and set home by a powerful preacher, no wonder if things of so great importance affect them for a time: it is said of those who received the seed of God's word on rocky ground that it "sprang up quickly," and that they "immediately" received it "with joy" (Matthew 13:5, 20); but it quickly withered for want of rooting. These kinds of hearers can no more delight continually in one preacher, or one profession, or way, than a glutton in one dish, or an adulterer in one harlot: for it is but a kind of sensory or natural pleasure that they have in the highest truths; and all such delight must be fed with novelty and variety of objects. The Athenians were inquisitive after Paul's doctrine as a novelty, though afterward they rejected it as seeming to them incredible: "May we know what this new teaching is that you are proclaiming? For you are bringing some surprising things to our ears, so we want to know what they mean. (All the Athenians and the foreigners who lived there used to spend their time in nothing else than telling or listening to something new.)" (Acts 17:19–21).

To this kind of professing Christians, the greatest truths go out of fashion, and they grow weary of them as of dull and ordinary things: they must have some new light, or new way of religion that has lately come into fashion: their souls are weary of that manna that at first was acceptable to them as angels' food. Old things seem low, and new things high to them; and to take up some novelty in religion is to grow up to more maturity: and too many such at last so far outgrow their old clothes that the old Christ and old gospel are left behind them.

The light of the gospel is more quickly communicated than the heat; and this first part, being most acceptable to them, is soon received; and religion seems best to them at first. At first they have the light of knowledge alone; and then they have the warmth of a new and prosperous profession: there must be some time for the heat to work before it burns them; and then they have enough, and cast it away in as much haste as they took it up. If preachers would only give light, and shoot no thunderbolts, even a Herod himself would hear them gladly, and do many things as they say; but when his Herodias is meddled with, he cannot bear it. If preachers would speak only to men's fancies or understandings, and not meddle too sharply with their hearts, and lives, and carnal interests, the world would bear them, and hear them as it does stage players, or at least as lecturers in philosophy or medicine. A sermon that has nothing but some general toothless notions in a handsome dress of words seldom provokes offense or persecution: it is rare that such men's preaching is disliked by carnal hearers, or their persons hated for it. "Light is sweet, and it is pleasant for a person to see the sun" (Ecclesiastes 11:7); but not to be scorched by its heat. Christ himself at a distance, as promised, was greatly desired by the Jews: but when he came, they could not bear him; his teaching and life were so contrary to their expectations. "The Lord you are seeking will suddenly come to his temple, and the messenger of the covenant, whom you long for, is certainly coming," says the Lord of Heaven's Armies. "Who can endure the day of his coming? Who can keep standing when he appears? For he will be like a refiner's fire, like a launderer's soap" (Malachi 3:1–2). Many when they first come (by profession) to Christ little think that he would cast them into the fire, and refine them, and purge away their dross, and cast them anew into the mold of the gospel (Romans 6:17). Many will play a while by the light that will not endure to be melted by the fire. When the preacher comes to this, he is harsh and intolerable, and loses all the praise which he had won before, and the pleasing novelty of religion is over with them. The gospel is sent to make such work in the soul and life as these tender persons will not endure: it must take every thought captive to Christ, and kill every lust and pleasure which is against his will; and put a new and heavenly life into the soul: it must possess men with deep and vivid awareness of the great things of eternity; it is not wavering, dull opinions that will raise and carry on the soul to such vigorous, constant, victorious action as is necessary to salvation. When the gospel comes to the heart to do this great prevailing work, then these men are impatient of the search and smart, and at once have done with it. They are like children that love the book for the gilding and fineness of the cover, and take it up as soon as any; but it is to play with, and not to learn; they are weary of it when it comes to that. At first many come to Christ with wonder, and insist on being his servants for something in it that seems fine; until they hear that the Son of Man does not have the comforts of the birds or foxes; and that his teaching and way is hostile to their worldly, fleshly interest, and then they are gone. They first received Christ as a courtesy, thinking that he would please them, or not much contradict them; but when they find that they have received a guest that will rule them, and not be ruled by them, that will not allow them to take their pleasure, nor enjoy their riches, but hold them to a life which they cannot endure, and even undo them in the world, he is then no longer a guest for them. Whereas if Christ had been received as Christ, and truth and godliness deliberately welcomed for their well-discerned excellence and necessity, the deep rooting would have prevented this apostasy, and cured such hypocrisy.

But, alas! poor ministers find by sad experience that all do not prove saints who flock to hear them, and make up the crowd; nor all of those "that for a season rejoice in their light," and magnify them, and take their part. The blossom has its beauty and sweetness; but all that blossoms or appears in the bud does not come to perfect fruit: some will be blighted, and some blown down; some nipped with frosts, some eaten by worms; some quickly fall, and some hang on until the strongest blasts cast them down: some are deceived and poisoned by false teachers; some by worldly cares, and the deceitfulness of riches, become unfruitful and are turned aside; the lusts of some had deeper rooting than the word; and the friends of some had greater hold on them than Christ, and therefore they forsake him to satisfy their persistent demands: some are corrupted by the hopes of advancement, or the favor of man; some are frightened from Christ by their threats and frowns, and choose to risk damnation to escape persecution: and some are so worldly wise that they can see reason to relax their zeal, and can save their souls and bodies too; and prove that to be their duty which other men call sin (if the end will but meet their expectations): and some grow weary of truth and duty, as a dull and common thing, being supplied with that variety which might still continue the delights of novelty.

Yet do not mistake what I have said, as if all the affection furthered by novelty, and lessened by commonness and use, were a sign that the person is but a hypocrite. I know that there is something in the nature of man, remaining in the best, which disposes us to be much more passionately affected by things when they seem new to us, and are first grasped, than when they are old, and we have known or used them long. There is not, I believe, one man in a thousand but is much more delighted in the light of truth when it first appears to him than when it is stale and familiarly known; and is much more affected by a powerful minister at first than when he has long sat under him. The same sermon that even carried them away at the first hearing would affect them less if they had heard it preached a hundred times. The same books which greatly affected us at the first or second reading will affect us less when we have read them over twenty times. The same words of prayer that mean much to us when seldom used move our feelings less when they are used daily all the year. At our first conversion we have more passionate sorrow for our sin, and love for the godly, than we can afterward retain. And all this is the case of learned and unlearned, the sound and unsound, though not of all alike. Even heaven itself is spoken of by Christ as if it shared in this, when he says that "there will be more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents than over ninety-nine righteous people who have no need to repent" (Luke 15:7, 10). And I know it is the duty of ministers to take notice of this disposition in their hearers, and not to dull them by giving them always the same, but to profit them by a pleasant and profitable variety: not by preaching to them another Christ, or a new gospel: it is the same God, and Christ, and Spirit, and Scripture, and the same heaven, the same church, the same faith, and hope, and repentance, and obedience, that we must preach to them as long as we live; though they say, we have heard this a hundred times, let them hear it still, and bring them not a new creed. If they hear so often of God, and Christ, and heaven, until by faith, and love, and enjoyment they attain them as their end, they have heard well. But yet there is a pleasing variety of subordinate particulars, and of words, and methods, and timely applications, necessary to the right performance of our ministry, and to the profiting of the flocks: though the physician uses the same apothecary's shop, and drug manual, and drugs, yet how great a variety he must use of compositions, and times, and manner of administration.

But for all this, though the best are affected most by things that seem new, and are dulled by the long and frequent use of the same expressions, yet they are never weary of the substance of their religion, so as to desire a change. And though they are not so passionately affected by the same sermons, and books, or by the thoughts or mention of the same substantial matters of religion as at first they were; yet their judgments more solidly and firmly embrace them, and esteem them, and their wills as resolutely adhere to them, and use them, and in their lives they practice them better than before. Whereas those that take up their religion but for novelty will lay it down when it ceases to be new to them, and must either change for a newer or have none at all. [47]

And as unsound are those that are religious only because their education, or their friends, or the laws, or the judgment of their rulers, or the custom of the country has made it necessary to their reputation: these are hypocrites at the first setting out, and therefore cannot be saved by continuing in such a carnal religiousness as this. I know law, and custom, and education, and friends, when they side with godliness, are a great advantage to it, by providing help, and removing those obstacles that might weigh heavily with carnal minds. But truth is not your own until it is received on its proper evidence; nor is your faith divine until you believe what you believe because God is true who reveals it; nor are you the children of God until you love him for himself; nor are you truly religious until the truth and goodness of religion itself is the principal thing that makes you religious. It helps much to discover a man's sincerity when he is not only religious among the religious, but among the profane, and the enemies, and scorners, and persecutors of religion: and when a man does not pray only in a praying family, but among the prayerless, and the deriders of fervent constant prayer: and when a man is heavenly among those that are earthly, and temperate among the intemperate and riotous, and holds the truth among those that reproach it and that hold the contrary: when a man is not carried only by a stream of company, or outward advantages, to his religion, nor avoids sin for want of a temptation, but is religious though against the stream, and innocent when cast (unwillingly) upon temptations; and is godly where godliness is regarded as peculiarity, hypocrisy, divisiveness, moodiness, disobedience, or heresy; and will rather let go the reputation of his honesty than his honesty itself.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **John 5:35:** Baxter's "a burning and a shining light" is "a lamp that was burning and shining" in the NET. The NET's "wanted to rejoice greatly for a short time" is Baxter's "willing for a season to rejoice."
- **Matthew 13:5, 20:** NET wording ("sprang up quickly"; "immediately receives it with joy"). Baxter writes "forthwith it sprung up" and "anon with joy received it."
- **Acts 17:19–21:** NET wording. The NET puts verse 21 in parentheses as an aside. Baxter's "bringest certain strange things to our ears" is "bringing some surprising things to our ears."
- **Ecclesiastes 11:7:** NET wording ("Light is sweet"). Baxter's text is "pleasant … to behold the sun."
- **Malachi 3:1–2:** the NET begins verse 1 with "I am about to send my messenger, who will clear the way before me," which Baxter does not quote. We start the quotation where Baxter does. The NET's "launderer's soap" is "fullers' soap" in the King James.
- **Luke 15:7, 10:** NET wording for verse 7. "ninety-nine righteous people who have no need to repent" is Baxter's "ninety and nine just persons, that need no repentance."
- **Romans 6:17** (cited only): "cast them anew into the mold of the gospel" echoes Paul's "pattern of teaching."
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): manna as "angels' food" (Exodus 16; Psalm 78:25); "a Herod … do many things" and Herodias (Mark 6:17–20); "the Son of Man hath not where to lay his head" (Matthew 8:20; Luke 9:58); "the deceitfulness of riches" (Matthew 13:22); "the Jews" desiring and then rejecting Christ.
- **professing Christians:** Baxter's "professors," those who profess religion. **newsbooks:** the printed news pamphlets of Baxter's day. **stage players:** actors. **physic:** medicine.
- **outgrow their old clothes:** Baxter's "overthrive their old apparel." **receive … as a courtesy:** his "entertained … in compliment."
- **sensory or natural pleasure:** Baxter's "sensual or natural pleasure." **peculiarity:** his "singularity." **divisiveness:** his "faction." **moodiness:** his "humour."
- **drug manual:** Baxter's "dispensatory," a book of medicines and how to prepare them. **enjoyment:** his "fruition." **pleasing:** his "grateful."
- **persistent demands:** Baxter's "importunity." **advancement:** his "preferment." **blighted:** his "blasted." **risk:** his "venture."
- **a hundred times** (paragraph 5): Baxter allows that ministers should vary their words and applications, but never the substance of the gospel.
- **"one man in a thousand"; "learned and unlearned"** (paragraph 5): Baxter's point is that novelty affects everyone, so the pull of the new is not itself a sign of insincerity.
- **Note [46]:** Baxter's note that he has since written a book on this subject, probably a reference to his later writing on the early stages of the Christian life.
- **Structure of Chapter 2:** twenty numbered Directions. This section holds the opening and Direction 1. Direction 2 begins at line 5831.
