# Chapter 8: Part 6, against sinful excess of sleep (when sleep is excessive, and wherein its sinfulness consists, 1-3)

**Part 6.**

**Directions against Sinful Excess of Sleep.**

Of this, something is said already, chapter 5, part 1, and more afterwards in the directions against idleness. Therefore I shall say but little now. 1. I shall show you when sleep is excessive. 2. Wherein the sinfulness of it consists. 3. What to do for the cure of it.

I. Sleep is given us for the necessary relaxation of the animal operations, and of the labor or motion of the exterior parts, by the quieting of the senses, or shutting them up: that the natural and vital operations may have the less disturbance. It is necessary, 1. To our rest. 2. To digestion. Therefore weariness and lack of digestion are the chief indications, to tell us how much is needful for us. Sleep is sinfully excessive, 1. When it is voluntarily more than is needful to our health. 2. When it is unseasonable, at forbidden times.

It is not all weariness or sleepiness that makes sleep lawful or needful; for some is contracted by laziness, and some by many diseases, and some by other constant causes which make men almost always weary. Nor is it all lack of digestion that sleep is a remedy for; some may be caused by excess of eating, which must be cured a better way; and many diseases may cause it, which require other cure. Therefore none must indulge excess upon these pretexts. Nor must a present sense of the pleasure of sleeping, or the displeasure of waking, be the judge; for sluggards may think they feel it do them good, and that early rising does them hurt; but this good is but their present ease, and this hurt is but a little trouble to their head, and eyes, and lazy flesh, just at the time. But reason and experience must judge what measure is best for your health, and that you must not exceed. To some five hours is enough; to the ordinary sort of healthy persons six hours is enough; to many weak, sickly persons seven hours is needful: to sick persons I am not to give directions.

2. Sleep is excessive at that particular time when it is unseasonable. As, 1. When we are asleep when we should be doing some necessary business which calls for immediate dispatch. 2. Or when we should be hearing the sermon, or praying, in public or private. In a word, when it puts aside any greater duty which we should then perform. As, when the disciples slept when Christ was in his agony: "couldn't you stay awake with me for one hour? Stay awake and pray that you will not fall into temptation" (Matthew 26:40–41).

It is a foolishness and abuse of God and ourselves, to think that the breaking of our sleep is a thing that of itself pleases God; or that rising to pray at midnight is more acceptable to God than at another hour: usually such rising to pray is sinful, 1. Because it is done in an erroneous notion that God accepts it better than in the daytime. 2. Because they waste time in dressing and undressing. 3. Or else hurt their health by cold in the winter, and so lose more time than they redeem by shortening their lives. 4. And usually they are more drowsy and unfit. But to rise in the night to prayer is fitting on some extraordinary occasion that calls for it; as to pray with or for a dying person, or such like; or when an extraordinary fervor and fitness prepares us for it; and when we can stay up when we are up, and not lose time in going to bed again. But ordinarily that way is to be chosen that best redeems time; and that is, to consider just how much sleep our health requires, and to take it if we can together without interruption, and to rise then and go about our duties. But those that cannot sleep in the night must redeem that time as discretion shall direct them.

It is the voluntariness of the excess that the sinfulness principally consists in; and therefore the more voluntary the more sinful. In a lethargy or caros it is no sin: and when long watching, or some bodily weakness or disorder, make it almost unavoidable, the sin is the smaller: therefore in case of long watching and heaviness, Christ partly excused his disciples, saying, "The spirit is willing, but the flesh is weak" (Matthew 26:41). But when it comes from a flesh-pleasing sloth, or from a disregard of any holy exercise that you are about, it is a grievous sin. And though it is involuntary just at the time, and you say, I would gladly refrain from sleeping now if I could; yet if it is voluntary remotely and in its causes, it is your sin. You would now refrain from sleeping; but you would not refrain from that pampering your body, and stuffing your guts, which causes it; you would not deny the flesh its ease to avoid it.

II. The sinfulness of excess of sleep lies in these particulars: 1. That it is a sinful wasting of every minute of that time which is consumed in it. [469] And this is a very grievous thing, to a heart that is aware of the preciousness of time: when we think how short our lives are, and how great our work is, it should tell us how great a sin it is to cast away any of this little time in needless sleep. And yet what abundance of it with many is thus spent! Almost half their whole lives is spent in bed, by many drones, that think they may sleep because they are rich, and have not a necessity of laboring to supply their wants. I was never tempted (that I remember) so much to grumble at God's natural ordering of man, in anything, as that we are obliged to waste so much of our little time in sleep: nor was I ever tempted to grumble at my weakness so much on any account as this, that it deprived me of so much precious time, which else might have been used in some profitable work. The preciousness of time makes excessive sleeping to be a great sin, according to the measure of the excess.

2. It is a neglect of all our powers and abilities which should all that time be exercised. Reason is idle and buried all that while: all your wisdom and knowledge are of no use to you. [470] All the learning of the greatest scholar in the world is of no more service than if he were illiterate; nor all the prudence and shrewdness of the wisest, than if they were mere idiots. All the strength and health of the strongest are of no more service than if they were sick; nor the skill of the greatest craftsman, than if he had never learned his craft; nor any of your limbs or senses, than if you were lame, or blind, or deaf, or senseless. And I leave it to any man's consideration and judgment, whether if drunkenness is so odious a sin, because it deprives a man voluntarily of the use of his reason and abilities, it must not be a very great sin to do the same by sleeping, by frequent, voluntary, excessive sleeping. For no man I think is drunk so often as the sluggard is dead in sleep: sluggards quite kill their reason, when most drunkards do but maim it, or make it sick. Sluggards bury their wits and abilities usually ten times as long in the year as the filthiest drunkards do. And has God given you reason, and abilities, and strength for no better use than to bury it for so considerable a part of your lives?

3. Excess of sleep is guilty of all the omissions of those duties which should all that time have been performed: of the omission of every holy thought, and word, and deed which should have been then exercised; and of the omission of all the duties of your callings: of the omission of every prayer you should have then prayed, and every chapter you should have read; and all the good which you should have got to yourselves, or done to others, to wife, husband, children, parents, servants, neighbors. And you know that omissions are one half, and the greater half, of the sins of the world; and that God will condemn the wicked at last for their omissions; for not feeding the poor, not clothing them, not visiting: and that he requires the improvement of all his talents; and that it is his terrible sentence, Matthew 25:26, 30, "Evil and lazy slave! ... And throw that worthless slave into the outer darkness, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth." What then shall we think of the willful omission, not of one duty, but of all duty whatsoever; not now and then, but constantly for an hour, or two, or three once in twenty-four hours! No love of God, no desires towards him, no good is exercised all that time.

*In the margin: Whether love of sleep may be a mortal sin?*

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Part 6:** this file opens Baxter's Part VI of Chapter 8. The source later jumps from Part VI to Part VIII (there is no Part VII); that skip falls outside this file, and Baxter's numbering is kept throughout.
- **Cross-references:** "chapter 5, part 1" (Baxter's "chap. v. part i.") and "the directions against idleness" are Baxter's own pointers; we have not checked them against the other files.
- **Matthew 26:40–41** (paragraph 4): NET wording, keeping the extent Baxter quotes (from "Could ye not watch"); the NET's opening "So," is left out. The King James has "watch" twice; the NET has "stay awake" both times. This sharpens, rather than changes, the sense Baxter leans on, since his point is the disciples' sleeping when they should have been awake.
- **Matthew 26:41** (paragraph 6): "The spirit is willing, but the flesh is weak" is the same in the NET as in the King James. **watching** (same paragraph): kept; in Baxter it means going without sleep, staying awake.
- **Matthew 25:26, 30** (paragraph 9): NET wording; Baxter's "&c." between the two verses becomes "...". The King James has "Thou wicked and slothful servant" and "Cast ye the unprofitable servant"; the NET has "Evil and lazy slave" and "throw that worthless slave." Baxter leans on "slothful" and "unprofitable" (doing no good with what one was given), and "lazy" and "worthless" keep that sense, though "worthless" is less tied to the idea of yielding no profit. The reference stays where Baxter placed it, before the quotation.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): God condemning "for not feeding the poor, not clothing them, not visiting" (Matthew 25:42–43); "the improvement of all his talents" (the parable of the talents, Matthew 25:14–30); "redeem time" (compare Ephesians 5:16, Colossians 4:5).
- **animal operations; natural and vital operations** (paragraph 2): kept as Baxter's terms from the older physiology, in which "animal" (from Latin *anima*, soul) operations were sensation and voluntary motion, while "natural" and "vital" operations were bodily processes such as nourishment, heartbeat, and breathing. **relaxation:** Baxter's "remission" (a letting up, a pause).
- **digestion:** Baxter's "concoction" (the older word for the body's digestion of food). **lack:** his "want."
- **sickly:** Baxter's "valetudinary" (in poor or delicate health). **healthy:** his "healthful."
- **pretexts:** Baxter's "pretences." **immediate dispatch:** his "present despatch." **puts aside:** his "puts by."
- **foolishness:** Baxter's "foppery" (folly, foolish affectation). **notion:** his "conceit." **fitting:** his "meet."
- **caros** (paragraph 6): kept; an old medical term (from Greek) for a deep, heavy stupor or sleep from which one can hardly be roused. Like "lethargy," it names a diseased sleepiness. **disorder:** Baxter's "distemper."
- **I would gladly refrain from:** Baxter's "I would fain forbear." **refrain from:** his "forbear" (twice more in the same paragraph). **we are obliged to:** his "we are fain to" (in paragraph 7, where "fain" means compelled, not glad).
- **aware of:** Baxter's "sensible of." **grumble at:** his "grudge at."
- **abilities:** Baxter's "parts" (natural gifts and capacities), throughout paragraph 8. **shrewdness:** his "policy" (practical cunning). **craftsman ... craft:** his "artist ... art" (a skilled worker and his trade). **senseless:** kept in its literal sense, without the use of the senses.
- **improvement** (paragraph 9): kept; in Baxter's usage it means putting something to profitable use.
- **drones; sluggards:** kept as Baxter wrote.
- **twenty-four hours:** Baxter's "four and twenty hours."
- **The margin note** at the end ("Whether love of sleep may be a mortal sin?") stands at the end of this file's line range; it heads Baxter's following question, which begins the next file. "Mortal" is Baxter's technical term (a sin that rules and damns, as opposed to venial).
- **Note 469:** "Ber." is most likely Bernard (of Clairvaux), but we have not confirmed the source of the saying.
- **Note 470:** "Laert." is presumably Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of the Philosophers*. A similar saying ("a sleeping man is worth nothing") is often traced to Plato's *Laws*, book 7, but we have not confirmed where Baxter took it from.
