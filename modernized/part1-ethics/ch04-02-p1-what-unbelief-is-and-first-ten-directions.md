# Chapter 4: What unbelief is, and the first ten Directions against it

**Directions against Unbelief.**

*In the margin: Whether not to believe that my sins are pardoned, is indeed unbelief?*

I know that most poor troubled Christians, when they complain of the sin of unbelief, do mean by it, their not believing that they are sincere believers, and personally justified, and shall be saved. And I know that some divines have affirmed that the sense of that article of the creed, "I believe the remission of sins," is, I believe my sins are actually forgiven. But the truth is, to believe that I am elect or justified, or that my sins are forgiven, or that I am a sincere believer, is not to believe any word of God at all: for no word of God says any of these; nor anything equivalent; nor anything out of which it can be gathered: for it is a rational conclusion; and one of the premises which do infer it must be found in myself by reflection, or internal sense, and self-knowledge. The Scripture only says, "He who truly believes is justified, and shall be saved." But it is conscience, and not belief of Scripture, which must say, I do sincerely believe: therefore the conclusion, that I am justified, and shall be saved, is a rational inference from what I find in Scripture and in myself, set together; and resulting from both, can be no firmer or surer than is the weaker of the premises. Now certainty is objective or subjective; in the thing, or in my apprehension. As to objective certainty in the thing itself, all truths are equally true; but all truths are not equally discernible, there being much more cause of doubting concerning some, which are less evident, than concerning others, which are more evident. And so the truth of God's promise of justification to believers is more certain; that is, has fuller, surer evidence to be discerned by, than the truth of my sincere believing. And, that I sincerely believe is the weaker of the premises, and therefore the conclusion follows this in its weakness; and so can be no article of faith. And as to the subjective certainty, that varies according to men's various apprehensions. The premises, as in their evidence or fitness to make us certain, are the cause of the conclusion as evident, or knowable. And the premises, as apprehended, are the cause of the conclusion as known.

*In the margin: Whether a man can be more certain that he believes, than he is that the thing believed is true?*

Now it is a great doubt with some, Whether a man can possibly be more certain that he believes, than he is that the thing believed is true; because the act can extend no further than the object; and to be sure I believe, is but to be sure that I take the thing believed to be true. But I shall grant the contrary, that a man may possibly be surer that he believes, than he is that the thing believed is true; because my believing is not always a full subjective certainty that the thing is true, but a believing that it is true. And though you are fully certain that all God's word is true; yet you may believe that this is his word, with some mixture of unbelief or doubting. And so the question is but this, Whether you may not certainly, without doubting, know that you believe the word of God to be true, though with some doubting. And it seems you may. But then it is a further question, Whether you can be surer of the saving sincerity of your faith, than you are that this word of God is true. And that ordinarily men doubt of the first, as much as they doubt of the latter, I think is a truth proved by experience. But yet grant that with some it may be otherwise, (because he believes sincerely who so far believes the word of God, as to trust his life and soul upon it, and forsake all in obedience to it: and that I do so, I may know with less doubting, than I yet have about the truth of the word so believed,) all that will follow is but this, that of those men that doubt of their justification and salvation, some of their doubts are caused more by their doubting of God's word, than by the doubting whether they sincerely though doubtingly believe it; and the doubts of others, whether they are justified and shall be saved, are caused much more by their doubting of their own sincere belief, than by their doubting of the truth of Scriptures. And the far greatest number of Christians seem to themselves to be of this latter sort. For no doubt, but though a man of clear understanding can scarcely believe, and yet not know that he believes; yet he may believe sincerely, and not know that he believes sincerely. But still the knowledge of our own justification is but the effect or offspring of our belief of the word of God, and of our knowledge that we do sincerely believe it, which jointly are the parents and causes of it: and it can be no stronger than the weaker of the parents (which *in esse cognoscibili* is our faith, but *in esse cognito* is sometimes the one, and sometimes the other). And the effect is not the cause; the effect of faith and knowledge joined is not faith itself. It is not a believing the word of God, to believe that you believe, or that you are justified; but yet, because that faith is one of the parents of it, some call it by the name of faith, though they should call it but an effect of faith, as one of the causes. And well may our doubtings of our own salvation be said to be from unbelief, because unbelief is one of the causes of them, and the most sinful cause.

*In the margin: The article of remission of sin to be believed applyingly.*

And that the article of remission of sin is to be believed with application to ourselves is certain: but not with the application of assurance, persuasion, or belief that we are already pardoned; but with an applying acceptance of an offered pardon, and consent to the covenant which makes it ours. We believe that Christ has purchased remission of sin, and made a conditional grant of it in his gospel, to all, namely if they will repent, and believe in him, or take him for their Savior, or become penitent Christians. And we consent to do so, and to accept it on these terms. And we believe that all are actually pardoned that thus consent.

By all this you may perceive that those troubled Christians which doubt not of the truth of the word of God, but only of their own sincerity, and consequently of their justification and salvation, do ignorantly complain that they have not faith, or that they cannot believe: for it is no act of unbelief at all, for me to doubt whether my own heart is sincere: this is my ignorance of myself, but it is not any degree of unbelief; for God's word does nowhere say that I am sincere, and therefore I may doubt of this, without doubting of God's word at all. And let all troubled Christians know that they have no more belief in them, than they have doubting or unbelief of the truth of the word of God. Even that despair itself, which has none of this in it, has no unbelief in it (if there is any such). I thought it needful thus far to tell you what unbelief is, before I come to give you directions against it. And though the mere doubting of our own sincerity is no unbelief at all, yet real unbelief of the very truth of the holy Scriptures is so common and dangerous a sin, and some degree of it is latent in the best, that I think we can no way so much further the work of grace, as by destroying this. The weakness of our faith in the truth of Scriptures, and the remnant of our unbelief of it, is the principal cause of all the languishings of our love and obedience, and every grace; and to strengthen faith is to strengthen all. What I have more fully written in my *Saints' Rest*, part 2, and my *Treatise against Infidelity*, I here suppose.

**Direction 1.** Consider well how much of religion nature itself teaches, and reason, (without supernatural revelation,) must needs confess: (as, that there is another life which man was made for, and that he is obliged to the fullest love and obedience to God, and the rest before laid down in the Introduction.) And then observe how fittingly the doctrine of Christ comes in, to help where nature is at a loss, and how exactly it suits with natural truths, and how clearly it explains them, and fully contains so much of them as is necessary to salvation; and how suitable and proper a means it is to attain their ends; and how great a testimony the doctrines of nature and grace give to each other. [137]

**Direction 2.** Consider that man's end being in the life to come, and God being the righteous and merciful Governor of man in order to that end, it must needs be that God will give him sufficient means to know his will in order to that end; and that the clearest, fullest means must needs demonstrate most of the government and mercy of God.

**Direction 3.** Consider what full and sad experience the world has of its depravity and great corruption; and that the natural tendency of reason is to those high and excellent things, which corruption and brutishness almost extinguish or cast out with the most; and that the success of the lower faculties against right reason is so lamentable and universal, to the confusion of the world, that it is enough to tell us that this is not the state that God first made us in, and that certainly sin has sullied and disordered his work. The wickedness of the world is a great confirmation of the Scripture.

**Direction 4.** Consider how exactly the doctrine of the gospel, and covenant of grace, are suited to the fallen state of man; even as the law of works was suited to his state of innocency: so that the gospel may be called the law of fallen nature, as suited to it, though not as revealed by it; as the other was the law of entire nature.

**Direction 5.** Compare the many prophecies of Christ, with the fulfilling of them in his person. As that of Moses recited by Stephen, Acts 7:37; and Isaiah 58; Daniel 9:24–26, etc. And consider that those Jews which are the Christians' bitterest enemies acknowledge and preserve those prophecies, and all the Old Testament, which gives so full a testimony to the New.

**Direction 6.** Consider what an admirable suitableness there is in the doctrine of Christ, to the taste of a serious, heavenly mind: and how all that is spiritual and truly good in us closes with it and embraces it from a certain fittingness of natures, as the eye does with the light, and the stomach with its proper food. Every good man in reading the holy Scripture feels something (even all that is good) within him bear witness to it. And only our worse part is quarreling with it, and rebels against it.

**Direction 7.** Consider how all the first churches were planted by the success of all those miracles mentioned in the Scripture. And that the apostles and thousands of others saw the miracles of Christ: and the churches saw the miracles of the apostles, and heard them speak in languages unlearned; and had the same extraordinary gifts communicated to themselves. And these being openly and frequently shown, convinced unbelievers; and were openly urged by the apostles to stop the mouths of opposers, and confirm believers; (Galatians 3:1–3;) who would all have scorned their arguments, and the faith which they supported, if all these had been fictions, of which they themselves were said to be eyewitnesses and agents. So that the very existence of the churches was a testimony to the matter of fact. And what testimony can be greater of God's interest and approval, than Christ's resurrection, and all these miracles.

**Direction 8.** Consider how no one of all the heretics or apostates ever contradicted the matters of fact, or has left the world any kind of refutation of them, which they did not lack malice, or encouragement, or opportunity to have done.

**Direction 9.** Consider how no one of all those thousands that asserted these miracles is ever mentioned in any history as repenting of it, either in their health, or at the hour of death: whereas it would have been so heinous a villainy to have cheated the world in so great a cause, that some consciences of dying men, especially of men that placed all their hopes in the life to come, must needs have repented of it.

**Direction 10.** Consider that the witnesses of all these miracles, and all the churches that believed them, were taught by their own doctrine and experience, to forsake all that they had in the world, and to be reproached, hated, and persecuted by all men, and to be as lambs among wolves, in expectation of death; and all this for the hope of that blessedness promised them by a crucified, risen Christ. So that no worldly end could move them to deceive, or willingly to be deceived.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[137] I must profess that the nature and wonderful difference of the godly and ungodly, and their conduct in the world, are perpetual, visible evidences in my eyes, of the truth of the holy Scriptures.

1. That there should be so universal and implacable a hatred against the godly in the common sort of unrenewed men, in all ages and nations of the earth, when these men deserve so well of them, and do them no wrong; is a visible proof of Adam's fall, and the need of a Savior and a Sanctifier.

2. That all those who are seriously Christians should be so far renewed, and recovered from the common corruption, as their heavenly minds and lives, and their wonderful difference from other men shows, this is a visible proof that Christianity is of God.

3. That God so plainly shows a particular special providence in the converting and confirming souls, by distinguishing grace, and work on the soul, as the sanctified feel, shows that indeed the work is his.

4. That God so plainly grants many of his servants' prayers, by special providences, proves his owning them and his promises.

5. That God allows his servants in all times and places ordinarily to suffer so much for his love and service, from the world and flesh, shows that there is a judgment, and rewards and punishments hereafter. Or else our highest duty would be our greatest loss; and then how should his government of men be just?

6. That the renewed nature (which makes men better, and therefore is of God) wholly looks at the life to come, and leads us to it, and lives upon it, this shows that such a life there is; or else this would be delusory and empty, and goodness itself would be a deceit.

7. When it is undeniable that *de facto esse*, the world is not governed without the hopes and fears of another life; almost all nations among the heathens believing it, (and showing, by their very worshiping their dead heroes as gods, that they believed that their souls did live,) and even the wicked generally being restrained by those hopes and fears in themselves. And also that, *de posse*, it is not possible the world should be governed agreeably to man's rational nature, without the hopes and fears of another life; but men would be worse than beasts, and all villainies would be the allowed practice of the world. (As every man may feel in himself what he would be likely to be and do, if he had no such restraint.) And there being no doctrine or life comparable to Christianity, in their tendency to the life to come. All these are visible standing evidences, assisted so much by common sense and reason, and still apparent to all, that they leave unbelief without excuse; and are ever at hand to help our faith, and resist temptations to unbelief.

8. And if the world had not a beginning according to the Scriptures, 1. We should have found monuments of antiquity above six thousand years old. 2. Arts and sciences would have come to more perfection, and printing, guns, etc. not have been of so late invention. 3. And so much of America and other parts of the world would not have been yet uninhabited, unplanted, or undiscovered.

Of atheism I have spoken before in the Introduction; and nature so clearly reveals a God, that I take it as almost needless to say much of it to sober men.

## Translator's notes

- **Section heading:** "Directions against Unbelief" is Baxter's own heading for this section of Chapter 4. Our range begins at that heading and ends after Direction 10; the next file continues the Directions.
- **Baxter's own footnotes [134]-[136]** (source lines 21335-21350) sit just before this range, apparently belonging to earlier text, and are not carried here. Only footnote [137] has its marker inside this range.
- **Margin note typo:** the source's second margin note reads "that be believeth"; we read "that he believes." The first margin note ("Whether not to believe that my sins are pardoned, be indeed unbelief?") has its verb modernized to "is."
- **The creed (first paragraph):** Baxter's "I believe the remission of sins" is his wording of the article of the Apostles' Creed. It is not Scripture, so no NET text is used; we left his wording ("remission").
- **Unmarked Scripture quotation:** "He who truly believes is justified, and shall be saved" has no reference in Baxter's text. It looks like his own summary of the gospel promise (compare Mark 16:16; Acts 13:39), but we have not confirmed which verse he meant, so we quote nothing from the NET here. Marked as not in Baxter's text.
- **Scripture references (Direction 5, 7):** Baxter gives references only and does not quote them, so there is no NET wording to flag. We fetched the NET to check them. Acts 7:37 does contain Moses' words about a prophet like himself (the NET: "God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among your brothers"). Galatians 3:1-3 does speak of the Galatians receiving the Spirit, which fits his point about the gifts they had received. **Isaiah 58** (Baxter's "Isa. lviii") is a call to true fasting and does not read as a prophecy of Christ; he may have meant Isaiah 53, but we are not sure, so we have kept his reference. Daniel 9:24-26 is kept as printed.
- **Technical terms kept:** *in esse cognoscibili* and *in esse cognito* are scholastic phrases left as printed, with a draft English translation: (English: "in knowable being" and "in known being," that is, faith as the thing that can be known, and faith as actually known; rendered loosely.) *de facto esse* and *de posse* (note [137], item 7) are left as printed: (English: "in actual fact" and "as to what is possible"; rendered loosely.) The oddity in "*de facto esse*" is Baxter's own.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): *debile* to "weaker"; *debility* to "weakness"; *collection* (an inference drawn) to "inference"; *aptitude to ascertain us* to "fitness to make us certain"; *conjunctly* to "jointly"; *progeny* to "offspring"; *sinfullest* to "most sinful"; *experimented truth* to "truth proved by experience"; *pravity* to "depravity"; *prevalency* to "success"; *lapsed* to "fallen"; *congruously / congruity* to "fittingly / fittingness"; *relish* to "taste"; *confutation* to "refutation"; *approbation* to "approval"; *infidelity* to "unbelief" (note [137]; in the title of his own book, *Treatise against Infidelity*, kept); *differencing grace* to "distinguishing grace"; *suffereth* to "allows"; *conversation* (note [137]) to "conduct"; *vain* (note [137], item 6) to "empty"; *wanted not* to "did not lack."
- **Kept as written:** *apprehension* (his sense: how a thing is grasped or understood by the mind); *premises*; *objective / subjective certainty*; *divines* (theologians); *elect*; *Sanctifier*; *heathens*; *brutishness*. **God's interest** (Direction 7): his sense is God's concern or backing.
- **"Saints' Rest, part 2" and "Treatise against Infidelity"** are Baxter's own earlier books, as he says; we have not checked the cross-references.
- **"Introduction"** (Direction 1 and note [137]): Baxter's reference back to the opening of the book; not checked here.
- **Direction 3, "brutishness":** Baxter means the animal-like condition that sin brings; left as written.
- **Note [137], item 8:** the claim about "six thousand years" and the lateness of printing, guns, and the settling of America are Baxter's own and reflect the chronology and knowledge of his day; kept as his, with no comment from us.
- **Polemic:** Direction 5's remark that the Jews are "the Christians' bitterest enemies" is Baxter's own and is kept exactly as he wrote it.
