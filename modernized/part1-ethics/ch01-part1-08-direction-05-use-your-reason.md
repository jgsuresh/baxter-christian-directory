# Chapter 1, Part 1: Direction 5

**Direction 5. If you would not be destitute of saving grace, let your reason be exercised about the matters of your salvation, in some proportion of frequent, sober, serious thoughts, as you are convinced the weight of the matter requires.**

*In the margin: 1 Corinthians 13:5; Psalm 4:4–7; 1 Corinthians 11:28.*

To have reason is common to all men, even the sleepy and distracted: to use reason is common to all that have their senses awake, and fit to serve their minds: to use reason in the greatest matters is proper to wise men, who know for what end God made them rational. [23] Thoughtless men are all ungodly men; for reason not used is as bad as no reason, and will prove much worse in the day of reckoning. The truth is, though sinners are exceedingly blind and mistaken about the things of God, yet all God's precepts are so reasonable, and tend so clearly to our joy and happiness, that if the devil did not win most souls by silencing reason, and laying it asleep, or drowning its voice with the noise and crowd of worldly business, hell would not have so many miserable inhabitants. I scarcely believe that God will condemn any sinner that ever lived in the world that had the use of reason; no, not the heathens that had but one talent, but he will be able to say to them, as in Luke 19:22, "I will judge you by your own words, you wicked slave! So you knew, did you," etc. To serve God and labor diligently for salvation, and prefer it to all worldly things, is so reasonable a thing that everyone that repents of the contrary course calls it from his heart an impious madness. Reason must necessarily be for God who made it. Reason must necessarily be for that which is its proper end and use. Sin, as it is in the understanding, is nothing but unreasonableness; a blindness and error; a loss and corruption of reason in the matters of God and our salvation. And grace, as in the understanding, does but cure this folly and distraction, and make us rational again; it is but the opening of our eyes, and making us wise in the greatest matters. It is not a more unmanly thing to love and plead for blindness, madness, and diseases, and to hate both sight, and health, and wits, than it is to love and plead for sin, and to hate and vilify a holy life.

Grant me but this one thing, that you will but soberly exercise your reason about these great, important questions; Where must I abide forever? What must I do to be saved? What was I created and redeemed for? and I shall hope that your own understanding, as faulty as it is, will work out something that will promote your good. Do but withdraw yourself one hour in a day from company and other business, and consider but as soberly and seriously your end and life as you know the nature and weight of the matter requires, and I am persuaded your own reason and conscience will call you to repentance, and set you, at least, in a far better way than you were in before. When you walk alone, or when you wake in the night, remember soberly that God is present, that time is hastening to an end, that judgment is at hand, where you must give account of all your hours, of your lusts, and passions, and desires; of all your thoughts, and words, and deeds; and that your endless joy or misery depends wholly and certainly on this little time. Think but soberly on such things as these, but one hour in a day or two, and try whether it will not at last restore you to sense and godliness; and folly and sin will vanish away before the force of considering reason, as the darkness vanishes before the light. I entreat you now as in the presence of God, and as you will answer for the denial of so reasonable a request at the day of judgment, that you will but resolve to try this course of a sober, serious consideration about your sin, your duty, your danger, your hope, your account, and your everlasting state: try it sometimes, especially on the Lord's days; and do but mark the result of all; and where it is that such sober consideration does point or lead you? whether it is not toward a diligent, holy, heavenly life? If you deny me this much, God and your conscience shall bear witness that you thought your salvation of little worth, and therefore may justly be denied it.

Would it not be strange that a man should be penitent and godly who never once thought of the matter with any seriousness in his life? Can so many and great diseases of the soul be cured before you have once soberly considered that you have them, and how great and dangerous they are, and by what remedies they must be cured? Can grace be obtained and exercised while you never so much as think of it? Can the main business of our lives be done without any serious thoughts; when we think it fit to bestow so many upon the trivial matters of this world? Do the world and the flesh deserve to be remembered all the day, and week, and year? and do not God and your salvation deserve to be thought on one hour in a day, or one day in a week? Judge of these things only as a man of reason. If you expect that God, who has given you reason to guide your will, and a will to command your actions, should yet carry you to heaven like a stone, or save you against or without your will, before you ever once soberly thought of it, you may have leisure in hell to lament the folly of such expectations.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced exactly as in the source. A draft English translation follows the Latin passage in parentheses; it was prepared for this project and is not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check it before relying on fine points. Baxter's English in the note is modernized.

[23] The word itself excites reason, and preachers are by reason to shame all sin as a thing unreasonable. And the lack of such excitation, by powerful preaching, and plain instruction, and the persons' own consideration, is a great cause of the world's undoing. For those preachers that lay all the blame on the people's stupidity or malice, I ask them to read a satisfactory answer in Acosta the Jesuit, book 4, chapters 2, 3, and 4. Few souls perish, comparatively, where all the means are used which should be used by their superiors for their salvation: if every parish had holy, skillful, hardworking pastors, who would publicly and privately do their part, great things might be expected in the world. But, says Acosta, Itaque præcipua causa ad ministros parum idoneos redit. Quæ namque est prædicatio nostra? quæ fiducia? signa certè non edimus: vitæ sanctitate non eminemus; beneficentia non invitamus; verbi ac spiritus efficacia non persuademus; lachrymis ac precibus à Deo non impetramus; imo ne magnopere quidem curamus. Quæ ergo nostra querela est? quæ tanta Indorum accusatio? lib. iv. p. 365. (And so the chief cause comes back to ministers who are not very suitable. For what is our preaching? What is our confidence? We certainly do not perform signs; we do not stand out by holiness of life; we do not draw people by kindness; we do not persuade by the power of word and Spirit; we do not win from God by tears and prayers; indeed we do not even care very much. What, then, is our complaint? What is so great an accusation against the Indians [the native peoples of the Americas]? Book 4, p. 365.) A frank confession of the Roman priesthood. And such priests can expect no better success. But having seen another sort of ministers, through God's mercy, I have seen a corresponding fruit of their efforts.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Margin references:** reproduced as Baxter printed them. We did not check whether they fit the passage.
- **Luke 19:22** (paragraph 1): Baxter quotes the King James ("Out of thine own mouth will I judge thee, thou wicked servant. Thou knewest"). The NET reads "I will judge you by your own words, you wicked slave! So you knew, did you." The NET reading puts the stress on the servant's own words, which is Baxter's point: even a person with one talent is judged by what they themselves knew. "One talent" refers to the parable of the minas or talents.
- **rational** (paragraph 1): Baxter's "reasonable" in "made them reasonable" and "make us reasonable again," meaning able to reason, not "sensible."
- **thoughtless:** Baxter's "inconsiderate." **faulty:** Baxter's "erroneous." **miserable:** Baxter's "sad." **wits:** Baxter's "wit," meaning sound mind. **restore you to sense:** Baxter's "recover thee to wit."
- **"It is not a more unmanly thing …"** (paragraph 1): Baxter's point is that loving sin is just as unmanly as loving blindness and disease. Left in his wording.
- **Lord's days** (paragraph 2): Sundays. Kept as Baxter's term.
- **"the Indians"** (note 23): in Acosta (José de Acosta, a Jesuit who worked in Peru), the native peoples of the Americas. Baxter uses Acosta's own self-criticism of the Catholic missionaries as a mirror for Protestant preachers.
