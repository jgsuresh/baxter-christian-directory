# Chapter 3: Grand Direction 11 (part 5 of 8)

*This section begins at Direction 8 inside Grand Direction 11 and ends partway through Direction 10; the next file continues it.*

**Direction 8. Strive for the truest and fullest conceptions of the goodness and excellencies of God, which are his lovableness; and abhor all misrepresentations of him as unlovely.**

That which is apprehended as unlovely cannot be loved; and that which is apprehended as evil is apprehended as unlovely. Therefore it is the grand design of Satan to hide God's goodness, and to misrepresent him as evil: not to deny him to be good in himself, for in that he has no hope to be believed; but to persuade men that he is not good to them, or to make them forget or overlook his goodness. Not to persuade them that God is evil in himself; but that he is evil to them, by restraining them from their beloved sins, and hating them as sinners, and resolving to damn them if they go on impenitently. This, which is part of the goodness of God, he makes them believe is evil, by engaging them in a way and interest which he knows that God is engaged against, and enticing them under the strokes of his justice. And he tempts believers themselves to poor, diminutive, unworthy thoughts of the goodness and mercifulness of God, and to continual apprehensions of his wrath and terrors. And if he can make them believe that God is their enemy, and think of him only as a consuming fire, how little are they likely to love him! If Christians knew how much of the devil's malice against God and them exercises itself in this, to make God appear to man unlovely, they would more carefully watch against such misrepresentations, and flee from them with greater hatred. [116] Not that we must first, by the advice of arrogant reason and self-love, as some do, draw a false description of goodness and lovableness in our minds, and make that the measure of our judgment of God, his nature, attributes, and decrees; nor take his goodness to be only his suitableness to our opinions, wills, and interest. But we must take out from the word and works of God that true description of his goodness which he has given of himself, and expunge out of our notions whatever is contrary to it. **Think of God's goodness in proportion with his other attributes.** O my soul, how unequally have you thought of God! You easily believe that his power is omnipotence, and that his knowledge is omniscience; but of his goodness, how narrow and poor are your conceivings! as if it were nothing next to his power and knowledge. How often have you been amazed in the consideration of his greatness, and how seldom affected with the apprehensions of his goodness! You please him who would have you believe and tremble as he does himself, and not him who would have you believe and love. How often have you allowed the malicious enemy to accuse God to you, and make you believe that he is a hater of man, and hateful to a man, or a hater of you, that he might make you hate him! How often have you allowed him to draw in your thoughts a false representation of your dearest Lord, and show him to you in that unlovely shape! How often have your conceptions dishonored and blasphemed his love and goodness, while you have seemed to magnify his knowledge and his power! Think of him now as love itself, as fuller of goodness than the sea of water, or the sun of light. Love freely and boldly, without the hindrances of suspicions and fears, where you are sure you can never love enough; and if all the love of men and angels were united in one flame, they could never love too much, or come near the proportion of the glorious goodness which they love! Throw yourself boldly into this ocean of delights. Though the narrowness of your own capacity confines you, yet, as there are no bounds in the object of your love, do not let false, unbelieving thoughts confine you. Oh that I were all eye, to see the glorious lovableness of my God! Oh that I were all love, that I might be filled with his goodness! Oh that all the passions of my soul were turned into this holy passion! Oh that all my fears, and cares, and sorrows were turned into love! and that all the thoughts that confusedly crowd in upon me and molest me were turned into this one incessant thought, of the infinite goodness of my God! Oh that all my tears and groanings, yes, and all my other mirth and pleasures, were turned into the melodious songs of love! and that the pulse, and voice, and operations of love were all the motion of my soul! Surely in heaven it will be so, though it is not to be expected here.

**Direction 9. The great means of promoting love to God is duly to behold him in his appearances to man, in the ways of nature, grace, and glory. First, therefore, learn to understand and make good use of his appearances in nature, and to see the Creator in all his works, and by the knowledge and love of them to be raised to the knowledge and love of him.**

Though sin has so disabled us from the due use of these appearances of God in nature, that grace must restore us before we can do it effectively and acceptably; yet objectively nature is still the same in substance, and affords us much help to the knowledge and love of God. He knows nothing of the world aright who does not know God in it and by it. Some observe that the greatest students of nature are not usually the most advanced in grace; and that philosophers and physicians are seldom great admirers of piety; but this is to judge the wise by the foolish, and to attribute the ignorance and impiety of some to others who abhor it. Doubtless he is no philosopher, but a fool, who does not see and admire the Creator in his works. Indeed if a man were to give himself wholly to learning the shape and form of letters, and to writing them elaborately, or cutting them in brass or stone, or printing them, and not to understand their meanings or use, it is no wonder if he is ignorant of the arts and sciences which those letters, well understood, would teach him; such a man may be called an engraver, a copyist, a printer, but not a scholar: and no better can the atheist be called a philosopher or learned man, who denies the most wise Almighty Author while he beholds his works, when the nature and name of God is so plainly engraved upon them all. It is a great part of a Christian's daily business to see and admire God in his works, and to use them as steps to ascend by to himself. Psalm 111:2-4, "The Lord's deeds are great, eagerly awaited by all who desire them. His work is majestic and glorious, and his faithfulness endures forever. He does amazing things that will be remembered; the Lord is merciful and compassionate." Psalm 143:5, "I meditate on all you have done; I reflect on your accomplishments." Psalm 77:12, "I will think about all you have done; I will reflect upon your deeds." Psalm 92:4, 6, "For you, O Lord, have made me happy by your work. I will sing for joy because of what you have done. ... The spiritually insensitive do not recognize this; the fool does not understand this." As the praising of God's works, so the observing of God in his works is much of the work of a holy soul. Psalm 145:3-7, 10, 17, "The Lord is great and certainly worthy of praise. No one can fathom his greatness. One generation will praise your deeds to another and tell about your mighty acts. I will focus on your honor and majestic splendor and your amazing deeds. They will proclaim the power of your awesome acts. I will declare your great deeds. They will talk about the fame of your great kindness and sing about your justice. ... All your works will give thanks to you, Lord. Your loyal followers will praise you. ... The Lord is just in all his actions and exhibits love in all he does." Romans 1:19-20, "because what can be known about God is plain to them, because God has made it plain to them. For since the creation of the world his invisible attributes—his eternal power and divine nature—have been clearly seen because they are understood through what has been made. So people are without excuse." If we live in the world as believers or rational creatures ought, we should as often as David repeat those words, Psalm 107: "Let them give thanks to the Lord for his loyal love and for the amazing things he has done for people. Let them present thank offerings, and loudly proclaim what he has done. Some traveled on the sea in ships and carried cargo over the vast waters. They witnessed the acts of the Lord, his amazing feats on the deep water" (verses 21-24). But this is a subject fitter for a volume (of physics theologically handled) than for so short a touch. What an excellent book is the visible world for the daily study of a holy soul! Light is not more visible to the eye in the sun than the goodness of God is in it and in all the creatures to the mind. If I do not love God, when all the world reveals his loveliness, and every creature tells me that he is good, what a blind and wicked heart have I! O wonderful wisdom, and goodness, and power which appears in everything we see! in every tree, and plant, and flower; in every bird, and beast, and fish; in every worm, and fly, and creeping thing; in every part of the body of man or beast, much more in the admirable arrangement of the whole; in the sun, and moon, and stars, and meteors; in the lightning and thunder, the air and winds, the rain and waters, the heat and cold, the fire and the earth, especially in the ordered frame of all, so far as we can see them set together; in the admirable order and co-operation of all things; in their times and seasons, and the wonderful usefulness of all for man. O how glorious is the power, and wisdom, and goodness of God in all the frame of nature! Every creature silently speaks his praise, declaring him to man, whose office is, as the world's high priest, to stand between them and the great Creator, and explicitly offer him the praise of all. Psalm 8:3-6, 9, "When I look up at the heavens, which your fingers made, and see the moon and the stars, which you set in place, of what importance is the human race, that you should notice them? Of what importance is mankind, that you should pay attention to them? You made them a little less than the heavenly beings. You crowned mankind with honor and majesty. You appoint them to rule over your creation; you have placed everything under their authority. ... O Lord, our Lord, how magnificent is your reputation throughout the earth!" "Let them give thanks to the Lord for his loyal love and for the amazing things he has done for people." "The Lord's faithfulness extends throughout the earth," Psalm 33:5-9. Read Psalm 65. Thus love God as appearing in the works of nature.

**Direction 10. Study to know God as he appears more clearly to sinners in his goodness in the works of grace; especially in his Son, his covenant, and his saints, and there to love him, in the admiration of his love.**

Here love has made itself an advantage of our sin and unworthiness, of our necessities and miseries, of the law and justice, and the flames of hell. The abounding of sin and misery has glorified abounding grace; that grace which fetches sons for God from among the willing vassals of the devil, which fetches children of light out of darkness, and living souls from among the dead, and heirs for heaven from the gates of hell; and brings us as from the gallows to the throne. 1. A believing view of the nature, undertaking, love, obedience, doctrine, example, sufferings, intercession, and kingdom of Jesus Christ must needs inflame the believer's heart with a corresponding degree of the love of God. To look on a Christ and not to love God is to have eyes and not to see, and to overlook him while we seem to look on him. He is the liveliest image of Infinite Goodness, and the messenger of the most unsearchable, astonishing love, and the purchaser of the most invaluable benefits that were ever revealed to the sons of men. Our greatest love must be kindled by the greatest revelations and communications of the love of God. And "No one has greater love than this—that one lays down his life for his friends" (John 15:13). That is, men have no dearer and clearer way to express their love to their friends; but that love is magnified indeed which will express itself as far for enemies. "But God demonstrates his own love for us, in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us. ... For if while we were enemies we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son, how much more, since we have been reconciled, will we be saved by his life?" (Romans 5:8, 10). Steep, then, that stiff and hardened heart in the blood of Christ, and it will melt: come near, with Thomas, and by the passage of his wounds get near to his heart, and it will change your unkind, ungrateful heart into the very nature of love. Christ is the best teacher of the lesson of love that the world ever had; who taught it not only by his words, but by his blood, by his life, and by his death: if you cannot learn it of him you can never learn it. Love is the greatest commander of love, and the most effective argument that can irresistibly constrain us to it: and none ever loved at the measure and rates that Christ has loved. To stand by such a fire is the way for a frozen heart to melt, and the coldest affections to grow warm. A lively faith still holding Christ, the mirror of infinite love and goodness, before our faces is the greatest lesson in the art of love.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source. The Latin translation below is a draft prepared for this project, not from a published edition; a Latinist should check it, and fine points should not be relied on until then.

[116] O orator, in tua oratione plus dilige Deum quam teipsum et alia: et si hoc facis, justus es et prudens, et de charitate et sanctitate habituatus: Qui habitus est amicus tuus in oratione--O Orator! quando orabis pro commissis, justitiam, Dei tecum teneas diligendo; non autem odiendo: quia si sic, misericordia Dei non posset esse tua amica, eo quia injustus esset; et tuus habitus esset crudelis et à spe et charitate prolongatus et tuum amare in odire esset perversum, de quo odire esset in æternum habituatus. Raim. Lullius, Arte Magna de Applic. cap. 114. p. 557, 558. (English, rendered loosely because the Latin is awkward: "O speaker, in your speech love God more than yourself and other things; and if you do this, you are just and prudent, and have become accustomed [habituated] to love [charitas] and holiness: which habit is your friend in speech.--O speaker! when you pray about your offenses, keep God's justice with you by loving it, not by hating it; for if you did so, the mercy of God could not be your friend, because it would be unjust; and your habit would be cruel and drawn far away from hope and love, and your loving would be perverted into hating, in which hating you would be habituated forever." Raymond Lull, *Ars Magna*, "On Application," chapter 114, pp. 557-558. The printed "odiendo" and "odire" are kept as in the source.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®, fetched from labs.bible.org; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). Baxter's references were converted to modern form.
- **Structure:** the range opens at Direction 8 and ends within Direction 10, in the middle of its first numbered point ("1."); the numbered point "2." and the rest follow in the next file. The inner run-in heading "Think of God's goodness in proportion with his other attributes" (Direction 8) is set in bold, as Baxter ran it into the text with a dash.
- **Psalm 111:2-4:** NET "eagerly awaited by all who desire them" differs from the King James "sought out of all them that have pleasure therein," which Baxter echoes by speaking of those who study God's works; the NET does not clearly say the works are "sought out" or studied. Also "honourable" becomes "majestic," and "righteousness" becomes "faithfulness." Baxter leans on the studying of God's works, so the NET gives less support to that point here.
- **Psalm 92:6:** King James "A brutish man"; NET "The spiritually insensitive." Same sense for Baxter's use.
- **Psalm 145:3-7, 10, 17:** Baxter's quotation skips verses 8-9 and 11-16 without marking it; we mark the gaps with "...". Where the King James has "his goodness" (verse 7) the NET has "great kindness"; at verse 17 the King James "righteous in all his ways, and holy in all his works" becomes "just in all his actions and exhibits love in all he does." Verse 10, "thy saints shall bless thee" becomes "Your loyal followers will praise you."
- **Romans 1:19-20:** King James "Godhead" becomes NET "divine nature"; "manifest" becomes "plain." The sense Baxter relies on (God known through creation) is unchanged.
- **Psalm 107:21-24:** Baxter's text is the King James: "O that men would praise the Lord for his goodness ... sacrifice the sacrifices of thanksgiving." The NET has "give thanks to the Lord for his loyal love" and "thank offerings." The word "goodness," which suits Baxter's theme, is not in the NET's wording; "loyal love" is its replacement. His second, shorter quotation ("Oh that men would praise the Lord for his goodness, and declare his wondrous works to the children of men!") has no reference in the source; it matches the refrain repeated in Psalm 107 (verses 8, 15, 21, 31). We give the NET refrain, which is the same in each of those verses; this reference is ours, not Baxter's. The opening of the first quotation ("And let them sacrifice ... and declare his works with rejoicing") is carried by the NET's "present thank offerings, and loudly proclaim what he has done."
- **Psalm 8:3-6, 9:** King James "lower than the angels" becomes NET "less than the heavenly beings"; "the son of man" becomes "mankind"; "visitest" becomes "pay attention to." Verses 7-8 are skipped by Baxter; marked "...". The NET's verse 6 ("you appoint them to rule over your creation") begins in lower case in the printed NET text; we capitalized "You."
- **Psalm 33:5-9:** Baxter's quoted words, "The earth is full of the goodness of the Lord," are the King James of verse 5. The NET reads "the Lord's faithfulness extends throughout the earth." This changes the sense Baxter leans on (the earth displays God's goodness): the NET says "faithfulness," not "goodness." Baxter's reference runs on to verse 9, but his quoted words are only from verse 5.
- **John 15:13:** King James "lay down his life for his friends" and NET "lays down his life for his friends" match in sense.
- **Romans 5:8, 10:** King James "commendeth his love" becomes NET "demonstrates his own love"; "saved by his life" is unchanged in sense. The "..." marks the omitted verse 9, which Baxter joins without a mark. Baxter's opening "And if" is in the NET "For if."
- **Psalm 143:5; Psalm 77:12:** NET wording differs from the King James ("muse on the work of thy hands"; "talk of thy doings") but not in a way that affects Baxter's point.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "believe and tremble" (James 2:19, said of devils); "a consuming fire" (Hebrews 12:29; also Deuteronomy 4:24); "children of light out of darkness" (compare 1 Thessalonians 5:5; 1 Peter 2:9); "come near, with Thomas" (John 20:24-29). We have not confirmed each of these allusions against the text; they are suggestions, not Baxter's references.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): amiable/amiableness to lovable/lovableness; labour to strive; conceits to notions; fly (from) to flee; suffered (allowed); improve (make good use of; in Baxter, "improve" means turn to good account); proficients (advanced); curiously (elaborately); scrivener (copyist, a professional writer of documents by hand); composure (arrangement); stops (hindrances); insuperably (irresistibly); aggravated (magnified; Baxter means heightened, not made worse); glass (mirror); answerable (corresponding); congealed (frozen); converse (live); expressly (explicitly).
- **Kept as Baxter's terms:** *apprehended / apprehensions* (grasped in the mind; fears); *conceptions* (ideas); *affections*; *vassals* (bond-servants); *meteors* (anything seen in the sky, such as comets, lightning, and weather); *physics theologically handled* (natural philosophy treated in the light of faith).
- **Direction 8, "engaging them in a way and interest":** left as written. Baxter's sense is that Satan draws people into a course of life and a set of aims that God opposes.
- **"Godhead" and "Thou gratifiest him that would have thee believe and tremble as he doth himself":** the "him" is the devil.
- **Misprint:** the source reads "must he kindled" in Direction 10; we read "must be kindled."
- **The "1." in Direction 10:** Baxter numbers this point but the next numbered point falls outside this range.
- **Raymond Lull (note 116):** Baxter gives "Raim. Lullius, Arte Magna." We have not checked the chapter and page numbers against an edition. The Latin is loose and its "habit" language is technical (a settled disposition); the translation above is a rendering only. Baxter does not explain why he cites the passage; it appears at the point where he warns against thinking of God's justice in a hateful way.
- **Cross-reference not checked:** Baxter's "Read Psalm 65" is left as his reference only.
