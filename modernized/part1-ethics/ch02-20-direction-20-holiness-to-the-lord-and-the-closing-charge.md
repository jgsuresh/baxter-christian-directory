# Chapter 2: Direction 20

**Direction 20. See that your religion be purely divine, and animated all by God, as the beginning, the way, and the end; and that first upon your soul, and then upon all that you have or do, there be written "HOLINESS TO THE LORD;" and that you not corrupt all with an excessive hypocritical regard for man.**

To be holy is to be divine, or devoted to God, and set apart for him, and his will, and use; and that our hearts and lives not be common and unclean. [77] To be godly is to live to God, as those that from their hearts believe that he is God indeed, and that "he rewards those who seek him," that he is "our God all-sufficient, our shield and exceeding great reward" (Hebrews 11:6; Genesis 15:1; 17:1); and that "from him and through him and to him are all things," that all may give the glory forever to him (Romans 11:36). As God is infinitely above all creatures, so living upon God, and to God, must needs lift us above the highest physical life; and therefore religion is transcendently above all sciences or arts. So much of God as is in you and upon you, so much more excellent are you than the highest worldly perfection can make you. God should be the First, and Last, and All in the mind, and mouth, and life of a believer. God must be the principal matter of your religion. The understanding and will must be exercised upon him. When you awake you should be still with him (Psalm 139:18). Your meditations on him should be sweet, and you should be glad in the Lord (Psalm 104:34). Yet creatures, under him, may be the frequent, less principal matter of your religion; but still as referred to him. God must be the author of your religion: God must institute it, if you expect him to accept it and reward it. God must be the rule of your religion, as revealing his will concerning it in his word. God must be the ultimate end of your religion; it must be intended to please and glorify him. God must be the continual motive and reason of your religion, and of all you do: you must be able truly to fetch your reason from heaven, and to say, I do it because it is his will; I do it to please, and glorify, and enjoy him. God must be taken as the Sovereign Judge of your religion, and of you, and of all you do; and you must wholly look to his justification and approval, and avoid whatever he condemns. Can you take God for your Owner, your Sovereign, your Savior, your sufficient Protector, your Portion, your All? If not, you cannot be godly, nor be saved: if his authority does not have more power upon you than the authority of the greatest on earth, you are godless hypocrites, and not truly religious, whatever you claim. If "holiness to the Lord" is written upon you, and all that is yours, you are devoted to him as his very own. If your names are set upon your sheep, or silver, or clothes, you will say, if another should take them, They are mine; do you not see my mark upon them? Slavery to the flesh, the world, and the devil is the mark that is written upon the ungodly (upon the foreheads of the profane, and upon the hearts of hypocrites and all); and Satan, the world, and the flesh have their service. If you are consecrated to God, and bear his name and mark upon you, tell everyone who would lay claim to you that you are his, and resolved to live to him, to love him, to trust him, and to stand or fall to him alone. Let God be the very life, and sense, and end of all you do.

When once man has too much of your regard and notice, so that you value too much his favor and esteem, or look too much to him in your profession and practice; when man's approval comforts you too much, and man's displeasure or disapproval troubles you too much; when your fear, and love, and care, and obedience are too much taken up with man; you so far withdraw yourselves from God, and are becoming the servants of men, and friends of the world, and turning back to bondage, and forsaking our Rock and Portion, and your excellence; the soul of religion is departing from you, and it is dying and returning to the dust. And if once man gets the pre-eminence over God, and is preferred and set above him in your hearts or lives, and feared, trusted, and obeyed before him, you are then dead to God, and alive to the world; and as men are taken for your gods, you must settle for such a salvation as they can give you. If your alms and prayer are done to be seen by men, and to win their good thoughts and words; if you get them, make your best of them; "for truly," your Judge has said to you, "you have your reward" (Matthew 6:1-3).

Not that man is absolutely to be despised or disregarded. [78] No; under God, your superiors must be obeyed; you must do wrong to none, and do good to all, as far as you are able; you must avoid offense, and give a good example, and, under God, have so much regard to men as to become all things to all men for their salvation. But if once you set them above their rank, and turn yourselves to an excessive dependence on them, and make too great a matter of their opinion or words concerning you, you are losing your godliness or divine disposition, and turning it into man-pleasing and hypocrisy. When man stands in competition with God for your first and chief regard, or in opposition to him, or as a sharer in co-ordination with him, and not purely in subordination to him, he is to be numbered with things to be forsaken. Even good men, whom you must love and honor, and whose communion and help you must highly value, yet may be made the object of your sin, and may become your snare. Your honoring of them, or love for them, must not entice you to desire excessively to be honored by them, nor cause you to set too much by their approval. If you do, you will find that while you are too much looking to man, you are losing God, and corrupting your religion at the very heart. And you may fall among those who, however holy, may have great mistakes in matters of religion, tending to much sin, and may be somewhat censorious against those who are not of their mind; and so the keeping of their esteem, and the avoiding of their censures, may become one of the greatest temptations of your lives. And you will find that man-pleasing is a very difficult and yet unprofitable task. Love Christ as he appears in any of his servants, and be followers of them as they are followers of Christ, and regard their approval as it agrees with Christ's: but O see that you are able to live upon the favor of God alone, and to be quieted in his acceptance, though man despises you; and to be pleased so far as God is pleased, though man is displeased with you; and to rejoice in his justification, though men condemn you with the most hateful slanders and the greatest infamy, and cast out your names as evildoers. See that God be taken as enough for you, or else you do not take him as your God; even as enough without man, and enough against man; that you may be able to say, "If God is for us, who can be against us? ... Who is the one who will condemn? ... It is God who justifies" (Romans 8:31, 33, 34). "Or am I trying to please people? If I were still trying to please people, I would not be a slave of Christ!" (Galatians 1:10). Jeremiah 17:5-8, "The Lord says, 'I will put a curse on people who trust in mere human beings, who depend on mere flesh and blood for their strength, and whose hearts have turned away from the Lord. ... They will be like a shrub in the arid rift valley. They will not experience good things even when they happen. [79] ... My blessing is on those people who trust in me, who put their confidence in me. They will be like a tree planted near a stream whose roots spread out toward the water. It has nothing to fear when the heat comes. Its leaves are always green. It has no need to be concerned in a year of drought. It does not stop bearing fruit.'" Isaiah 2:22, "Stop trusting in human beings, whose life's breath is in their nostrils. For why should they be given special consideration?"

Having given you these directions, I must tell you in the conclusion that they are like food, which will not nourish you by standing on your table, or like medicine, which will not cure you by standing in the box: they must be taken and digested, or you will find none of the benefit. It is not the reading of them that will serve the turn to so great use, as the safe progress and confirmation of beginners or novices in religion: it will require humility to perceive the need of them; and labor to learn, digest, and practice them. Those slothful souls who will refuse the labor must bear the sad effects of their negligence: there is not one of all these directions, as to the matter of them, which can be spared. Study them, understand them, and remember them, as things that must be done. If either a senselessness of your need, or a notion that the Spirit must do it without so much labor and diligence of your own, should prevail with you to put off all these with a mere approval, the result may be sadder than you can yet foresee. Though I suppose you to have some beginnings of grace, I must tell you that it will be comparatively a sad kind of life to be erroneous, and scandalous, and troublesome to the church, or full of doubts, and fears, and passions, and to be burdensome to others and yourselves! Yes, it is reasonable that you be very suspicious of your sincerity, if you do not desire to increase in grace, and are not willing to use the means which are necessary to your increase. He is not sincere who does not desire to be perfect; and he does not desire sincerely who is not willing to take the labor and cost which are necessary to obtaining the thing desired. I beg you, therefore, as you love the happiness of prudent, strong, and comfortable Christians, and would escape the misery of those grievous diseases which would turn your lives into languishing, uselessness, and pain; that you seriously study these directions, and get them into your minds, and memories, and hearts; and let the faithful practice of them be your greatest care, and the constant employment of your lives.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source. A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[77] De bonis et malis ita disserebat Plato: Finem esse Deo similem fieri: Virtutem sufficere quidem ad bene beateque vivendum; cæterum instrumentis indigere, corporis bonis, robore, sanitate, integritate sensuum, &c. Exterioribus etiam, opibus, generis claritate, gloria, &c. Ea et si non affluerint, nihilominus tamen beatum fore sapientem.--Arbitratur et Deos humana cernere atque curare: et demones esse--Porro in dialogis justitiam divinam legem arbitratus est, ut ad juste agendum potentius persuaderet, nè post mortem pœnas improbi luerent. Laert. in Plat. (English: Plato argued about goods and evils thus: that the end is to become like God; that virtue is indeed sufficient for living well and happily, but needs instruments, namely bodily goods, strength, health, soundness of the senses, etc., and also external goods, wealth, nobility of birth, glory, etc.; and that even if these are not abundant, the wise man will nonetheless be happy.--He also holds that the gods see and care for human affairs, and that there are spirits [*demones*].--Moreover, in the dialogues he judged divine justice to be a law, in order to persuade more strongly to just action, so that the wicked would not pay penalties after death. Laertius, in his *Life of Plato*.)

[78] Alte spectare si voles, atque hanc sedem, et æternam domum contueri, neque sermonibus vulgi dederis te, nec in præviis humanis spem posueris rerum tuarum: suis te illecebris oportet ipsa virtus trahat ad verum decus. Cicero somn. Scip. Cœlestia semper spectato: illa humana contemnito. Id. Ibid. (English: If you wish to look on high, and to behold this abode and eternal home, do not give yourself up to the talk of the crowd, nor place the hope of your affairs in human [rewards; the source prints "præviis," probably a misprint for "præmiis"]: virtue itself must draw you by its own attractions to true honor. Cicero, *Dream of Scipio*. Always look to heavenly things; despise those human things. The same, in the same place.)

[79] Nihil tam firmum cui periculum non sit; etiam ab invalido. (English: Nothing is so firm that it is not in danger, even from a weak one.) The source gives no author for this line.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). All NET text was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Hebrews 11:6:** Baxter has "the rewarder of them that diligently seek him" (King James). The NET has "he rewards those who seek him," with no "diligently." Baxter's own argument in this Direction is about whole-hearted devotion, so he loses a word he would have liked; the point still stands from the rest of the verse.
- **Genesis 15:1; 17:1 ("our God all-sufficient, our shield and exceeding great reward"):** this is Baxter's own blend, not a quotation of one verse, so we keep his words. The NET has Genesis 15:1 "I am your shield and the one who will reward you in great abundance" and Genesis 17:1 "I am the Sovereign God." The NET's "Sovereign God" at 17:1 does not carry "all-sufficient" (we have not checked how the King James words that verse, and do not claim where Baxter's "all-sufficient" comes from), and Baxter leans on that idea when he later says God must be "enough" for you. His quotation marks are kept.
- **Romans 11:36:** NET "from him and through him and to him are all things"; Baxter's "of him" is the King James. No change of sense.
- **Psalm 139:18 (corrected reference):** Baxter prints "Psal. cxxxix. 8." for "When you awake you should be still with him." The words he uses are those of the King James at Psalm 139:18 ("when I awake, I am still with thee"), so we believe the printed "8" is a misprint and give 139:18. This is his paraphrase, so we keep his wording. The NET at 139:18 reads: "If I tried to count them, they would outnumber the grains of sand. Even if I finished counting them, I would still have to contend with you." The NET does not have the thought "when I awake I am still with you" that Baxter uses; his point rests on the King James reading. Psalm 139:8 in the NET is "If I were to ascend to heaven, you would be there."
- **Psalm 104:34:** Baxter's "Your meditations on him should be sweet, and you should be glad in the Lord" is his paraphrase; we keep it. The NET reads "May my thoughts be pleasing to him. I will rejoice in the Lord." The King James has "My meditation of him shall be sweet" (sweet to the one meditating); the NET makes the thoughts pleasing to God. Baxter's sense (sweetness of meditating on God) follows the older reading.
- **Matthew 6:1-3:** Baxter's "for verily ... you have your reward" is his own adaptation, with the Judge speaking to "you." The NET has "they have their reward!" (6:2; also 6:5) and "I tell you the truth" in 6:2. Since he changes the person, we keep his wording, modernized ("verily" to "truly").
- **Romans 8:31, 33, 34:** Baxter quotes the clauses in a different order than the verses: he puts "Who is he that condemneth?" before "It is God that justifieth." In the NET, "It is God who justifies" (verse 33) answers "Who will bring any charge against God's elect?", and "Who is the one who will condemn?" (verse 34) is answered by "Christ is the one who died..." Baxter's reading lets "God justifies" answer the question about condemnation. We used the NET wording of each clause, in his order, with "..." for what he skips.
- **Galatians 1:10:** NET "slave of Christ" where Baxter (King James) has "servant of Christ." "Slave" is the stronger word; Baxter's contrast with being a servant of men is not harmed. The NET's first clause ("Am I now trying to gain the approval of people, or of God?") is not in Baxter's quotation and is left out here.
- **Jeremiah 17:5-8:** Baxter's quotation is King James with a skip (his "--"). The NET differs in ways that matter to his argument. NET "mere flesh and blood" replaces the King James "maketh flesh his arm," a Hebrew idiom of trusting in human strength. NET verse 7 is spoken in the first person ("My blessing is on those people who trust in me"), where the King James has "Blessed is the man that trusteth in the Lord, and whose hope the Lord is." NET "shrub in the arid rift valley" replaces "heath in the desert." Baxter's "shall not see when good cometh" is NET "will not experience good things even when they happen." His closing "shall not be careful in the year of drought" is NET "no need to be concerned." The footnote marker [79] stands in the source after "when good cometh," and is placed after the matching words here. The fetched NET text of these verses carries no textual-variant brackets.
- **Isaiah 2:22:** NET "For why should they be given special consideration?" for Baxter's "wherein is he to be accounted of?" The thought is the same: man is of little account.
- **"HOLINESS TO THE LORD":** Baxter does not give a reference. It is the inscription on the gold plate on the high priest's turban (Exodus 28:36; NET: "Holiness to the Lord"). Not in Baxter's text; added here only as a note. His idea of writing it upon the soul and on all one has is his own use of the image.
- **Unmarked allusions (kept in Baxter's wording):** "First, and Last" (Revelation 1:17; 22:13); "become all things to all men" (1 Corinthians 9:22); "followers of them as they are followers of Christ" (1 Corinthians 11:1); "Rock and Portion" (Psalms such as 73:26; Deuteronomy 32); "friends of the world" (James 4:4); "turning back to bondage" (Galatians 4:9); "do good to all" (Galatians 6:10); "as far as ... lieth in you" (Romans 12:18); "to be seen of men" (Matthew 6:1); "peculiar" people (Titus 2:14; 1 Peter 2:9); "stand or fall" (Romans 14:4). These are our suggestions, not Baxter's; check before relying on them.
- **Word swaps (Baxter's word, then ours):** thou/thee/thy forms and -eth endings to modern; sensual to physical ("the highest sensual life" to "the highest physical life"); approbation to approval; dispraise to disapproval; inordinate to excessive; Saviour to Savior; pretend to claim; atheistical to godless; peculiar ones to "his very own" (an older sense: a people's own special possession); plate to silver (plate meant silver articles); conceit to notion; appropriated to set apart ("appropriated to him" to "set apart for him"); contemned to despised; retaining to keeping; proceeding to progress; "take up with" to settle for; "it is reason" to "it is reasonable" (Yea to Yes); "to be at the labour and cost" to "to take the labor and cost"; beseech to beg; unto to to; forever for "for ever"; offence to offense; physic to medicine; "serve the turn" kept; consequent to result; "or dost" dropped with the pronoun changes; unserviceableness to uselessness; christians to Christians; "advance us above" to "lift us above" and "advance you to" to "make you." Other changes are spelling, verb endings, and pronouns.
- **religion; sciences or arts; profession; passions; man-pleasing; censorious; scandalous; erroneous; comparatively:** kept. "Religion" is worship and practiced devotion; "sciences or arts" means branches of learning; "profession" is open claim of faith (as in "your profession and practice"); "passions" is strong feelings; "scandalous" means causing scandal to the church; "erroneous" means holding false doctrine.
- **"man" for people:** Baxter often means man in general ("when once man has too much of your regard"); kept, since the contrast is with God.
- **the spirit:** Baxter's lowercase "the spirit must do it" is printed "the Spirit" here, since the sense is the Holy Spirit.
- **thou / you:** the heading sentence uses "thy" and "thou"; the body uses "you." We use "you" throughout.
- **Plato / Diogenes Laertius (note 77)** and **Cicero (note 78):** Baxter cites "Laert. in Plat." and "Cicero somn. Scip." We take these to be Diogenes Laertius's life of Plato and Cicero's "Dream of Scipio," but we have not confirmed this against the works.
- **Concluding paragraph:** Baxter closes Chapter 2 with this charge to practice the Directions. Cross-references to other Directions have not been checked.
