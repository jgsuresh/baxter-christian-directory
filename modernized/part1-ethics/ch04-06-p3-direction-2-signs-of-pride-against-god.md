# Chapter 4: Direction 2 against Pride, the signs of pride against God (Signs 1-15)

**Direction 2. Observe the motions and discoveries of pride, towards God and man, that it may not, like the devil, prevail by keeping out of sight.** Because this is the chief part of my work, I shall here distinctly show you the signs and motions of it, in its several ways against God and man.

**Signs of the worst part of Pride against God.**

*Sign 1.* Self-idolizing pride causes men to glory in their supposed greatness, when the greatness of God should show them their contemptible vileness; and to magnify themselves, when they should magnify their Maker. It makes the strong man glory in his strength, and the rich man in his wealth, and the conqueror in his victories; [206] and princes, and rulers, and lords of the earth, in their dominions, and high offices, and power to do hurt or good to others; and say as Nebuchadnezzar, "Is this not the great Babylon that I have built for a royal residence by my own mighty strength and for my majestic honor?" (Daniel 4:30). How hard is it to be great and truly humble, and not to swell, and be lifted up in heart, as they rise in power! This God abhors as unsuitable to worms, and dust, and injurious to his honor, and will make them know that "power, and riches, and strength are his, and that the Most High rules in the kingdom of men, and gives it to whom he will" (Daniel 4:32).

*Sign 2.* Pride causes men to set up their supposed worth and goodness above or against the Lord: so that they make themselves their principal end, and practice that which some of late presume to teach, that it is not God that can or ought to be man's end, but himself alone: as if we were made only for ourselves, and not for our Creator. Pride makes men so considerable in their own esteem, that they live wholly to themselves, as if the world were to stand or fall with them: if they are well, all is well with them; if they are to die, they take it as if the world were at an end. They value God, but as they do their food, or health, or pleasure, even as a means to their own happiness; not as preferring him before themselves, nor making him the chief in their end. [207] They love themselves much better than God: and so far is man fallen from God to himself, that he feels himself disposed to this as strongly, as that he takes it to be his primitive nature, and therefore justifiable, and that it is impossible to go higher.

*In the margin: How God is man's end.*

God is to be man's end, though we can add nothing to him. The highest love supposes no want in him that we love, but an excellence of glory, wisdom, and goodness, to which all our faculties offer up themselves in admiration, love, and praise: not only for the delights of these, nor only that our persons may herein be happy; but chiefly that God may have his due, and his will may be pleased and fulfilled; and because his excellences deserve all this from men and angels. When we love a man of wonderful learning, and wisdom, and meekness, and charity, and holiness, and other goodness, it is not chiefly for ourselves that we love him, that we may receive something from him; for we feel his excellence command our love, though we were sure that we should never receive anything from him: nor is the delight of loving him our chief end, but a consequence, or the lesser part of our end; for we feel that we love him before we think of the delight. [208] The admiration, love, and praise of God our ultimate end, has no end beside their proper object; for it is itself the final act, even man's perfection. Lovableness magnetically attracts love: if you ask an angel why he loves God, he will say, because he is infinitely lovable: and though in such motions nature secretly aims at its own perfection and happiness, and lawfully interests itself in this final motion, yet the union being of such as are infinitely unequal, oh how little do the glorified spirits respect themselves in comparison of the blessed, glorious God! See what I said of this before, Chapter 3, Directions 11 and 15.

*Sign 3.* Pride makes men more desirous to be over-loved themselves, than that God be loved by themselves or others. They would gladly have the eyes and hearts of all men turned upon them, as if they were as the sun, to be admired and loved by all that see them.

*Sign 4.* Pride causes men to depend upon themselves, and contrive excessively for themselves, and trust in themselves; as if they lived by their own wit, and power, and industry, more than by the favor and providence of God. Isaiah 9:9; Obadiah 3.

*Sign 5.* Pride makes men return the thanks to themselves which is due to God for the mercies which they have received. God is thanked by them but as a formality; but they seriously ascribe it to their care, or skill, or industry, or power, Daniel 4:30; they sacrifice to their net, Habakkuk 1:16, and say, Our hand, our contrivance, our power, our good management has done all this. [209]

*Sign 6.* Pride sets up the wisdom of a foolish man against the infinite wisdom of God; it makes men presume to judge their Judge, and judge his laws, before they understand them; and to quarrel with all that they find unsuitable to their own notions; and say, How improbable is this or that! and how can these things be? He that cannot undo a pair of tarrying irons, or unriddle a riddle until it is taught him, which afterwards appears plain, will question the truth of the word of God about the most high, unsearchable mysteries. Proud men think they could mend God's word, and they could better have ordered matters in the world, and for the church, and for themselves, and for their friends, than the providence of God has done. [210]

*Sign 7.* Pride makes men set up their own love and mercy above the love and mercy of God. Augustine mentions a sort of heretics called Misericordes, merciful men; and Origen was led hereby into his errors. When they think of hell fire, and the number of the miserable, and the fewness of the saved, they consult with their ignorant compassion, and think that this is below the love and mercy which is in themselves, and that they would not thus treat an enemy of their own; and therefore they criticize the holy Scriptures, and pride inclines them strongly to unbelief; while they forget the narrowness and darkness of their souls, and how unfit they are to criticize God, and how many truths may be unseen by them, which would fully satisfy them if they knew them; and how quickly God will show them that which shall justify his word and all his works, and convince them of the folly and arrogance of their unbelief and criticisms.

*Sign 8.* Pride makes men claim to be more just than God; and to think that they could more justly govern the world; and to criticize God's threatenings, and the sufferings of the good, and the prosperity of the wicked, as things so unjust, that they thereby incline to atheism. So James and John would be more just than Christ, and call down fire on the rejecters of the gospel; and the prodigal's brother, Luke 15, resented his father's leniency.

*Sign 9.* Pride makes men slight the authority and commands of God, and despise his messengers, and choose to be ruled by their own notions, and lusts, and interest, Jeremiah 13:15, 17; 43:2, 3; when the humble tremble at his word, and readily obey it, Isaiah 57:15; Nehemiah 9:16, 29; Isaiah 9:9.

*Sign 10.* A proud man in power will expect that his will be obeyed before the will of God; and that the subjects of God displease their Master rather than him: he will think it a crime for a man to inquire first what God would have him do; or to plead conscience and the commands of the God of heaven, against the obeying of his unjust commands. If he offers you preferment, as Balak did Balaam, he expects you should be more taken with it, than with God's offer of eternal life: if he threatens you, as Nebuchadnezzar did the three witnesses, he expects that you should be more afraid of him than of God, who threatens your damnation; and is angry if you are not.

*Sign 11.* A proud man is more offended with one that would question his authority, or speak slightingly of his power, or displease his will, or cross his interest, than with one that sins against the authority, and will, and interest of God. He is much more zealous for himself and his own honor, than for God's; and grieved more for his own dishonor, and hates his own enemies more than God's; and can tread down the interest of God and souls, if it seems but necessary to his honor or revenge: he is much more pleased and delighted with his own applause, and honor, and greatness, than with the glory of God, or the fulfilling of his will.

*Sign 12.* Proud men would gladly steal from God himself the honor of many of his most excellent works. [211] If they are rulers, they are more desirous that the thanks for the order and peace of societies be given by the people to them, than to God. If they are preachers, they would gladly have more than their due, of the honor of men's conversion and edification: if they are pastors, they would encroach upon Christ's part of the government of his church. If they are bountiful to the poor, and do any good works, they would have more of the praise than belongs to a steward, or messenger that delivers the gifts of God. If they are physicians, they would have the real honor of the cure, and have God to be but a barren formality: like the atheistic physician, that reviled and beat his patient for thanking God that he was well, When, says he, it was I that cured you, and do you thank God for it?

*Sign 13.* A proud man will give more to his honor than to God: his estate is more at the command of his pride, than of God. He gives more in the view or knowledge of others, than he could persuade himself to do in secret. He is more bountiful in gifts that tend to keep up the credit of his liberality, than he is to truly indigent persons: it is not the good that is done, but the honor which he expects by it, which is his principal motive. He had rather be scant in works of greatest secret charity, than in apparel, and an attractive bearing, and the entertaining of friends, or anything that is for show, and for himself.

*Sign 14.* A proud man would have as great a dependence of others upon him as he can. He would have the estates, and lives, and welfare of all others at his will and power; that he might be much feared, and loved, and thanked, and that many may be indebted to him as the god or great benefactor of the world. He is not contented that good is done, and men's wants supplied, unless he has the doing of it, that so he may have the praise. If he saves his enemy, it is but to make him indebted to him, and be said to have given him his life. Gladly he would be taken to be as the sun to the world, which mankind cannot be without.

*Sign 15.* A proud man is very patient when men ascribe to him that which he knows to be above his due, though it is to the injury of God. He can easily forgive those that value and love him more than he deserves, though they sin in doing it. He is seldom offended with any for over-praising him; nor for reverencing or honoring him too much; nor for setting him too high, or for giving or ascribing too much power to him; nor for obeying him before God himself. He cares not how much love, and honor, and praises, and thanks he has; when an humble soul says, as Psalm 115:1, "Not to us, O Lord, not to us, but to your name bring honor:" and as the angel to John, who would have worshiped him, "Do not do this! I am a fellow servant with you..." (Revelation 22:9). They know God will not give "his glory to another" (Isaiah 42:8). "Everyone in his temple says, 'Majestic!'" (Psalm 29:9). But of themselves they say, "I am a worm, not a man" (Psalm 22:6). I am less "than the least of all your mercies" (Genesis 32:10); "less than the least of all the saints" (Ephesians 3:8); the chief of sinners, 1 Timothy 1:15: how unfit am I for so much love, and praise, and honor!

A draft English translation follows each Latin or Greek passage in parentheses; these translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[206] Jeremiah 9:23, 24; Psalm 49:6; 2 Chronicles 25:19.

[207] Ut lumen lunæ in præsentia solis non apparet, pari ratione esse secundum in præsentia primi; nec meritum nostrum præsente merito Christi. Paul. Scaliger. Thess. 73, 74. de Mundo Archetyp. Epist. 1. 14. (English, rendered loosely because the sentence is elliptical: As the light of the moon does not appear in the presence of the sun, so, by the same reasoning, the second [does not appear] in the presence of the first; nor does our merit [appear] in the presence of the merit of Christ. Paul. Scaliger, "Thess." 73, 74 (probably Theses; unconfirmed), "de Mundo Archetyp." (on the archetypal world), Epist. 1. 14.)

[208] Idem sonant, summe amari, et esse finem ultimum: ac proculdubio Deus summe amandus est. Unum vero finem Aristoteles declaravit esse, usum virtutis in vita sancta et integra. Hesych. Illust. in Aristot. (English: To be loved supremely and to be the ultimate end sound the same [mean the same]; and without doubt God is to be loved supremely. But Aristotle declared that there is one end, the exercise of virtue in a holy and upright life. Hesych. Illust., on Aristotle.)

[209] Laert. on Thales speaks of the oracle of Delphos awarding the tripod to the wisest; so it was sent to Thales, and from him to another, until it came to Solon, who sent it to the oracle; saying, None is wiser than God. So should we all send back to God the praise and glory of all that is ascribed to us.

[210] Laert. says that Pythagoras first called himself a philosopher. Nullum enim hominem, sed solum Deum esse sapientem asserit: antea σοφια dicta, quæ nunc philosophia: et qui hanc profitebantur σοφοι appellati. quicunque ad summum animis virtutem excreverunt, hos nunc honestiore vocabulo, authore Pythagora, philosophos appellamus, p. 7. (English: For he asserts that no man, but God alone, is wise; what is now called philosophy was formerly called *sophia* [Greek σοφια], and those who professed it were called *sophoi* [σοφοι]. Those who have grown in their minds to the highest virtue, these we now call by the more honorable name, on the authority of Pythagoras, philosophers. Page 7.)

[211] Quicquid boni egeris, in deos refer. Bias in Laert. (English: Whatever good you have done, refer it to the gods. Bias, in Laertius.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). Every quoted verse below was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Range note:** the range begins at the start of Direction 2 (Baxter's "Direct. II."), after Direction 1 in the previous file. The line "Signs of the worst part of Pride against God" is a heading Baxter prints centered; it is rendered as a bold line. Baxter's "Sign I., II., ..." are rendered "Sign 1., 2., ..." in italics. The range ends at Sign 15 and the signs continue in the next file.
- **Daniel 4:30:** NET wording ("royal residence," "by my own mighty strength," "majestic honor"). The King James has "the house of the kingdom, by the might of my power, for the honour of my majesty," which is Baxter's wording; the NET sense is the same (a royal residence built for his own glory). Baxter's "Is not this" became NET's "Is this not."
- **Daniel 4:32:** not a pure quotation. "Power, and riches, and strength are his" is not in Daniel 4:32 as the NET has it; the second half is from the verse. Baxter's own blend or paraphrase is kept in his quotation marks, modernized. The NET reads: "the Most High is ruler over human kingdoms and gives them to whomever he wishes." Baxter's "it" (the kingdom) is the King James singular; the NET has "them" (kingdoms).
- **Psalm 115:1:** NET "bring honor" where the King James (Baxter's source) has "give glory." The sense is the same (the praise belongs to God); the NET gives the reason after the clause ("for the sake of your loyal love and faithfulness"), which Baxter does not quote. Baxter's colon inside the quotation is kept.
- **Revelation 22:9:** Baxter gives no reference; the verse is the NET's. We place the reference in parentheses. Baxter's "See thou do it not, for I am thy fellow-servant" is the King James; the NET has "Do not do this! I am a fellow servant with you" and continues with more words, so the quotation ends with "..." The reference is not in Baxter's text (marked as ours). Baxter's "angel to John" is as he wrote it.
- **Isaiah 42:8:** Baxter's "his glory to another" is King James phrasing turned to the third person ("God will not give his glory to another"). NET reads "I will not share my glory with anyone else." Baxter's wording is kept in his quotation marks, modernized only in spelling and verb form.
- **Psalm 29:9:** NET changes the sense Baxter leans on. The King James has "in his temple doth every one speak of his glory"; the NET has "Everyone in his temple says, 'Majestic!'" Baxter's point (those in God's temple speak of God's glory, not their own) is still present, but the word "glory" is not in the NET's sentence. The NET wording is used.
- **Psalm 22:6:** NET "I am a worm, not a man" for "I am a worm, and no man."
- **Genesis 32:10:** Baxter's "less than the least of all thy mercies" is the King James rendering ("I am not worthy of the least of all the mercies"). The NET reads: "I am not worthy of all the faithful love you have shown your servant." The NET does not have "the least," so the wording Baxter leans on ("I am less than the least") is not in it. Baxter's wording is kept (with "your" for "thy") and flagged here.
- **Ephesians 3:8:** NET "less than the least of all the saints" (Baxter's "less than the least of all saints," his reference). The NET reads the phrase as Paul describing himself. **1 Timothy 1:15:** Baxter does not put these words in quotation marks. His "the chiefest of sinners" is the King James wording; the NET has "I am the worst of them." Kept as Baxter's, modernized ("the chief of sinners"), reference converted.
- **References that are only cited (no quoted words):** Isaiah 9:9; Obadiah 3 (Sign 4); Daniel 4:30; Habakkuk 1:16 (Sign 5); Jeremiah 13:15, 17; 43:2, 3; Isaiah 57:15; Nehemiah 9:16, 29; Isaiah 9:9 (Sign 9). In the NET, Isaiah 9:9, Jeremiah 13:15, Jeremiah 43:2, Nehemiah 9:16, 29, and Obadiah 3 speak of arrogance or presumption; Isaiah 57:15 speaks of God dwelling with the humble. Baxter's text lists them together after "the humble tremble at his word"; we have kept his order of references and have not checked which verses he assigns to which clause. Baxter wrote "Jer. xiii. 15, 17; xliii. 2, 3" as one reference; we split it as "Jeremiah 13:15, 17; 43:2, 3."
- **Unmarked allusions (kept in Baxter's wording):** James and John wanting to call down fire (Luke 9:54, as we believe); the prodigal's brother (Luke 15:28-30, which Baxter cites as "Luke xv."); Balak and Balaam (Numbers 22-24, probably); Nebuchadnezzar and "the three witnesses" (probably Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, Daniel 3); "to sacrifice to their net" (Habakkuk 1:16, which Baxter cites).
- **Word swaps (Baxter's word, then ours):** doth/hath/saith and -eth endings to plain forms; honour to honor; felicity to happiness; amiable/amiableness to lovable/lovableness; excellency to excellence; consequent to consequence; fain to gladly; contrive inordinately to contrive excessively; "in compliment" to "as a formality" and "barren compliment" to "barren formality" (a polite empty gesture); conceits to notions; censure to criticize; arrogancy to arrogance; pretend to claim; repined at to resented; lenity to leniency; diminutively to slightingly; dignities to high offices; warrantable to justifiable; chiefest to chief; atheistical to atheistic; beholden to indebted; "a comely port" to "an attractive bearing"; ostentation to show; husbandry to management; "in their end" is kept. **practise** (verb) to practice.
- **Words kept:** *lusts*, *interest* (one's own advantage or standing), *preferment* (promotion to office), *estate(s)* (wealth and property), *indigent*, *scant*, *self-idolizing*, *primitive nature* (original nature). **a pair of tarrying irons:** kept as Baxter wrote it; we have not confirmed what it was (probably a puzzle of linked metal pieces to be taken apart).
- **Sign 2:** Baxter's "some of late presume to teach" refers to unnamed writers; we have not identified them. "Chapter 3, Directions 11 and 15" is Baxter's own cross-reference ("chap. iii. direct. xi. and xv."), which we have not checked.
- **Sign 7:** *Misericordes* ("the merciful"), a Latin label Baxter gives to a sort of heretics he says Augustine mentions. We have not checked the claim about Augustine or Origen; it is Baxter's. **the number of the miserable** means the number of those in misery.
- **Sign 12:** Baxter's story of the physician who beat his patient is his; we have not traced it.
- **Note 206:** Psalm 49:6 and 2 Chronicles 25:19 are cited in a list of references only; Baxter gives no quoted words.
- **Note 207:** Baxter's "Paul. Scaliger. Thess. 73, 74. de Mundo Archetyp. Epist. 1. 14." is kept as printed. We have not identified the work; the author may be a Scaliger, but we have not confirmed which. The Latin is elliptical (no verb in the second clause), so the English is a loose reading.
- **Note 208:** "Hesych. Illust. in Aristot." is kept as printed; we take it to refer to a commentator on Aristotle and have not verified it. The Latin "Idem sonant" is rendered "mean the same" (literally "sound the same").
- **Notes 209-211:** "Laert." is Baxter's abbreviation, probably for Diogenes Laertius's *Lives of the Philosophers* (we have not checked the passages). "Thal." is Thales; "Delphos" is Baxter's spelling for Delphi; "Tripos" (Greek) is a three-legged vessel, rendered "tripod." In note 210 the Greek words are kept exactly as printed (σοφια, σοφοι) with transliterations in the English. The note-210 passage ends "p. 7." as printed. "Bias" (note 211) is probably Bias of Priene, one of the Seven Sages; this is not confirmed. In note 210 "Pythagoras" and his coinage of "philosopher" are Laertius's claim as Baxter reports it.
