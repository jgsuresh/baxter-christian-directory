# Chapter 8: Part 8, directions against sinful dreams

**Part 8.**

**Directions against sinful dreams.**

Dreams are neither good nor sinful simply in themselves, because they are not rational and voluntary, nor in our power; but they are often made sinful by some other voluntary act: they may be sinful by participation and by consequence. And the acts that make them sinful are either such as go before, or such as follow after.

1. The antecedent causes are any sinful act which disorders the body, or any sin which inclines the imagination and mind to them; or the omission of what was necessary to prevent them. 2. The causes which afterwards make them objectively sinful are the ill uses that men make of them; as when they take their dreams to be divine revelations, or trust in them, or are frightened by them as ominous, or as prophetic; and make them the ground of their actions, and lead themselves astray by the phantasms of their own brains.

**Direction 1. Avoid those bodily disorders as much as you can which cause sinful dreams, especially fullness of diet:**

a full stomach causes troublesome dreams, and lustful dreams; and has its ill effects by night and by day.

**Direction 2. Work at the cure of those sinful disorders of the mind which cause sinful dreams.**

The cure of a worldly mind is the best way to cure worldly, greedy dreams; and the cure of a lustful heart is the best way to cure lustful dreams; and so of the rest: cleanse the fountain, and the waters will be sweeter day and night.

**Direction 3. Do not allow your thoughts, or tongue, or actions to run sinfully upon that in the day which you would not dream sinfully of in the night. [472]**

Common experience tells us that our dreams are apt to follow our previous thoughts, and words, and deeds. If you think most frequently and affectionately of that which is good, you will dream of that which is good. If you think of lustful, filthy objects, or speak of them, or meddle with them, you will dream of them; and so of greedy and ambitious dreams, and they that make no conscience of sinning while awake are not likely much to scruple sinning in their sleep.

**Direction 4. Commend yourselves to God by prayer before you take your rest, and beg him to set a guard upon your imagination when you cannot guard it.**

Cast the care upon him, and flee to him for help by faith and prayer with a sense of your insufficiency.

**Direction 5. Let your last thoughts before your sleep always be holy, and yet quieting and comforting thoughts. [473]**

The dreams are apt to follow our last thoughts. If you go to sleep with worldliness or emptiness in your minds, you cannot expect to be wiser or better when you are asleep than when you are awake. But if you close your day's thoughts with God, and sleep finds them upon any holy subject, it is likely to use them as it finds them. Yet if it is distrustful, unbelieving, fearful thoughts which you grieve along with, your dreams may taste of the same disorder. Frightening and often sinful dreams do follow sinful doubts and fears. But if you sweeten your last thoughts with the love of Christ, and the remembrance of your former mercies, or the foresight of eternal joys, or can confidently cast them and yourselves upon some promise, it will tend to the quietness of your sleep, and to the wholesomeness of your dreams: and if you should die before morning, will it not be most desirable that your last thoughts are holy?

**Direction 6. When you have found any corruption appearing in your dreams, make use of them for the renewing of your repentance, and stirring up your efforts to mortify that corruption.**

A corruption may be perceived in dreams, 1. When such dreams as reveal it are frequent: 2. When they are earnest and violent: 3. When they are pleasing and delightful to your imaginations: not that any certain knowledge can be drawn from them, but some conjecture as added to other signs. As, if you should frequently, earnestly, and delightfully dream of promotions and honors, of the favors of great men, suspect ambition, and do the more to discover and mortify it. If it is of riches, and gain, and money, suspect a greedy mind. If it is of revenge or hurt to any man that you dislike, suspect some malice, and quickly mortify it: so if it is of lust, or feasting, or drinking, or empty recreations, amusements and games, do the like.

**Direction 7. Lay no greater stress upon your dreams than there is just cause.**

As, 1. When you have searched, and find no such sin powerful in you as your dreams seem to suggest, do not conclude that you have more than your waking evidence reveals. Do not prefer your sleeping signs before your waking signs and search. 2. When you are conscious that you indulge no corruption to occasion such a dream, do not suppose it to be faulty of itself, and do not lay the blame of your bodily temperament, or unknown causes, upon your soul, with too heavy and unjust a charge. 3. Abhor the presumptuous folly of those that are accustomed to predict by their dreams, and measure their expectations by them, and cast themselves into hopes or fears by them. Says Diogenes, "What folly is it to be careless of your waking thoughts and actions, and inquisitive about your dreams? A man's happiness or misery rests upon what he does when he is awake, and not upon what he suffers in his sleep."

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source. A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[472] Cogitationes sanctiores sequuntur somnia blandiora et delectabiliora. Greg. Moral. (Holier thoughts are followed by gentler and more delightful dreams. Gregory, *Moralia*.)

[473] Iturus in somnum aliquid tecum defert in memoria et cogitatione in quo placide obdormias, quod etiam somniare juvet: sic tibi nox ut dies illuminatur, et in deliciis tuis placide obdormies: in pace quiesces, facile evigilabis, et surgens promptus eris ad redeundum in id, unde non totus discessisti. (When you are going to sleep, carry something with you in memory and thought in which you may peacefully fall asleep, something it is even pleasant to dream of: so the night is lit for you like the day, and you will fall asleep peacefully in your delights: you will rest in peace, you will wake easily, and on rising you will be ready to return to that from which you had not wholly departed.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). No Scripture is quoted in this section, so no NET text is used.
- **"Part 8."** The source jumps from PART VI. to PART VIII.; there is no PART VII. We keep Baxter's (or his printer's) numbering.
- **by participation and by consequence** (first paragraph): Baxter's "by participation and consequently." Dreams are not sinful in themselves but share in (participate in) the sin of a voluntary act before or after them. "by" is added before "consequence" for modern grammar.
- **"1."** (second paragraph): the source prints "I." where the sequence (followed by "2.") shows a "1." is meant; we print 1.
- **disorders:** Baxter's "distempers" and "distempereth" (bodily or mental disorder, imbalance), here and in Directions 1, 2, and 5.
- **imagination:** Baxter's "fantasy" (the image-making faculty of the mind in the older psychology, not a daydream), in the second paragraph and Directions 4 and 6 ("fantasies," rendered "imaginations").
- **objectively sinful:** kept. Baxter means dreams become sinful as the object of later wrong use (taking them as revelations, being ruled by them), not in themselves.
- **lead themselves astray:** Baxter's "seduce themselves." **phantasms:** kept; mental images or illusions produced by the imagination. **frightened:** his "affrighted." **prophetic:** his "prophetical."
- **Work at the cure** (Direction 2): Baxter's "Endeavour the cure" (strive for the cure).
- **cleanse the fountain, and the waters will be sweeter** (Direction 2): possibly an echo of Elisha healing the spring at Jericho (2 Kings 2:19–22) or of James 3:11; Baxter gives no reference, and the image may simply be proverbial.
- **affectionately** (Direction 3): kept; for Baxter it means with the affections (feelings and desires) engaged, not merely fondly. **make no conscience of sinning while awake:** his "make no conscience to sin waking." **are not likely much to scruple:** his "are not like much to scruple" (are not likely to hesitate much over).
- **beg him:** Baxter's "beseech him." **Cast the care upon him** (Direction 4): Baxter wrote "Cast the cure upon him," where "cure" has the older sense of charge or care (as in "the cure of souls"); we render it "care." **flee to him:** his "fly to him." **with a sense of:** his "in the sense of."
- **always** (Direction 5): Baxter's "still" (constantly). **comforting:** his "consolatory." **go to sleep:** his "betake yourselves to sleep." **emptiness:** his "vanity" (empty, worthless things, not conceit). **close your day's thoughts:** his "shut up your day's thoughts." **grieve along with:** his "condole with" (dwell on in sorrow). **taste of:** his "savour of." **Frightening:** his "Frightful." **wholesomeness:** his "savouriness" (pleasantness, good relish).
- **stirring up your efforts:** Baxter's "exciting your endeavours" (Direction 6). **reveal:** his "discover" in "such dreams as discover it"; the later "discover and mortify it" keeps "discover" in the sense of find out. **drawn:** his "fetched." **promotions:** his "preferments." **dislike:** his "distaste." **amusements:** his "sports." **mortify:** kept (put to death, subdue a sin).
- **powerful** (Direction 7): Baxter's "prevailing." **suggest:** his "intimate." **are accustomed to predict:** his "use to prognosticate." **rests upon:** his "lieth upon." **suffers** is kept in its sense of "undergoes."
- **Diogenes** (Direction 7): Baxter does not say which Diogenes or where the saying comes from; we have not identified the source.
- **Gregory, *Moralia*** (note 472): probably Gregory the Great's *Moralia* on Job; we have not confirmed the passage.
- **Note 473:** the source gives no author for this Latin passage; we have not identified it.
