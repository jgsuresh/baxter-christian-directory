# Chapter 9: Title 4, Directions 2-3 on the aggravations and the mischief of idle talk

**Direction 2. Understand also the aggravations of idle words; which of them are the greatest sins, that they may be most carefully avoided.**

Though all idle words are sins, yet all are not equally sinful: the worst are such as these that follow.

1. When idle words are frequent, multiplied, and made their common talk and custom: which is the case of some men, but of abundance of loquacious women; whose natural disposition inclines them to it. One that has but little wit, and much self-conceitedness, and passion, will have a torrent of words for a drop of sense. [530] If they meet but with a person so patient and idle as to give them the hearing, they will sit a whole hour together with you, yes, many hours, to tell you first how the affairs go between them and their husbands, or children, or servants; and then talk of their cattle, house, or land; and then tell you of news, and enter into a long conversation about other men's matters, which they neither understand nor have anything to do with: and next they talk of the weather; and then of the market, what is cheap and what is dear; and then they tell you what this body said to them, and what the other body said; and then they tell you a story of the old times, and how the world is changed, and how much better the former times were than these: then they tell you what wrong such a one did them, and what he said of them, and how bad this or that man is, and what they said or did amiss; and what the report of the country is of such and such: then they tell you what clothes such a one wears, and how fine and showy such a one is, and who keeps a good house, and who is stingy and sparing: then they tell you what food was at such and such a table or feast; and if they are at a meal, they have something to say about every dish, and every sort of food or drink; especially news takes up much of their talk. [531] And it is well if in all this, the sermon of the preacher, or his prayer, or his life, is not brought in to fill up the empty places of the talk; and it may be the king and his council, and his laws, and his doings, shall be defiled by these parrots' irreverent prattling, as well as lower things and persons: so that, as Theophrastus says, he that would not fall into a fever, let him run from them in all the haste he can. I should rather think it would cast one into the scurvy, if weariness is so great a symptom of it as they say. He that has nothing to do in this world, nor anything to do for the world to come; and that has no use for his time, or wit, or tongue, or hands, but wakes as he sleeps, and lives as he must lie when he is dead; he that has neither master, work, nor wages, but thinks he is made to see leaves wag, or hear flies buzz; let him choose such a companion, and let him sit and hear such people chat. For my part, I can more easily endure to have them call me morose, or proud, or uncivil, or anything; nay, I had rather be digging, or plowing, or clearing gutters, than endure the tediousness of their talk. [532] Dionysius sent one to be put to death, for finding fault with his poetry; but called him again to try him once more; and the man rose up in the midst of his recitation, saying, Come, let me go to the gallows, as choosing to die rather than to be so wearied. I am not so impatient; but I should be glad if I could sleep well while I am tied to such company. And if I had one to send to school that was sick of the talking evil, the *morbus loquendi*, I would give (as Isocrates required) a double pay to the schoolmaster willingly, one part for teaching him to hold his tongue, and the other half for teaching him to speak. I should think many such men and women half cured, if they were half as weary of speaking as I am of hearing them. He that lets such chattering swallows build in his chimney may expect to have his soup taste of their dung. Nay, though they may have some learning and goodness to season their talk, their too much loquacity will make one's stomach turn against it; and the surfeit may make some queasy stomachs dislike even the more wholesome food. Pompey was so weary of Cicero's talkativeness that he wished he had been on Caesar's side, for then he would have feared me, (says he,) whereas now his familiarity wearies me.

> *Omne supervacuum pleno de pectore manat.*

2. It is an aggravation of the sin of loquacity and idle talk, when it is done in a proud self-conceitedness of your own wit, with an unmannerly contempt of others. This is the case of abundance that have not the manners or patience to stay till another man has done his speech. They think others so long that their willingness will not hold till they come to the end. Yes, many supposedly learned men and disputants have this disease, that without any shame, or respect to order, or their own reputation, they are in such haste to answer, and talk themselves, that they cut off the speech of others in the midst, as if they should say, Hold your tongue, and let me speak that am wiser. And their excuse is, You are so long that I shall forget half before you come to the end. But if it is in a disputation or about great matters, it is usually much more to the advantage of the truth and hearers, to speak all that necessarily must be considered together, in a continued speech: for the parts of truth have such a dependence one upon another, like the members of a body, or the wheels of a watch, that they are not understood separately, half the sense of them being relative to the other parts. Therefore to deliver it (in such cases) by fragments, and chopping of words, and frequent interruptions one of another, is to chat or contend, and not to open the truth with the clearness and gravity which it requires. These, therefore, that accuse others of speaking too long, to excuse their uncivil interruptions, may take their answer from Augustine, *Absit ut multiloquium deputem quando necessaria dicuntur, quantalibet sermonum multitudine ars prolixitate dicantur*. The huge volumes of Augustine, Chrysostom, Suarez, Calvin, yes, Tostatus himself, are seldom accused of idle words. If you assign to each their equal share of time, a composed speech is fitter and saves time better than interrupting altercations and exchange of words; and if your memory cannot hold all that is said, either take notes, or ask the help of some repetition, or answer the part which you do remember.

3. Idle talk is worst when it is about holy things, and tends to profane them: when men irreverently babble about the Scriptures, or controversies of religion; or when by fluent tongues men design the increase of some faction, or propagating of some error, or the displaying of their abilities. Says Jerome, (to Nepotian,) *Verba volvere et apud imperitum vulgus admirationem sui facere, indoctorum hominum est: nihil tam facile quam vilem plebem et indoctam, volubilitate linguæ decipere, quæ quicquid non intelligit, plus miratur*. Profane loquacity is the worst kind of loquacity.

4. Idle words are the greater sin when they are magnified and justified, and taken to be lawful, if not some excellent thing. As some unfortunate scholars that spend whole days and months about some trivial, unnecessary studies, while Christ, the wisdom of God, (or the subject of divine philosophy,) is neglected: [533] he that hears some of their supposed critical curiosities would say with Paul, "The Lord knows that the thoughts of the wise are futile" (1 Corinthians 3:20). And if he compares their lives with their studies, perhaps he will remember, "they became futile in their thoughts, and their senseless hearts were darkened. Although they claimed to be wise, they became fools" (Romans 1:21–22).

5. Idle words are an aggravated sin when they are studied, and pompously set forth at great labor and cost, as a matter to be gloried in; as in plays and romances: worse than tobacco-houses where men sell smoke. The pleasure, the love, the labor, the cost, the time, the deceit, the temptation, the impenitence, are the great aggravations of this sin.

**Direction 3. Understand and consider the mischief of the sin of babbling, idle talk.**

For the common cause of it is that men take it to be so small a sin that they think there is no danger in it; and therefore they fear it no more than a scratched finger.

1. (Besides the general evil mentioned in Title 1, Direction 1,) consider that much idle talk is a multitude of sins. Though one idle word were ever so small a sin, yet when it comes to hundreds and thousands, and is your daily, hourly custom, all set together cannot be small. Many thousand pence is more than one shilling or pound. And your frequent custom of idle talk may amount to a greater sinfulness than Noah's one-time drunkenness, or David's one-time adultery, or Peter's one-time denying of Christ. If a swearer should swear as often, or a liar lie as often, or a thief steal as often as many women (and men too) speak idly, what monsters should we take them for!

2. Idle talk excludes all the good conversation and edifying speech that should have been used all that time. [534] We have many greater uses for our tongues: you have your business to talk of, and your God, and your souls, and your duties, and your sins, and the life to come to talk of! Oh how many great and necessary things! And will you shut out all this edifying speech by your idle chat? Will you hinder others as well as yourselves?

3. Idle talk is a sinful consumer of time: you have greater business to spend your hours in: if you saw what a world you are ready to go to, and saw how near you are to it, you would think yourselves that you had greater business than idle chat to spend your time in. Do you know what you lose in losing all those hours?

4. Idle talk corrupts the hearers' minds, and tends to make them light, and worthless, and empty, even as good conversation tends to make them good. Why do you talk to others, but to communicate your sense and affections to them by your words? And for all that many take it for a little sin, I am sure it is not a little hurt that it does. If men were not used to being entertained with so much empty talk, they could not tell how to keep better things from their minds or mouths; nor would their thoughts be so habituated to emptiness; nor would they make such returns of idle words; whereas one empty conversation begets another, and it is a multiplying and very infectious sin.

5. As your tongues are misemployed, so your wits and minds are dishonored by empty talk. Even good words will grow contemptible when they are too cheap and common. A fiddler at the door passes only for a rogue, though music and musicians are honored: whoever took a talkative babbler for a wise man? He that is *logophilus* is seldom *philologus*, much less *philosophus*. [535] As Demosthenes said to a chatterer, If you knew more, you would say less. They seldom pass for men of action and virtue that talk much; they that say much usually do little: women, and children, and old folks are commonly the greatest talkers (I may add, mad folks). Livy notes that soldiers that prate and brag much seldom fight well; and Erasmus notes that children that quickly learn to speak are long in learning to walk. It is not the barking cur that bites. Let it be the honor of a parrot to speak much, but of a man to speak wisely. The mobility of their tongues (an honor common to an aspen leaf) is all their honor, that can *multis verbis pauca dicere*, say a little in a great many words; but *multa paucis*, much in few words, is the character of the wise, unless when the quality of the hearers prohibits it: and *qui sunt in dicendo brevissimi*, if the hearers can bear it, shall be accounted the best speakers. I am not of his mind that said, He often repented speaking, but never repented silence. But, except they are ministers, few men have so much cause to repent of silence as of speech. *Non quam multa, sed quam bene*, must be the Christian's care. As one said of philosophy, I may much more say of religion, that though an orator's excellence appears only in speaking, yet the philosopher's (and the Christian's) appears as much in silence.

6. Where there is much idle talk, there will be much sinful talk. Proverbs 10:19, "When words abound, transgression is inevitable, but the one who restrains his words is wise." There are lies, or backbitings, or meddling with other folks' matters, or scurrilous jests, if not many such sins that go along with a course of idle talk: it is the vehicle in which the devil gives his most poisonous drinks. Says Lipsius, It is given to chatterers, *non multa tantum sed male*; to speak ill, as well as to speak much.

7. Empty words hinder your own edification. Who knows if you would hold your tongues, but some one would speak more wisely, that might do you good? [536]

8. And you weary the hearers (unless they are strangely patient) when you intend to please them (or else you might as well talk all that by yourself). It is scarcely good manners for them, unless you are much their inferiors, to tell you they are weary of hearing you, and to entreat you to hold your tongues; but you little know how often they think so: I judge of others by myself; I flee from a talkative person, as from a bed that has fleas or lice: I would shut my doors against them, as I close up my windows against the wind and cold in winter. How glad am I when they have done, and gladder when they are gone! Do not make yourselves a burden to your company or friends by the troublesome noise of a tireless tongue.

9. Many words are the common causers of contention. Some word or other will fall that offends those that hear it; or else will be carried to those that are absent, and made the occasion of resentments, retellings, brawls, or lawsuits. There is no keeping quietness, peace, and love with talkative prattlers; at least not long.

10. Are you not aware what pride and impudence is in it, when you think yourselves worthiest to speak? As if you should say, You are all children to me; hold your tongues, and hear me speak! If you had Christian humility and modesty, you would in honor prefer others before yourselves, Romans 12.

10. You would think yourselves unworthiest to speak, (unless the contrary is very evident,) and desire rather to hear and learn. As Heraclitus, being asked why he alone was silent in the company, answered, That you may talk; so when you talk above your abilities, it is as if you told the company, I talk that all of you may be silent.

11. It is a voluntary sin and not repented of. For you may easily forbear it if you will; and you willfully continue in it; and therefore impenitence is your danger.

12. Lastly, consider how unprofitable a sin it is; and how little you have to hire you to commit it. What do you get by it? Will you daily sin against God for nothing?

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[530] Megabyzus, a great Persian lord, was told by Apelles that while he was silent they revered him for his gold and rich attire, but when he talked of what he did not understand, the boys in the shop laughed at him. Plutarch, *De Tranquillitate Animi*, page 154.

[531] See Ezekiel 33:30. Sollius Apollinaris Sidonius, in his description of king Theodoric, says that at his feasts, Maximum tunc pondus in verbis est: quippe quum illic aut nulla narrantur aut seria. (English: Then there is the greatest weight in the words, since there either nothing is told, or serious things.)

[532] Difficile est cum iis durare qui neque otii neque negotii tempora distinguere norunt. Theophrastus. (English: It is hard to bear with those who do not know how to tell the times of leisure from the times of business. Theophrastus.)

[533] Colossians 2:8.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Where this file begins and ends:** it opens at the start of Direction 2 of Title 4 (against idle talk) and ends with the last numbered point of Direction 3. "Title" is Baxter's word ("Tit.") for one of the six main divisions of Chapter 9; his cross-reference "tit. 1. direct. i." in Direction 3, point 1, is rendered "Title 1, Direction 1" (the general directions for governing the tongue at the start of the chapter; we have not checked which passage he means).
- **aggravations:** kept as Baxter's technical term: the circumstances that make a sin heavier or worse.
- **1 Corinthians 3:20:** NET wording. The King James had "the thoughts of the wise, that they are vain." Baxter leans on "vain" (empty, worthless), the same word he uses of idle talk; the NET's "futile" keeps the sense but loses the verbal link.
- **Romans 1:21–22:** Baxter cites "Rom. i. 21," but the last clause ("professing themselves wise, they became fools") is verse 22, so the reference is given as 1:21–22. NET wording. The King James had "became vain in their imaginations; ... their foolish heart was darkened"; the NET has "futile in their thoughts" and "senseless hearts," again losing the word "vain" that ties the verse to his point about empty words.
- **Proverbs 10:19:** NET wording. The King James had "In the multitude of words there wanteth not sin: but he that refraineth his lips is wise." The NET's "When words abound, transgression is inevitable" keeps his point (many words bring sin).
- **"in honor prefer others before yourselves, Romans 12":** Baxter's paraphrase of Romans 12:10 (King James "in honour preferring one another"), kept in his wording, not quoted. The NET has "showing eagerness in honoring one another." He cites only the chapter.
- **Two points numbered "10."** in Direction 3: the source numbers both "10." We keep Baxter's numbers. (The second reads like a continuation of the first.)
- **Latin in the body** (draft translations):
  - *Omne supervacuum pleno de pectore manat*: "Everything superfluous flows out of a full breast [mind]." Baxter gives no source; it is probably Horace, *Ars Poetica* (we have not confirmed the line).
  - *morbus loquendi*: "the talking disease," as Baxter glosses it.
  - Augustine, *Absit ut multiloquium deputem quando necessaria dicuntur, quantalibet sermonum multitudine ars prolixitate dicantur*: "Far be it from me to count it as much-speaking when necessary things are said, with however great a multitude of words and length they are said." The source prints "ars," probably a misprint for "ac" ("and"); kept as printed. We have not located the passage.
  - Jerome, to Nepotian, *Verba volvere ... plus miratur*: "To roll out words and win admiration for oneself among the unskilled crowd is the mark of unlearned men: nothing is so easy as to deceive the base and unlearned common people with a fluent tongue, for they admire all the more whatever they do not understand." "Hierom" in the source is Jerome.
  - *logophilus ... philologus ... philosophus*: a play on Greek-derived words: a "lover of words" is seldom a "lover of learning," much less a "lover of wisdom."
  - *multis verbis pauca dicere; multa paucis*: Baxter translates these himself.
  - *qui sunt in dicendo brevissimi*: "those who are briefest in speaking."
  - *Non quam multa, sed quam bene*: "Not how much, but how well."
  - *non multa tantum sed male*: "not only much but badly," as Baxter glosses it.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): discourse to talk / conversation; gallant to showy; niggardly to stingy; meat to food, and "at meat" to "at a meal"; unreverend to irreverent; meaner to lower; easilier to more easily; ridding kennels to clearing gutters ("kennel" was the gutter or channel in a street); gibbet to gallows; twattling to chattering; look to to expect; pottage to soup; savour of to taste of; queazy to queasy; distaste to dislike; Tully to Cicero (the same person); list to willingness (Baxter's "list" is inclination or desire); pretended learned to supposedly learned; disjunctly to separately; respective to relative; depute to assign; spareth to saves; crave to ask; parts to abilities; unhappy to unfortunate; impenitency to impenitence; never so small to ever so small; once drunkenness (and the like) to one-time drunkenness; vain / vanity to empty / worthless / emptiness; goes for to passes for; prater to chatterer; go (walk) to walk; auditors to hearers; draughts to drinks; wiselier to more wisely; scarce manners to scarcely good manners; fly from to flee from; stop my windows to close up my windows; unwearied to tireless; heart-burnings to resentments; rehearsals to retellings; sensible to aware; impudency to impudence; wilfully to willfully.
- **wit:** kept; in Baxter it means intelligence or understanding, not only humor.
- **keeps a good house:** keeps a generous table and is hospitable.
- **scurvy:** kept; Baxter's joke is that if weariness is a symptom of scurvy, such company would bring it on.
- **tobacco-houses:** kept; shops or houses where tobacco was sold and smoked.
- **"parrots," "mad folks," and his remarks on "loquacious women":** Baxter's own words and judgments, kept as he wrote them.
- **Persons named** (Baxter's anecdotes are kept as his; we have not checked them against their sources): Theophrastus; Dionysius (probably the tyrant of Syracuse) and the critic of his poetry; Isocrates and the double fee; Pompey and Cicero; Demosthenes; Livy; Erasmus; Lipsius (probably Justus Lipsius); Heraclitus. Tostatus is probably Alonso Tostado, a Spanish bishop and commentator whose works fill many volumes, which would fit Baxter's point in "Tostatus himself." Suarez is probably the Jesuit theologian Francisco Suárez. We have not confirmed these identifications.
- **Note 531:** Baxter's source is presumably Sidonius Apollinaris (probably the fifth-century Gallic bishop), describing the Gothic king Theodoric (probably Theodoric II), perhaps in one of his letters; we have not confirmed the passage. Baxter writes "Sollius Apollinar. Sidon."
- **Note 530:** "Plutarch de Tranquil. Anim." is Plutarch's essay *On Tranquillity of Mind*; the page number refers to an edition Baxter used.
