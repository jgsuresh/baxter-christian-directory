# Chapter 8: Part 4, Title 1: against gluttony, Directions 9–14 and closing cautions

*This section begins partway through Direction 8 (not to make your table a snare), with two further objections and answers.*

*Objection.* But the scandal of greed must be avoided as well as gluttony. Folks will say that all this is done merely from a miserly, worldly mind.

*Answer.* 1. It is easier to bear that censure than the displeasure of God. 2. No scandal must be avoided by sin; it is a scandal taken and not given. 3. With temperate persons your excess is much more scandalous. 4. I will teach you a cure for this in the next direction.

*Objection.* But what if I set variety and plenty on my table? May not men choose whether they will eat too much? Do you think men are swine, that do not know when they have enough?

*Answer.* Yes, we see by certain experience that most men do not know when they have enough, and do exceed when they think they do not. There is not one of many but is much more prone to exceed than to come short, and a great many sin in excess, for one that sins by defect: and is sin so small a matter with you that you will lay snares before men, and then say, They may take heed? So men may choose whether they will go into a whorehouse, and yet the pope does scarcely deal honestly to license them at Rome; much less is it well to prepare them, and invite men to them. Will you excuse the devil for tempting Eve with the forbidden fruit, because she might choose whether she would meddle with it? What does that do on your table which is purposely cooked to the tempting of the appetite, and is fitted to draw men to gulosity and excess, and is in no way needful? "Woe to him that lays a stumbling block before the blind!" "...Never to place an obstacle or a trap before a brother or sister." It is the wicked's curse, "Let their table become a snare and trap, a stumbling block." And it was Balaam's sin that he taught Balak to tempt Israel, or lay a stumbling block before them. [428]

**Direction 9. Resolve to bestow the cost of such superfluities upon the poor, or some other charitable use; that so it does not become a sacrifice to the belly.**

Let the greatest and most needful uses be served first; it is no time for you to be glutting your appetites, and wallowing in excess, when any (yes, so many) about you lack even clothes and bread. If you thus lay out all upon the poor which you spare from feeding your own and other men's excess, then none can say that your sparing is through greedy stinginess; and so that reproach is taken off. The price of one feast will buy bread for a great many poor people. It is small thanks to you to give to the poor some leftovers, when your bellies are first glutted with as much as the appetite desired: this costs you nothing: a swine will leave that to another which he cannot eat. But if you will pinch your flesh a little, or deny yourselves, and live more sparingly and thriftily, that you may have the more to give to the poor, this is commendable indeed.

**Direction 10. Do not over-persuade any to eat when there is no need, but rather help one another against running into excess; by well-timed talk of the sinfulness of gluttony, and of the excellency of abstinence, and by friendly watching over and warning one another.**

Satan and the flesh, and its unavoidable baits, are temptation strong enough; we need not by unhappy kindness add more.

**Direction 11. When you feel your appetites eager, against reason and conscience, check them, and resolve that they shall not be pleased.**

Indecision keeps up the temptation; if you would but resolve once, you would be quiet: but when the devil finds you yielding, or wavering, or unresolved, he will never give you rest: Proverbs 23:1–3, "When you sit down to eat with a ruler, consider carefully what is before you, and put a knife to your throat if you possess a large appetite. Do not crave that ruler's delicacies, for that food is deceptive." The words translated "if you possess a large appetite" (agreeable to the Septuagint and the Arabic) are translated by Montanus, and in the Vulgate Latin, and the Chaldee Paraphrase, if you have the power of your own soul, or are master of your soul, *Compos animæ*, show that you are master of yourself by abstinence. Instead of "put a knife to your throat," that is, threaten yourself into abstinence, the Syriac and various expositors translate it, You do, or, lest you put a knife to your throat, that is, You are as bad as cutting your throat, or destroying yourself, when you are gluttonously feeding yourself. Keep up resolution and the power of reason.

**Direction 12. Remember what your body is, and what it will shortly be, and how loathsome and vile it will be in the dust. And then think how far such a body should be pampered and pleased; and at what rates. [429]**

Do not pay too dearly for a feast for worms: look into the grave, and see what is the end of all your pleasant foods and drinks; of all your elaborate, costly fare. You may see there the skulls cast up, and the ugly hole of that mouth which devoured so many sweet, delicious morsels; but there is none of the pleasure of it left now. Oh astonishing folly! that men can so easily, so eagerly, so obstinately waste their estates, and neglect their souls, and displease their God, and in effect even sell their hopes of heaven, for so small and sordid a delight as the pleasing of such a piece of flesh, that must shortly have so vile an end! Was it worth so much care, and toil, and cost, and the casting away of your salvation, to pamper that body a little while that must shortly be such a loathsome carcass? [430] I think one sight of a skull or a grave should make you think gluttony and luxury madness. Ecclesiastes 7:2, "It is better to go to a funeral than a feast. For death is the destiny of every person, and the living should take this to heart." David says of the wicked, "I will not eat their delicacies"; but, "May the godly strike me in love and correct me" (Psalm 141:4, 5). So dangerous a thing is feasting even among friends, where of itself it is lawful, that Job thought it a season for his fears and sacrifice; Job 1:4, 5, "Now his sons used to go and hold a feast in the house of each one in turn, and they would send and invite their three sisters to eat and to drink with them." But Job sacrificed for them, saying, "Perhaps my children have sinned and cursed" (that is, thought provokingly, irreverently, unholily, or contemptuously of) "God in their hearts. This was Job's customary practice." A funeral is a safer place for you than a feast.

**Direction 13. Go into the houses of the poor sometimes, and see what provision they live upon, and what time they spend at meals; and then consider whether their diet or yours tends more to the mortification of fleshly lusts? and whether theirs will not be as sweet as yours at the last? and whether mere riches should make so great a difference in eating and drinking between them and you?**

I know that where they lack what is necessary to their health, it is lawful for you to exceed them, and be thankful; but not so as to forget their wants, nor so as to turn your plenty to excess. The very sight now and then of a poor man's diet and manner of life would do you good: seeing affects more than hearsay.

**Direction 14. Look upon the ancient Christians, the patterns of abstinence, and think whether their lives were like yours.**

They were much in fastings and abstinence, and strangers to gluttony and excess; they were so prone to excess of abstinence, rather than excess of food, that a great many of them lived in wildernesses or cells, upon roots, or upon bread and water: (from the imitation of whom, in a formal, hypocritical manner, came the swarms of friars that are now in the world:) and will you commend their holiness and abstinence, and yet be so far from any serious imitation of them that you will, in gluttony and excess, oppose yourselves directly against them?

I have now exposed the odiousness of this sin, and told you, if you are willing, how you may best avoid it: if all this will not serve, but there is any "godless person" among you "like Esau, who sold his own birthright for a single meal" (Hebrews 12:16), [431] who for the pleasing of his throat will sell his soul, let him know that God has another kind of cure for such: he may cast you into poverty, where you shall be a glutton only in desire, but not have the means to satisfy your desire; he may shortly cast you into those diseases which shall make you loathe your pleasant fare, and wish you had the poor man's fare and appetite; and make you say of all the baits of your indulgence in physical pleasure, "I have no pleasure in them" (Ecclesiastes 12:1). The case will be altered with you when all your wealth, and friends, and greatness cannot keep your pampered carcass from corruption, nor obtain for your soul a comfort equal to a drop of water to cool your tongue, tormented in the flames of God's displeasure: then all the comfort you can obtain from God and conscience will be but this sad reminder, "Remember that in your lifetime you received your good things and Lazarus likewise bad things, but now he is comforted here and you are in anguish" (Luke 16:25). James 5:1, 5, "Come now, you rich! Weep and cry aloud over the miseries that are coming on you.... You have lived indulgently and luxuriously on the earth. You have fattened your hearts in a day of slaughter. You have condemned and murdered the righteous person," etc.

Yet after all this, I shall remind you that you do not run into the contrary extreme: do not place more religion in external abstinence and fastings than you ought: know your own condition, and how far either fasting or eating is really a help or a hindrance to you in those greater things which are their ends, and use them so far. [432] A decaying body must be carefully supported: an unruly body must be carefully subdued: the same medicines do not serve for contrary constitutions and diseases: to think that abstaining from meat, and glutting yourselves with fish and other foods, is acceptable to God; or that mere abstaining so many hours in a week, and serving your appetite on the rest, is meritorious; or that abstinence from food will prove you holy, without an abstinence from sin, all this is self-deluding error. Nor must you raise a great many perplexing scruples about all that you eat or drink, to no edification, but merely to your vexation; but in cheerful temperance preserve your health, and subdue concupiscence.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[428] 1 Corinthians 8:9; Leviticus 19:14; Romans 14:13; 11:9; Revelation 2:14.

[429] See 1 Corinthians 6:13. Qui Christum desiderat, et illo pane vescitur, non curat magnopere quàm de pretiosis cibis stercus conficiat. Hieron. Epist. ad Paul. (He who longs for Christ and feeds on that bread does not care greatly from how costly foods he makes dung. Jerome, Letter to Paul[inus or Paula].)

[430] Nihil tam æque tibi proderit ad temperantiam, quam frequens cogitatio brevis ævi, et incerti: Quicquid facis respice mortem. Senec. (Nothing will help you toward temperance so much as frequent thought of the shortness and uncertainty of life: whatever you do, look to death. Seneca.)

[431] Luke 6:25, "Woe to you who are well satisfied with food now, for you will be hungry."

[432] Temperantiam exigit philosophia, non pœnam. Senec. (Philosophy demands temperance, not punishment [of oneself]. Seneca.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Where this file sits:** Part 4 ("Directions for governing the taste and appetite"), Title 1 ("Directions against gluttony"). "Title" (Baxter's "_Tit._") is his word for a heading or division within a Part. The range opens in the middle of Direction 8, whose directive sentence ("If you can lawfully avoid it, make not your table a [snare]...") is in the previous file. Title 2, against drunkenness, follows directly after this range.
- **"Woe to him that lays a stumbling block before the blind!"** (Direction 8): no verse reads this way. It looks like Baxter's own blend of Leviticus 19:14 (which he cites in note 428) with a "woe" form; we keep his wording, modernized. The NET at Leviticus 19:14 reads "You must not curse a deaf person or put a stumbling block in front of a blind person."
- **Romans 14:13:** Baxter's "Let no man put a stumblingblock in his brother's way" is a loose form of the King James ("that no man put a stumblingblock or an occasion to fall in his brother's way"). The NET has "determine never to place an obstacle or a trap before a brother or sister"; we give the NET phrase. **This changes the sense he leans on:** his three quotations here are linked by the one word "stumbling block," and the NET's "obstacle" loses the link at this verse (it keeps "stumbling block" at Romans 11:9 and Leviticus 19:14).
- **Romans 11:9:** NET wording; Paul is quoting Psalm 69:22 (NET there: "May their dining table become a trap before them"). Baxter's King James has "Let their table be made a snare, and a trap, and a stumblingblock."
- **Revelation 2:14** (Balaam and Balak): Baxter paraphrases without quotation marks; kept as his paraphrase.
- **Proverbs 23:1–3:** NET wording. Baxter then discusses how the ancient versions render the phrase "if thou be a man given to appetite" (King James) and "put a knife to thy throat." We put the NET's "if you possess a large appetite" in his sentence, since it agrees with the King James sense he is describing (the reading he says agrees with the Septuagint and the Arabic); the alternative renderings he reports (master of your soul; "lest you put a knife to your throat") are his, modernized. We have not checked his reports of the ancient versions. **Montanus** is probably Benedict Arias Montanus, whose interlinear Latin of the Hebrew was widely used; not confirmed. **the Vulgate Latin:** Baxter's "the vulgar Latin." **the Chaldee Paraphrase:** the Aramaic paraphrase (Targum) of the Old Testament, as Baxter's age called it. *Compos animæ:* Latin, "master of [one's] soul."
- **Ecclesiastes 7:2:** NET wording. King James "the house of mourning ... the house of feasting: for that is the end of all men"; the NET's "a funeral than a feast" and "death is the destiny of every person" keep the sense, and fit Baxter's closing line ("A funeral is a safer place for you than a feast").
- **Psalm 141:4–5:** NET wording. Baxter's King James "Let me not eat of their dainties" is a prayer; the NET's "I will not eat their delicacies" is a resolve. His point (keep away from the feasts of the wicked) stands either way. The NET's "strike me in love" adds "in love," which the King James ("smite me; it shall be a kindness") puts differently.
- **Job 1:4–5:** NET wording. Baxter's parenthesis inside the quotation ("that is, thought provokingly...") is his own gloss on "cursed," kept where he put it. The NET's "my children" replaces King James "my sons"; Baxter does not lean on the difference. He includes "This was Job's customary practice" (King James "Thus did Job continually") inside the quotation; we keep it there.
- **Hebrews 12:16:** NET wording. King James "any fornicator, or profane person, as Esau, who for one morsel of meat sold his birthright." The NET has "godless" for "profane" and "a single meal" for "one morsel of meat." Baxter's "among you" is his own and is kept outside the quotation marks. "A single meal" slightly weakens the smallness he stresses ("one morsel... for the pleasing of his throat"), but the sense holds.
- **Ecclesiastes 12:1:** "I have no pleasure in them": NET and King James agree. In the verse the words are said of the years of old age; Baxter applies them to food.
- **Luke 16:25:** NET wording. The NET's "in anguish" replaces King James "tormented," the word Baxter uses in his own sentence just before ("tormented in the flames").
- **James 5:1, 5:** NET wording. Baxter cites verses 1 and 5 but his quotation runs into verse 6 ("You have condemned and murdered the righteous person"); we keep his reference. King James "nourished your hearts" and "been wanton" are NET "fattened your hearts" and "luxuriously," which suit his subject well.
- **Unmarked allusion:** "a drop of water to cool your tongue" (Luke 16:24, the rich man and Lazarus), kept in Baxter's wording.
- **"a scandal taken and not given"** (first answer): a standard distinction in Baxter's casuistry: offense *given* is caused by a wrong act; offense *taken* is someone's taking offense at a right one. **scandal:** here, offense or a stumbling block to others, not merely public disgrace.
- **The pope and licensed brothels at Rome** (second answer): Baxter's polemic against Rome, kept as he wrote it; we have not checked the historical claim.
- **"the swarms of friars"** (Direction 14): Baxter's polemic against the mendicant orders, kept as written.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): covetousness to greed; covetous niggardice to greedy stinginess; miserable (miserly) to miserly; abundance (a great many) to a great many; leavings to leftovers; seasonable discourses to well-timed talk; unresolvedness to indecision; divers to various; curious (fare) to elaborate; Methinks to I think; wonderful (astonishing) to astonishing; meat (food) to food or meals; flesh (as food, in "abstaining from flesh") to meat; bethink you to consider; want to lack (but "their wants" kept); detected (exposed) to exposed; procure to obtain; memento to reminder; remember (remind) to remind; tempers (bodily constitutions) to constitutions; sensuality to indulgence in physical pleasure; hinderance to hindrance; "not to satisfy" to "not have the means to satisfy" (Baxter's "not have to satisfy").
- **Kept words:** **gulosity** (greediness of appetite, a synonym for gluttony); **luxury** (indulgence and excess at table, not wealth or comfort as such); **mortification** (putting to death the desires of the flesh); **concupiscence** (disordered desire, especially bodily desire); **meritorious** (earning merit before God, which Baxter denies of mere fasting); **religion** (practiced piety); **edification** (spiritual building up); **"at what rates"** (at what cost).
- **"abstaining from meat, and glutting yourselves with fish"** (last paragraph): Baxter's "abstaining from flesh" refers to the Roman Catholic rule of abstaining from meat on fast days while eating fish; "meat" in our text means animal flesh here, while elsewhere in the file it stands for Baxter's "meat" in the old sense of food generally.
- **Note 429 (Jerome):** "Epist. ad Paul." could mean a letter to Paulinus or to Paula; we have not located the passage. 1 Corinthians 6:13 is "Food is for the stomach and the stomach is for food, but God will do away with both" (cited, not quoted, by Baxter).
- **Note 430 (Seneca):** "tam æque ... quam" is kept as printed (an unusual combination); the translation takes it as "so much ... as." We have not located the passage.
- **Note 432 (Seneca):** possibly adapted from Seneca's saying that philosophy demands frugality, not punishment (in his *Letters*); not confirmed, and the source reads "temperantiam."
