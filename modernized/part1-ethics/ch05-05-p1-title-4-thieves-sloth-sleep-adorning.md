# Chapter 5: Title 4, the thieves or time-wasters (Thieves 1-3: sloth, excess of sleep, inordinate adorning of the body)

**Title 4. The thieves or time-wasters to be watchfully avoided.**

**Thief 1.** One of the greatest time-wasting sins is idleness, or sloth. The slothful see their time pass away, and their work undone, and can hear of the necessity of redeeming it, and yet they do not have hearts to stir. When they are convinced that duty must be done, they are always delaying, and putting it off from day to day, and always saying, I will do it tomorrow, or hereafter. Tomorrow is always the sluggard's working day; and today is his idle day. He spends his time in fruitless wishes: he lies in bed, or sits idly, and wishes, If only this were laboring: he feasts his flesh, and wishes that this were fasting: he follows his amusements and pleasures, and wishes that this were prayer, and a mortified life: he lets his heart run after lust, or pride, or greed, and wishes that this were heavenly-mindedness, and a laying up a treasure above. Thus "The appetite of the sluggard craves but gets nothing, but the desire of the diligent will be abundantly satisfied" (Proverbs 13:4). "What the sluggard desires will kill him, for his hands have refused to work" (Proverbs 21:25). Every little opposition or difficulty will put him off a duty. "The sluggard will not plow during the planting season, so at harvest time he asks for grain but has nothing" (Proverbs 20:4). "The sluggard has said, 'There is a lion outside! I will be killed in the middle of the streets!'" (Proverbs 22:13). "Like a door that turns on its hinges, so a sluggard turns on his bed. The sluggard has plunged his hand in the dish; he is too lazy to bring it back to his mouth" (Proverbs 26:14–16). And at last his sloth depraves his reason, and bribes it to plead the cause of his negligence. "The sluggard is wiser in his own opinion than seven people who respond with good sense." Time will slide on, and duty will be undone, and your souls undone, if impious slothfulness is predominant. "The way of the sluggard is like a hedge of thorns, but the path of the upright is like a highway" (Proverbs 15:19). You seem always to go through so many difficulties that you will never make a successful journey of it. Indeed, when he is in duty, the slothful is always losing time. He prays as if he did not pray, and labors as if he did not labor; as if the fruit of holiness passed away as hastily as worldly pleasures. He is as slow as a snail; and covers so little ground, and does so little work, and so poorly resists opposition, that he makes little of it, and all is little more than sitting still and doing nothing. It is a sad thing that men should not only lose their time in sinful pleasures; but they must lose it also in reading, and hearing, and praying, by doing all in a heartless drowsiness! Thus "The one who is slack in his work is a brother to one who destroys" (Proverbs 18:9). If he "begins in the Spirit," and for a spurt seems to be earnest, he flags, and tires, and "ends in the flesh." "The lazy person does not roast his prey, but personal possessions are precious to the diligent" (Proverbs 12:27). If he sees and confesses a vice, he does not have a heart to rise against it, and resolutely resist it, and use the means by which it must be overcome. "I passed by the field of a sluggard, by the vineyard of one who lacks sense. I saw that thorns had grown up all over it; the ground was covered with weeds, and its stone wall was broken down. Then I scrutinized it. I was putting my mind to it—I saw; I took in a lesson: 'A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to relax, and your poverty will come like a bandit, and your need like an armed robber'" (Proverbs 24:30–34). Shake off then this unmanly sluggishness: remember that you run for the immortal crown; and therefore see that you lose no time, and do not look at the things that are behind; [292] that is, do not cast an eye, or lend an ear to any person or thing that would call you back, or stop you: heaven is before you. "...we saw their land and it is very good. You seem lethargic, but don't hesitate to invade and conquer the land" (Judges 18:9) (as the five Danite spies said to their brothers). Abhor a sluggish habit of mind: go cheerfully about what you have to do; and do it diligently, and with your might. Even about your lawful, worldly business, it is a time-wasting sin to be slothful. If you are servants or laborers, you rob your masters and those that hire you; who hired you to work, and not to be idle. Whatever you are, you rob God of your service, and yourselves of your precious time, and all that you might get in it. It is they that are lazy in their callings that can find no time for holy duties. Ply your business the rest of the day, and you may the better redeem some time for prayer and reading Scripture. Work hard on the weekdays, and you may the better spend the Lord's day entirely for your souls. Idle persons (servants or others) do put themselves behind in their work, and then say they have no time to pray or read the Scripture. Sloth robs multitudes of a great part of their lives. "Laziness brings on a deep sleep, and the idle person will go hungry" (Proverbs 19:15). You cannot say, "No one has hired you," when you are asked, "Why are you standing here ... without work?" (Matthew 20:3, 6). See how sharply Paul reproves idleness (2 Thessalonians 3), determining that "If anyone is not willing to work, neither should he eat"; and that they be avoided, as unfit for Christian society. And he sharply rebukes some women that "learn to be lazy," "going around from house to house" (1 Timothy 5:13). And "Do not lag in zeal, be enthusiastic in spirit, serve the Lord" (Romans 12:11). A painstaking, diligent person is always redeeming time, while he does that which is good; and a slothful person is always losing it.

**Thief 2.** The second thief or time-waster is excess of sleep. Necessity cures most of the poor of this; but many of the rich are guilty of it. If you ask me, What is excess? I answer, All that is more than is needful to our health and business. So much as is necessary to these, I do not condemn. And therefore the infirm may take more than the healthy; and the old more than the young: and those that find that an hour's more sleep will not hinder them, but further them in their work, so that they shall do the more, and not the less, as being unfit without it, may use it as a means to the later good use of their time. But when sluggish persons spend hours in bed, which neither their health nor labors need, merely out of a swinish love of sleep; indeed, when they will have no work to do, or calling to employ them, but what shall give place to their sleepy disease, and think they may sleep longer than is necessary, because they are rich and can afford it, and have no necessary business to call them up; these think they may consume their precious time, and sin more, and wrong their souls more, because God has given them more than others. As if their servant should plead that he may sleep more than others, because he has more wages than others. Oh, if these drowsy wretches knew what work they have to do for God, and their poor souls, and those about them, it would quickly awake them, and make them stir. If they only knew how earnestly they will shortly wish that they had all those hours to spend again, they would spend them better now than in drowsiness. If they only knew what a woeful account it will be, when they must be answerable for all their time, to say, we spent so many hours every week or morning in excess of sleep, they would be roused from their sty, and find some better use for their time, which will be sweeter in the review, when time is ended, and must be no more.

**Thief 3.** The next thief or time-waster is inordinate adorning of the body. The poor may thank God that they are free also from the temptations to this, and can quickly dress themselves and go about their business; but many ladies and gallants are so guilty of this vice that I wonder conscience is so patient with them. [293] O poor neglected, undressed souls! O filthy consciences, never cleansed from your pollutions by the Spirit or blood of Christ! Do you not have better use for precious hours than to be washing, and pinning, and dressing, and curling, and spotting, and powdering, till ten or eleven o'clock in the morning, when honest laborers have done one half of their day's work? While you are in health, would not six o'clock in the morning be a fitter hour for you to be dressed, that you might draw near to the most holy God in holy prayer, and read his word, and set your souls, and then your families, in order for the duties of the following day? I do not say that you may go no neater than poor laboring people, or that you may spend no more time than they in dressing yourselves: but I say that for your souls, and in your callings, you are bound by God to be as diligent as they; and have no more time given you to lose than they, and that you should spend as little of it in making yourselves neat as you can, and be aware that otherwise the loss is your own: and that abundance of precious hours which your pride consumes will lie heavy one day upon your consciences; and then you shall confess, I say, you shall confess it with aching hearts, that the duties you owed to God and man, and the care of your souls, and of your families, should have been preferred before your appearing neat and spruce to men. If you have only a journey to make, you can rise earlier and be sooner dressed; but for the good of your souls, and the redeeming of your precious time, you cannot. Oh that God would only show you what greater work you have to do with those precious hours! and how it will cut your hearts to think of them at last! If you were only lying hopelessly sick of a consumption, you would be cured, it is likely, of this proud disease, and spend less of your time in adorning the flesh, which is hastening to the grave and rottenness! And can you not now see how time and life waste away? and what cause you have, with all your care and diligence, to use it better before it is gone? I know those that are so much worse than childish as wastefully to cast away so many hours in making themselves fine for the sight of men, and are not ashamed to come forth and show their sin to others, will scarcely lack words to excuse their crime, and prove it lawful, be they sense or nonsense. But conscience itself shall answer all, when time is gone, and make you wish you had been wiser. You do not know, ladies and gallants, how precious a thing time is: you little feel what a price you yourselves will set upon it at the last: you little consider what you have to do with it: you do not see how it hastens, and how near you stand to vast eternity! You little know how despised time will look an awakened conscience in the face! or what it is to be found unready to die! I know you do not lay these things to heart; for if you did, you could not, I say, you could not so lightly cast away your time. If all were true that you say, that indeed your place and honor require that your precious morning hours be thus spent, I declare to you, I should pity you more than galley slaves, and I would bless myself from such a place and honor, and make haste into the course and company of the poor, and think them happy that may better spend their time. But indeed your excuses are frivolous and untrue, and only show that pride has prevailed to captivate your reason to its service. For we know lords and ladies, as great as the rest of you (though alas, too few), that can be quickly up and dressed, and spend their early hours in prayer and adorning their souls, and can be content to come forth in a plain and unelaborate attire; and yet are so far from being derided, or thought the worse of by any whose judgment is much to be regarded, that they are taken justly for the honor of their order: and if it were not that some few such keep up the honor of your rank, I will not tell you how little in point of morality it would be honored.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). All NET wording was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Title and Thief:** "Tit." in the source is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," meaning a heading or main division of Chapter 5; this file begins Title 4. Baxter numbers the items of this Title "_Thief_ I.," "II.," and so on, not "Direction"; we render them **Thief 1.**, **Thief 2.**, **Thief 3.** This file ends at the close of Thief 3; the next file continues Title 4.
- **References moved:** Baxter usually prints the Proverbs references before the quotation ("Prov. xxi. 25, 'The desire ...'"); as elsewhere in this project, we put them in parentheses after it.
- **Proverbs 13:4:** NET wording. Baxter's "the soul of the diligent shall be made fat" is "the desire of the diligent will be abundantly satisfied."
- **Proverbs 20:4 (flag):** Baxter introduces this verse with "Every little opposition or difficulty will put him off a duty," leaning on the King James "will not plough by reason of the cold." The NET reads "will not plow during the planting season," so the cold, the "little difficulty" Baxter points to, is gone.
- **Proverbs 26:14–16 (flag):** Baxter cites 14–16 but quotes verses 14–15 here, then quotes verse 16 ("The sluggard is wiser ...") two sentences later without a separate reference; we kept his single reference. In verse 15 the King James "hideth his hand in his bosom" is "has plunged his hand in the dish" in the NET. In verse 16 the King James "in his own conceit ... than seven men that can render a reason" is "in his own opinion than seven people who respond with good sense"; Baxter's lead-in, that sloth "depraves his reason," still fits.
- **Proverbs 15:19:** NET wording. "the way of the righteous is made plain" is "the path of the upright is like a highway."
- **Proverbs 18:9 (flag):** the King James calls the slothful "brother to him that is a great waster," and the word "waster" fits Baxter's theme of time-wasters. The NET reads "a brother to one who destroys," so the echo is lost. The NET also has "slack" for "slothful."
- **"begins in the Spirit ... ends in the flesh" (flag):** no reference in Baxter's text; it is Galatians 3:3 (our identification, not in Baxter's text), recast by Baxter into the third person. We kept his wording in his quotation marks. The NET reads "Although you began with the Spirit, are you now trying to finish by human effort?", so the contrast of Spirit and "flesh" that Baxter quotes is not in the NET wording.
- **Proverbs 12:27:** NET wording. The King James "roasteth not that which he took in hunting" is "does not roast his prey."
- **Proverbs 24:30–34:** NET wording. The NET sets verses 33–34 as a quotation (the lesson taken in), so we nested single quotation marks. The King James "nettles had covered the face thereof" is "the ground was covered with weeds," and "thy poverty come as one that travelleth; and thy want as an armed man" is "your poverty will come like a bandit, and your need like an armed robber."
- **"do not look at the things that are behind" and note 292:** Baxter's phrase is not set as a quotation; his note points to Philippians 3:11–14. The NET of 3:13 reads "Forgetting the things that are behind." "The immortal crown" and "run" echo 1 Corinthians 9:24–25 (our suggestion, not in Baxter's text).
- **Judges 18:9 (flag):** Baxter quotes "We have seen the land, and behold it is very good; and are ye still? be not slothful to go and to enter, and possess the land" (King James). The NET verse begins "They said, 'Come on, let's attack them,'" so we begin at "...we saw their land." The NET has "You seem lethargic, but don't hesitate," so the word "slothful," which is why Baxter cites the verse, is gone, though "lethargic" keeps the sense. "brethren" is rendered "brothers" (here, fellow members of the tribe of Dan).
- **Proverbs 19:15:** NET wording. "an idle soul shall suffer hunger" is "the idle person will go hungry."
- **Matthew 20:3, 6 (flag):** Baxter writes "No man hath hired you," adapting the laborers' answer "Because no man hath hired us" (King James), which is in verse 7, not 3 or 6. We kept his adapted words (modernized) as his and his reference. The NET of verse 7 reads "Because no one hired us." His "Why stand you idle?" is from verse 6, where the NET reads "Why are you standing here all day without work?"; we quote it with "all day" left out, as Baxter leaves it out. The NET's "without work" loses the word "idle" that Baxter is using.
- **2 Thessalonians 3:** Baxter quotes verse 10 ("they that will not work should not eat") in a looser, plural form; we give the NET, "If anyone is not willing to work, neither should he eat." His "that they be avoided, as unfit for Christian society" summarizes 3:6 and 3:14 (our identification).
- **1 Timothy 5:13:** Baxter quotes "learn to be idle, wandering about from house to house." The NET has the two phrases the other way round ("going around from house to house they learn to be lazy"), so we quote them as two NET fragments in Baxter's order. Baxter's "idle" is "lazy" in the NET. We moved his reference ("And 1 Tim. v. 13, he sharply rebuketh") to the end of the sentence, as with other references.
- **Romans 12:11 (flag, important):** Baxter quotes the King James "Not slothful in business, but fervent in spirit, serving the Lord," and uses it for diligence in one's daily work ("business"). The NET reads "Do not lag in zeal, be enthusiastic in spirit, serve the Lord"; "business" (here meaning diligence, earnestness) is "zeal," so the verse no longer speaks of work as Baxter applies it.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "a laying up a treasure above" (Matthew 6:20); "do it ... with your might" (Ecclesiastes 9:10); "prays as if he did not pray" may echo the "as if ... not" pattern of 1 Corinthians 7:29–31 (our suggestion, not in Baxter's text).
- **Word swaps:** "still" (meaning always, continually) to "always" throughout Thief 1 ("still delaying," "saying still," "still the sluggard's working day," "seem still to go," "still losing time," "still redeeming time"); "have not hearts to stir" and "hath not a heart" to "do not have hearts / a heart"; "Would this were labouring" to "If only this were laboring"; "sports" to "amusements"; "covetousness" to "greed"; "put him by a duty" to "put him off a duty" (turn him aside from it); "Yea" to "Indeed"; "rids so little ground" to "covers so little ground"; "all is but next to sitting still" to "all is little more than sitting still"; "therein" to "in it"; "do cast themselves behindhand" to "do put themselves behind"; "painful" (taking pains) to "painstaking"; "reprehend not" to "do not condemn"; "the after-improvement of their time" to "the later good use of their time" ("improve" time means make good use of it, as in the chapter title); "Did these ... know ... Did they but know" to "if these ... knew ... If they only knew"; "dress them" to "dress themselves"; "Have you not better use" to "Do you not have better use"; "were not six o'clock ... a fitter hour" to "would not six o'clock ... be a fitter hour"; "bestow" to "spend"; "neatifying you" to "making yourselves neat"; "be sensible that else" to "be aware that otherwise"; "If you have but a journey to go" to "If you have only a journey to make"; "would but show" to "would only show"; "If you lay but hopelessly sick" to "If you were only lying hopelessly sick"; "it is like" to "it is likely"; "hasting" to "hastening"; "how time and life consume" to "how time and life waste away"; "they that are ... as prodigally to cast away" to "those that are ... as wastefully to cast away"; "be not ashamed" to "are not ashamed"; "scarce want words" to "scarcely lack words"; "You know not ... you see not ... you lay not to heart" to "You do not know ... you do not see ... you do not lay ... to heart"; "a wakened conscience" to "an awakened conscience"; "I profess to you" to "I declare to you"; "bless me from" to "bless myself from"; "do but show" to "only show"; "incurious" (not elaborate, plain) to "unelaborate"; "thought the worse by" to "thought the worse of by."
- **Kept words:** "mortified" (put to death to sin; see the earlier notes); "impious slothfulness ... predominant" (ruling, as with a reigning sin); "callings" (one's occupation or station, seen as given by God); "the Lord's day" (Sunday); "swinish" and "sty" (Baxter's blunt words, kept); "gallants" (fashionable young men of the gentry); "spotting" (probably putting on the small black cosmetic patches fashionable in Baxter's day; we have not confirmed his exact sense); "a consumption" (a wasting disease, often what is now called tuberculosis); "galley slaves" (men condemned to row in galleys); "bless myself from" (to ward off, wish to be kept from, as by making the sign of the cross; an old idiom); "their order ... your rank" (their social rank, the nobility); "be they sense or nonsense" (whether the words are sensible or not); "prevailed to captivate your reason" (succeeded in taking your reason captive).
- **Source oddity:** the source prints "plead that; he may sleep more than others." The semicolon cuts the clause from "that" and is almost certainly a misprint; we removed it.
- **women and "ladies and gallants":** Baxter's remarks on women (1 Timothy 5:13; note 293, quoting Terence) and on fashionable people of rank are rendered as he wrote them; they reflect his time and his polemic against idleness among the rich.
- **Terence** (note 293): the Roman comic playwright. We have not confirmed which play the line comes from.
