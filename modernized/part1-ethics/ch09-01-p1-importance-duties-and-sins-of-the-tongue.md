# Chapter 9: Title 1, Directions 1–3 (the importance, duties, and sins of the tongue)

**Chapter 9. Directions for the Government of the Tongue. [474]**

**Title 1. The General Directions.**

**Direction 1. Understand in general of what importance and concern it is that the tongue be well governed and used.**

For they that think words are inconsiderable will use them inconsiderately. The notion that words are of small importance (as some say of thoughts, that they are free) causes men to use their tongues as if they were free, saying, "Our lips are our own: who is lord over us?" (Psalm 12:4).

*In the margin: The greatness of the sins and duties of the tongue.*

1. The tongue of man is his glory; [475] by which expressively he excels the brutes; and a wonderful work of God it is, that a man's tongue should be able to articulate such an exceeding number of words: and God has not given man so admirable a faculty for emptiness and sin; the nobler and more excellent it is, the more to be regarded, and the greater is the fault of those that abuse it. Hilary compares them to a bad barber that cuts a man's face and so disfigures him, when his work was to have made him more neat and handsome. So it is the function of the tongue to be excellently serviceable to the good of others, and to be the glory of mankind; the shame therefore of its faults is the more inexcusable.

2. The tongue is made to be the index or expresser of the mind; therefore if the mind is worth regard, the tongue is worth regard. And if the mind is not worth regard, the man is not worth regard. For our Lord tells us that the tree is known by its fruit; an evil tree brings forth evil fruits: and "the mouth speaks from what fills the heart." [476] And Aristotle says that "such as a man is, such are his speeches, such his works, and such his life." [477] Therefore by empty or sinful words you tell men the emptiness and corruption of your minds.

3. Men's works have a great dependence on their words; therefore if their deeds are worth regard, their words are worth regard. Deeds are stirred up or caused by words. Daily experience tells us the power of speech. A speech has saved a kingdom, and a speech has lost a kingdom. Great actions depend on them, and greater consequences.

4. If the men that we speak to are worth regard, words are worth regard. For words are powerful instruments of their good or hurt. God uses them by his ministers for men's conversion and salvation; and Satan uses them by his ministers for men's subversion and damnation. How many thousand souls are hurt every day by the words of others! some deceived, some puffed up, some hardened, and some provoked to sinful passions! And how many thousand are every day edified by words! either instructed, admonished, quickened, or comforted. Paul says, "The weapons of our warfare ... are made powerful by God" (2 Corinthians 10:4). And Pythagoras could say that "tongues cut deeper than swords, because they reach even to the soul." Tongue sins and duties therefore must necessarily be great.

5. Our tongues are the instruments of our Creator's praise, purposely given us to "speak good of his name," and to "declare his works with rejoicing." [478] It is no small part of that service which God expects from man which is performed by the tongue; nor a small part of the end of our creation: the use of all our highest faculties, parts, and graces, are expressively by the tongue: our wisdom and knowledge, our love and holiness, are much lost as to the honor of God, and the good of others, if not expressed. The tongue is the lantern or window of the soul, by which it looks out, and shines to others. Therefore the sin or duty of so noble an instrument is not to be made light of by any that regard the honor of our Maker.

6. Our words have a great reflection and operation upon our own hearts. As they come from them, so they recoil to them, as in prayer and conversation we daily observe. Therefore for our own good or hurt, our words are not to be made light of.

7. God's law and judgment will best teach you what regard you should have to words. Christ tells you that "by your words you will be justified, and by your words you will be condemned" (Matthew 12:37). And it is words of blasphemy against the Holy Spirit which are the unpardonable sin. "If someone does not stumble in what he says, he is a perfect individual, able to control the entire body as well" (James 3:2). "The tongue is a fire! The tongue represents the world of wrongdoing among the parts of our bodies. It pollutes the entire body and sets fire to the course of human existence—and is set on fire by hell" (verse 6). "If someone thinks he is religious yet does not bridle his tongue, and so deceives his heart, his religion is futile" (James 1:26). "For the one who wants to love life and see good days must keep his tongue from evil and his lips from uttering deceit" (1 Peter 3:10). "I tell you that on the day of judgment, people will give an account for every worthless word they speak" (Matthew 12:36). The third commandment tells us that "the Lord will not hold guiltless anyone who takes his name in vain." And Psalm 15:1–3, "speaks honestly. ... He does not slander," is the mark of him that shall be "a guest in [God's] home" and "live on [his] holy hill." And the very work of heaven is said to be the perpetual "praising of God" (Revelation 14:11). Judge now how God judges of your words.

8. And some conjecture may be made by the judgment of all the world. Do you not care yourselves what men speak of you and to you? Do you not care what language your children, or servants, or neighbors give you? Are not words against the king treasonable and capital, as well as deeds? The "wheel of affairs or course of nature is set on fire by words" (James 3:6). I may conclude then with Proverbs 18:21, "Death and life are in the power of the tongue": and Proverbs 21:23, "The one who guards his mouth and his tongue keeps his life from troubles."

*In the margin: The duties of the tongue.*

**Direction 2. Understand well and remember the particular duties of the tongue; for the mere restraint of it from evil is not enough; and they are these:**

1. To glorify God by the magnifying of his name; to speak of the praises of his attributes and works. 2. To sing psalms of praise to him, and delight our souls in the sweet commemoration of his excellencies. 3. To give him thanks for the mercies already received, and declare to others what he has done for our souls and bodies, for his church and for the world. 4. To pray to him for what we lack, and for our brethren, for the church, and for the conversion of his and our enemies. 5. To appeal to him and swear by his name when we are called to it lawfully. 6. To make our necessary covenants and vows to him, and to make open profession of our belief, subjection, and obedience to him, before men. 7. To preach his word, or declare it in conversation, and to teach those that are committed to our care, and edify the ignorant and erroneous as we have opportunity. 8. To defend the truth of God by conference or debate; and refute the false doctrine of deceivers. 9. To exhort men to their particular duties, and to reprove their particular sins; and try to do them good as we are able. 10. To confess our own sins to God and man as we have occasion. 11. To ask the advice and help of others for our souls; and inquire after the will of God, and the way to salvation. 12. To praise that which is good in others, and speak good of all men, superiors, equals, and inferiors, so far as there is just ground and cause. 13. To bear witness to the truth, when we are called to it. 14. To defend the cause of the just and innocent, and vindicate them against false accusers; and excuse those causes and persons that deserve excuse. 15. To communicate and convey to others the same good impressions and affections of mind which God has worked on us, and not only the bare truths themselves which we have received. 16. Lastly, to be instruments of common conversation; of expressing our mutual affections and respects, and transacting all our worldly business: for learning, arts, manufactures, etc. These are the uses and duties of the tongue. [480]

*In the margin: The sins of the tongue.*

**Direction 3. Understand and remember what are the sins of the tongue to be avoided.**

And they are very many, and many of them very great: the most observable are these:

1. (Not to say any more of the sins of omission; because it is easy to know them, when I have named the duties, which are done or omitted,) among the sins of commission, the first that I shall name is blasphemy, as being the greatest; which is the reproaching of God: to speak contemptuously of God, or to vilify him, or dishonor him, by the denying of his perfections, and to debase him by false titles, doctrines, images, resemblances, as likening him to man in any of our imperfections; anything that is a reproaching of God is blasphemy. Such as Rabshakeh used when he threatened Hezekiah; and such as unbelievers and heretics use, when they deny his omnipresence, omniscience, government, justice, particular providence or goodness; and affirm any evil of him, as that he is the author of sin, or false to his word, or that he governs the world by mere deceit, or the like.

2. Another sin of the tongue is false doctrine, or teaching things false and dangerous as from God. If anyone falsely says he had such or such a point by divine inspiration, vision, or revelation, that makes him a false prophet. But if he only says falsely that this or that doctrine is contained in the Scripture, or delivered by tradition to the church, this is but to be a false teacher; which is a sin greater or less according to the aggravations mentioned hereafter.

3. Another of the sins of the tongue is an opposing of godliness indirectly, by false application of true doctrine, and an opposing of godly persons for the sake of godliness, and quibbling against particular truths and duties of religion; or indirectly opposing the truth or duty under pretense of opposing only some disputed mode or imperfection in him that speaks or performs it: a defending of those points and practices which would subvert or undermine religion: a secret effort to make all serious godliness seem a needless thing. There are many that seem orthodox that are impious and malicious opposers of that truth in the application which they themselves notionally hold, and positively profess.

4. Another great sin of the tongue is the profane deriding of serious godliness, and the mocking, and jesting, and scorning at godly persons as such; or scorning at some of their real or supposed imperfections, for their piety's sake, to make them odious, that piety through them might be made odious. When men so speak that the drift and tendency of their speech is to draw men to a dislike of truth or holiness; and their mocks or scorns at some particular opinion, or practice, or mode, tend to the contempt of religion in the serious practice of it. When they mock at a preacher of the gospel, for some expressions or imperfections, or for truth itself, to bring him and his doctrine into contempt; or at the prayers and speeches of religious persons, to the injury of religion.

5. Another great sin of the tongue is unjustly to forbid Christ's ministers to preach his gospel, or speak in his name; or to stand up against them and contradict, resist, and hinder them in the preaching of the truth; and, as Gamaliel calls it, "fighting against God" (Acts 5:39). Yet thus they did to the apostles; "they summoned the apostles and had them beaten. Then they ordered them not to speak in the name of Jesus and released them" (verse 40). So "they called them in and ordered them not to speak or teach at all in the name of Jesus. But Peter and John replied, 'Whether it is right before God to obey you rather than God, you decide, for it is impossible for us not to speak about what we have seen and heard'" (Acts 4:18–19). "Who killed both the Lord Jesus and the prophets and persecuted us severely. They are displeasing to God and are opposed to all people because they hinder us from speaking to the Gentiles so that they may be saved. Thus they constantly fill up their measure of sins, but wrath has come upon them completely" (1 Thessalonians 2:15–16). As Dr. Hammond paraphrases it, "And this generally is the ground of their quarrel with us, that in spite of their prohibition we preach to the Gentiles."—

6. Another sin of the tongue is profane swearing, either by God or by creatures: and also all light and irreverent use of the name and attributes of God, of which more afterwards.

7. Much more is perjury or forswearing a most heinous sin, it being an appealing to God, the author and defender of truth, to bear witness to an untruth, and to judge the offender; and so a craving of vengeance from God.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). Every NET quotation here was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Title:** "Tit." in the source is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," meaning a heading or division of the chapter. Chapter 9 has six Titles; this file covers the opening of Title 1, the general directions.
- **Chapter heading and note 474:** "government of the tongue" means the governing or control of speech. In note 474, "holy conference" (rendered "holy conversation") means talk about the things of God; the cross-reference to Part 2, Chapter 10 has not been checked against our files.
- **Footnote 479 has no marker in the source body.** The Gutenberg text prints the note among the chapter's footnotes but no "[479]" appears anywhere in the text. Its opening reference (Matthew 12:31, blasphemy against the Spirit) suggests it belongs to the sentence about "the unpardonable sin" in point 7 of Direction 1. We carry the note over and have not added a marker Baxter's text does not have.
- **Psalm 12:4:** Baxter quotes the King James wording ("Our lips are our own: who is lord over us?"), and we keep his wording because his point rests on "our lips are our own." The NET reads differently: "We speak persuasively; we know how to flatter and boast. Who is our master?" So the NET keeps the defiant question but not the claim of owning one's lips.
- **Matthew 12:34** (point 2): NET "the mouth speaks from what fills the heart"; King James "out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh." The sense is the same. Baxter gives no reference in the body; note 476 does. "The tree is known by its fruit" (Matthew 12:33; 7:16–18) is given in his words, not as a quotation.
- **2 Corinthians 10:4:** King James "mighty through God"; NET "made powerful by God." We use "..." for the NET's "are not human weapons, but," which Baxter's King James quotation does not have in that form.
- **"speak good of his name"; "declare his works with rejoicing"** (point 5): kept in Baxter's wording. The second is the King James wording of Psalm 107:22 (NET: "loudly proclaim what he has done"), which is not among the references in note 478. The first does not match the wording of any verse we checked; it seems to be Baxter's summary of the psalms in note 478 (for example Psalm 96:2, NET "Praise his name"; Psalm 135:3, NET "Sing praises to his name").
- **Matthew 12:37, 12:36; James 3:2, 3:6, 1:26; 1 Peter 3:10; Exodus 20:7; Proverbs 18:21, 21:23:** NET wording. Baxter's references came before each quotation ("James iii. 2, ..."); we put them in parentheses after it, as elsewhere in this project. Sense changes worth noting: James 3:2 King James "bridle the whole body," NET "control the entire body"; James 1:26 King James "this man's religion is vain," NET "his religion is futile"; Matthew 12:36 King James "every idle word," NET "every worthless word" (Baxter's interest in idle talk carries through, though "worthless" is broader); Proverbs 21:23 King James "keepeth his soul from trouble," NET "keeps his life from troubles" (Baxter's spiritual reading of "soul" is weaker in the NET). In Exodus 20:7 Baxter wrote "God will not hold him guiltless"; the NET reads "the Lord."
- **Psalm 15:1–3:** Baxter quotes "Speaking the truth in his heart, and not backbiting with the tongue" and calls it the mark of him that shall "abide in God's tabernacle, and dwell in his holy hill." The NET has "speaks honestly" (verse 2) and "He does not slander" (verse 3), and asks "who may be a guest in your home? Who may live on your holy hill?" (verse 1). The NET drops "in his heart," which matters to Baxter's point that speech follows the heart. We put "[God's]" and "[his]" in square brackets where the NET's "your" (addressed to the Lord) would not fit Baxter's sentence.
- **Revelation 14:11:** Baxter's reference does not fit. That verse (NET) speaks of those who worship the beast having "no rest day or night"; it does not speak of praising God. He may have meant Revelation 4:8, where the living creatures "never rest day or night, saying: 'Holy, Holy, Holy...'", or Revelation 14:2–3, but we are not sure, so we keep his reference and his words "praising of God."
- **James 3:6 in point 8:** "wheel of affairs or course of nature is set on fire by words" is Baxter's own paraphrase (the Greek behind "course of nature" is literally a wheel), kept in his words. NET: "sets fire to the course of human existence."
- **Acts 5:39:** NET "fighting against God"; Baxter "to fight against God."
- **"ver. 46" corrected to verse 40:** Acts 5 has 42 verses; the words quoted are Acts 5:40.
- **Acts 4:18–19:** Baxter's quotation runs into verse 20 ("we cannot but speak the things which we have seen and heard"); we keep his reference as printed. NET wording; it begins "And they called them in," and we drop the "And" to fit Baxter's "So."
- **1 Thessalonians 2:15–16:** NET wording. King James "their own prophets," NET "the prophets." King James "Forbidding us to speak to the gentiles," NET "because they hinder us from speaking to the Gentiles": Baxter cites the verse as an example of forbidding preachers to preach, and the NET's "hinder" is a little weaker than "forbid," though it still fits his point.
- **Dr. Hammond:** probably Henry Hammond's *Paraphrase and Annotations upon the New Testament*; we have not checked the quotation. The source ends this paragraph with a dash ("----"), kept as a dash.
- **Hilary; Aristotle; Pythagoras; Charyllus (note 479):** Baxter's attributions, kept as his. We have not located the sayings. Charyllus is probably the Spartan king Charillus (or Charilaus) named in Plutarch's sayings of the Spartans; we have not confirmed the page reference.
- **Rabshakeh** (Direction 3, point 1): the Assyrian official who taunted Hezekiah and the God of Israel (2 Kings 18–19; Isaiah 36–37). Unmarked allusion; no reference in Baxter's text.
- **Word swaps:** importance (Baxter's "moment"); concern ("concernment"); notion ("conceit"); emptiness ("vanity," twice) and empty ("vain"); bad barber ("ill barber"); disfigures ("deformeth"); handsome ("comely"); function ("office"); inexcusable ("unexcusable"); worth regard ("regardable"); consequences ("consequents"); must necessarily ("must needs"); window ("casement," a hinged window); conversation ("conference" in point 6 and Direction 2 item 7's "discourse"; also "converse" in item 16); lack ("want"); debate ("disputation"); refute ("confute"); try ("endeavour," a verb here); ask ("crave," Direction 2 item 11); worked ("wrought"); unbelievers ("infidels"); false to his word ("false of his word"); quibbling ("cavilling"); disputed ("controverted"); mentioned hereafter ("hereafter mentioned"); effort ("endeavour," a noun); irreverent ("unreverent"); did to ("did by," meaning treated).
- **Kept words:** "expressively" (points 1 and 5) means "by way of expression," not "with feeling"; the clause "the use of all our highest faculties ... are expressively by the tongue" keeps Baxter's grammar. "brutes" (animals); "quickened" (made spiritually alive or lively); "capital" (punishable by death); "conference" in Direction 2 item 8 (formal discussion, beside "disputation"); "forswearing" (swearing falsely); "craving of vengeance" (asking for vengeance); "notionally hold" (hold in theory or opinion); "aggravations" (circumstances that make a sin worse); "brethren"; "particular providence" (God's care for individual persons and events).
- **Blasphemy and heretics** (Direction 3): Baxter's definitions and his list of what unbelievers and heretics say about God are his, kept as written.
