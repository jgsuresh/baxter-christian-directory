# Chapter 8: Part 4, Title 2, causes of tippling and drunkenness (3–8) and the greatness of the sin (1–7)

*This file begins partway through Baxter's numbered list of the causes of tippling and drunkenness (section II of Part 4, Title 2), at cause 3; causes 1–2 are in the previous file, and the list of reasons under section III continues in the next file.*

3. Another cause of tippling and drunkenness is a wicked heart, that loves the company of wicked men, and the foolish talk, and cards, and dice, by which they are entertained. One sin entices down another: [434] it is a delight to chatter over a pot, or rant and gamble, and drive away all thoughts that savor of sound reason, or the fear of God, or the care of their salvation. Many of them will say it is not for love of the drink, but of the company, that they use the alehouse; an excuse that makes their sin much worse, and shows them to be exceedingly wicked. To love the company of wicked men, and love to hear their vile and idle, foolish talk, and to gamble and play away your time with them, besides your tippling, this shows a wicked, fleshly heart, much worse than if you loved the drink alone. Such company as you love best, such are your own dispositions: if you were no tipplers or drunkards, it is a certain sign of an ungodly person, to love ungodly company better than the company of wise and godly men, that may edify you in the fear of God.

4. Another cause of tippling is idleness, when they do not have the constant employments of their callings to take them up. Some of them make it their chief excuse that they do it to pass away the time. Blind wretches! that are so near eternity, and can find no better uses for their time. To these I spoke before, chapter 5, part 1.

5. Another cause is the wicked neglect of their duties to their own families; making no conscience of loving their own relations, and teaching them the fear of God; nor following their business: and so they take no pleasure in being at home; the company of wife, and children, and servants is no delight to them, but they must go to an alehouse or tavern for more suitable company. Thus one sin brings on another.

6. Another cause is the ill management of matters at home with their own consciences; when they have brought themselves into so terrible and sad a case that they dare not be much alone, nor soberly think of their own condition, nor seriously look towards another world; but flee from themselves, and seek a place to hide themselves from their consciences, forgetting that sin will find them out. They run to an alehouse, as Saul to his music, to drink away melancholy, and drown the noise of a guilty, self-accusing mind; and to drive away all thoughts of God, and heaven, and sin, and hell, and death, and judgment, till it is too late. As if they were resolved to be damned, and therefore resolved not to think of their misery nor the remedy. But though they dare venture upon hell itself, the sots dare not venture upon the serious thoughts of it! Either there is a hell, or there is none: if there is none, why should you be afraid to think of it? If there is a hell (as you will find it if you keep on but a little longer), will not the feeling be more intolerable than the thoughts of it? And is not the thinking ahead on it a necessary and cheap prevention of the feeling? Oh how much wiser a course would it be to withdraw yourselves in secret, and there look before you to eternity, and hear what conscience has first to say to you concerning your past life, your sin and misery, and then what God has to say to you of the remedy. You will one day find that this is a more necessary work than any that you had at the alehouse, and that you had greater business with God and conscience than with your idle companions.

7. Another cause is the custom of pledging those that drink to you, and of drinking healths, by which the laws of the devil and the alehouse impose upon them the measures of excess, and make it their duty to disregard their duty to God: so lamentable a thing it is, to be the tractable slaves of men, and intractable rebels against God! Plutarch mentions one that being invited to a feast, made a stop when he heard that they compelled men to drink after the meal, and asked whether they compelled them to eat too? fearing that he went in danger of his belly. And it seems to be but custom that makes it appear less ridiculous or odious to constrain men to drinking than to eating.

8. Another great cause of excess is the devil's way of drawing them on by degrees: he does not tempt them directly to be drunk, but to drink one cup more, and then another and another, so that the worst that he seems to desire of them is but to "drink a little more." And thus, as Solomon says of the fornicator, they yield to the flatterer, and go on "like an ox that goes to the slaughter, like a stag prancing into a trapper's snare till an arrow pierces his liver—like a bird hurrying into a trap, and he does not know that it will cost him his life" (Proverbs 7:21–23).

III. The greatness of this sin appears in what is said before of gluttony. More specially, 1. Think how base a master you serve, being thus a slave to your throat. What a beastly thing it is, and worse than beastly! for few beasts but a swine will be forced to drink more than does them good. How low and poor is that man's reason that is not able to command his throat!

2. Think how you consume the creatures of God, that are given for service, and not for gluttony and indulgence. The earth shall be a witness against you, that it bore that fruit for better uses, which you misspend on your sin. Your servants and cattle that labor for it shall be witnesses against you. You offer the creatures of God as a sacrifice to the devil, for drunkenness and tippling is his service. It would be less folly to do as Diogenes did, who, when they gave him a large cup of wine, threw it under the table that it might do him no harm. You make yourself like caterpillars, and foxes, and wolves, and other destroying creatures, that live to do mischief, and consume that which should nourish man; and therefore are pursued as unfit to live. You are to the commonwealth as mice in the granary, or weeds in the grain. It is a great part of the work of faithful magistrates to weed out such as you.

3. You rob the poor, consuming that on your throat which should maintain them. If you have anything to spare, it will comfort you more at last to have given it to the needy than that a greedy throat devoured it. The greedy person is much better in this than the drunkard and the self-indulgent; for he is a gatherer, and the other is a scatterer. [435] The commonwealth maintains a double or triple cost in such as you are. As the same pasture will keep many sheep which will keep but one horse; so the same country may keep many temperate persons which will keep but a few gluttons and drunkards. The worldling makes provision cheaper by getting and sparing; but the drunkard and glutton make it dearer by wasting. The greedy man, that scrapes together for himself, often gathers for one that will pity the poor when he is dead (Proverbs 28:8); but the drunkard and the wasteful devour it while they are alive. One is like a hog that is good for something at last, though his feeding yields no profit while he lives; the other is like devouring vermin, that leave nothing to pay for what they consumed. The one is like a pike among the fishes, who pays when he is dead for that which he devoured alive; but the other is like the drain or gutter, that repays you nothing but stink and dirt for all that you cast into it.

4. You draw poverty and ruin upon yourself. Besides the value which you waste, God usually joins with the prodigal by his judgments, and scatters as fast as he. "The one who loves pleasure will be a poor person; whoever loves wine and anointing oil will not be rich" (Proverbs 21:17). "One person is generous and yet grows more wealthy" (Proverbs 11:24). But this is not the outcome of your scattering. "Listen, my child, and be wise, and guide your heart on the right way. Do not spend time among drunkards, among those who eat too much meat, because drunkards and gluttons become impoverished, and drowsiness clothes them with rags" (Proverbs 23:19–21). [436]

5. You are an enemy to your family. You grieve your friends. You impoverish your children, and rob those whom you are bound to make provision for. You fill your house with discontents and quarrels, and banish all quietness and fear of God. A discontented or a quarrelsome wife, and ragged, dissolute, untaught children, are often signs that a drunkard or wasteful person is the master of the family.

6. You are a heinous consumer of your precious time. This is far worse than the wasting of your estate. Oh that you did but know, as you shall know at last, what those hours are worth which you waste over your pots! and how much greater work you had to lay it out upon! How many thousands in hell are wishing now in vain that they had those hours again to spend in prayer and repentance which they spent in the alehouse, and senselessly cast away with their companions in sin! Is the hourglass turned upon you, and death hurrying towards you, to put an end to all your time, and lay you where you must dwell for ever; and yet can you sit tippling and chattering away your time, as if this were all that you had to do with it? Oh what a wonder of besotted folly and stupidity is a hardened sinner, that can live so much below his reason! The senses' neglect of your soul's concern, and greater matters, is the great part of your sin, more than the drunkenness itself.

7. How base a price do you set upon your Savior and salvation, that will not give up so much as a cup of drink for them! The smallness of the thing shows the smallness of your love to God, and the smallness of your regard for his word and for your soul. Is that loving God as God, when you love a cup of drink better? Are you not ashamed of your hypocrisy, when you say you love God above all, when you do not love him as well as your wine and ale? Surely he that does not love him above ale, does not love him above all! Your choice shows what you love best more certainly than your tongue does. It is the dish that a man greedily eats of that he loves, and not that which he commends but will not touch. God tests men's love to him by their keeping his commandments. [437] It was the aggravation of the first sin, that they would not deny themselves so small a thing as the forbidden fruit, in obedience to God! And so it is of yours, that will not leave a forbidden cup for him! O miserable wretch! do you not know that you cannot be Christ's disciple if you do not forsake all for him, and do not hate even your life in comparison with him, and would not rather die than forsake him (Luke 14:26, 33)? And are you likely to lay down your life for him, you that will not leave a cup of drink for him? Can you burn at a stake for him, you that cannot leave an alehouse, or empty company, or excess for him? What a sentence of condemnation you pass upon yourself! Will you sell your God and your soul for so small a matter as a cup of drink? Never delude yourself by saying, I hope I do not do so, when you know that God has told you in his word that "drunkards ... will not inherit the kingdom of God" (1 Corinthians 6:10). No, God has commanded those that will come to heaven to have no familiarity with you upon earth; "do not even eat" with you! (1 Corinthians 5:11). Read what Christ himself says: "But if that evil slave should say to himself, 'My master is staying away a long time,' and he begins to beat his fellow slaves and to eat and drink with drunkards, then the master of that slave will come on a day when he does not expect him and at an hour he does not foresee, and will cut him in two, and assign him a place with the hypocrites, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth" (Matthew 24:48–51). Read Deuteronomy 29:19–20: If when you "hear the words of God's curse, you bless yourself in your heart, saying, I shall have peace, though I walk in the imagination of my heart, to add drunkenness to thirst; the Lord will not spare that man, but then the anger of the Lord and his jealousy shall smoke against him, and all the curses that are written in this book shall lie upon him, and the Lord shall blot out his name from under heaven, and the Lord shall separate him to evil." You see here how God will spice your cups.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows the Latin in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[434] Why Gregory set up wakes, and church-ales, and meetings on holidays in England, you may see lib. x. Regist. Ep. 71. in policy to win the heathens: Qui boves solent multos in sacrificio dæmonum occidere, debet his etiam de hac re aliqua solemnitas immutari, ut die dedicationis vel natalitiis martyrum, tabernacula sibi circa easdem ecclesias, quæ ex fanis commutatæ sunt, de ramis arborum faciant, et religiosis conviviis solennitatem celebrent. Nec diabolo jam animalia immolent, sed ad laudem Dei in usu suo animalia occidant, et donatori omnium de satietate sua gratias agant, &c. (Since they are accustomed to kill many oxen in sacrifice to demons, some solemnity ought also to be given them in exchange for this, so that on the day of dedication, or on the birthdays of the martyrs, they may make themselves huts of tree branches around those same churches which have been converted from temples, and celebrate the solemnity with religious feasts. And let them no longer sacrifice animals to the devil, but kill animals for their own use to the praise of God, and give thanks to the Giver of all for their fill, etc.) [Book 10 of the *Register* (Gregory's letters), epistle 71.] But do Christians need this as heathens did, when we see the sad effects of such riotings? Lege Acost. 1. iii. c. 34. (Read Acosta, book 3, chapter 34.)

[435] Proverbs 13:23; 14:21; 21:13; 30:14; 22:9; 28:27.

[436] Diogenes begging of a prodigal asked a pound of him, when he asked but a penny of the next, Because, says he, I may often receive of them, but God knows whether I shall ever have more of him. Laert. in Diog. (Diogenes Laertius, in his life of Diogenes.) Proverbs 28:19.

[437] John 14:15; 1 John 5:2–3.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). All NET wording was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Labels:** this file sits in Part 4 ("Directions for governing the Taste and Appetite"), Title 2 (against drunkenness). "Tit." in the source is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," a heading or division within a Part. The roman "II." (causes) and "III." (the greatness of the sin) are Baxter's section numbers within Title 2; the numbered items keep his numbers.
- **Proverbs 7:21–23:** NET wording. The King James Baxter quotes has "as the ox to the slaughter, or as a fool to the correction of the stocks; till a dart strike through his liver; as a bird hasteth to the snare." The NET reads "like a stag prancing into a trapper's snare" where the King James has the fool going to the stocks, so Baxter's image of the fool led to punishment is gone; the ox, the arrow (his "dart"), and the bird remain, which carry his point about being drawn on step by step. "As Solomon says of the fornicator": in Proverbs 7 the one led away is the young man going after the adulterous woman; Baxter's word is kept.
- **Proverbs 11:24:** the King James "There is that scattereth, and yet increaseth" uses "scatter," and Baxter's next sentence builds on it ("this is not the outcome of your scattering"; compare "scatterer" above and God who "scatters as fast as he"). The NET's "One person is generous and yet grows more wealthy" drops the word, so the link between the verse and Baxter's following sentence is weaker in the NET.
- **Proverbs 23:19–21:** NET wording. Baxter's King James has "wine-bibbers" and "riotous eaters of flesh," where the NET has "drunkards" and "those who eat too much meat"; "guide thy heart in the way" is "guide your heart on the right way" in the NET. The sense Baxter uses is unchanged.
- **Proverbs 28:8** (item 3): Baxter paraphrases ("gather for one that will pity the poor"); kept in his words. The NET reads "The one who increases his wealth by increasing interest gathers it for someone who is gracious to the needy," so the NET's verse is specifically about interest, which Baxter's "greedy man that scrapes together" generalizes.
- **1 Corinthians 6:10:** the NET verse lists thieves, the greedy, drunkards, the verbally abusive, and swindlers; Baxter quotes only "drunkards shall not inherit the kingdom of God," so the omitted list is marked "...".
- **1 Corinthians 5:11:** Baxter's King James fragment is "no, not to eat" ("with such an one no not to eat"). The NET has "Do not even eat with such a person." We set the NET's "do not even eat" inside Baxter's sentence; the result reads as a quoted command. The NET also has "not to associate with" for the King James "not to keep company," which matches Baxter's "have no familiarity with."
- **Matthew 24:48–51:** NET wording. The NET has "slave" and "fellow slaves" for the King James "servant" and "fellow-servants," "beat" for "smite," and "staying away a long time" for "delayeth his coming." Baxter's reference stood before the quotation in the source; it is moved after it, following the project's practice.
- **Deuteronomy 29:19–21:** kept in Baxter's wording (modernized only in pronouns and verb endings), because he adapts the King James to the second person and because the word he leans on is not in the NET. The King James has "to add drunkenness to thirst," and Baxter's closing line ("how God will spice your cups") depends on it. The NET reads verse 19b quite differently: "This will destroy the watered ground with the parched." NET wording of the rest: "When such a person hears the words of this oath he secretly blesses himself and says, 'I will have peace though I continue to walk with a stubborn spirit.' ... The Lord will be unwilling to forgive him, and his intense anger will rage against that man; all the curses written in this scroll will fall upon him, and the Lord will obliterate his name from memory" (29:19–20). Baxter's last clause, "the Lord shall separate him to evil," is from verse 21 (NET: "The Lord will single him out for judgment from all the tribes of Israel"), though he cites only 19–20. "God's curse" is Baxter's wording for the King James "this curse" (NET "this oath"). "The imagination of my heart" is the King James phrase; the NET has "a stubborn spirit."
- **Luke 14:26, 33:** Baxter paraphrases (forsaking all, hating even one's life); no quotation marks in the source, so his wording is kept. NET: "does not hate ... even his own life, he cannot be my disciple" (14:26); "not one of you can be my disciple if he does not renounce all his own possessions" (14:33).
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "sin will find them out" (Numbers 32:23, NET "your sin will find you out"); "as Saul to his music" (David playing the lyre for Saul, 1 Samuel 16:23); the forbidden fruit (Genesis 3); "lay down your life for him" (compare John 13:37).
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): prate / prating → chatter / chattering; game → gamble; sport out → play away; lewd → vile (in 1673 "lewd" meant base or wicked generally, not only sexual); fly → flee; hold on → keep on; fore-thinking → thinking ahead; retire yourselves → withdraw yourselves; after meat → after the meal; apprehending → fearing; gulosity → gluttony; luxury → indulgence and luxurious → self-indulgent (Baxter's sense is excessive indulgence of appetite, not costly comfort); covetous → greedy; riotous → wasteful (in Baxter's day "riotous" meant wasteful, dissolute living, not disorderly); corn → grain (English "corn" meant grain generally); treble charge → triple cost; ofttimes → often; sink or channel → drain or gutter; issue → outcome; brawlings / brawling → quarrels / quarrelsome; posting → hurrying; glass → hourglass; sottishness → besotted folly; forbear → give up; meddle with → touch; trieth → tests; delude thyself to say → delude yourself by saying.
- **Words kept:** tippling (habitual small drinking, which Baxter distinguishes from outright drunkenness); pot (a drinking pot); pledging (answering a toast by drinking in return) and drinking healths (toasts); sots (habitual drunkards, fools); melancholy (see GLOSSARY.md); callings (occupations); creatures of God (created things, here food and drink); commonwealth; worldling (a worldly, money-minded person); prodigal (a spendthrift); dearer (more expensive); constrain (force); aggravation (Baxter's technical term for what makes a sin heavier); deny themselves (refuse themselves).
- **"making no conscience of loving their own relations"** (item 5 of the causes): the idiom means not treating it as a duty of conscience; kept.
- **"if you were no tipplers or drunkards"** (cause 3): Baxter's sense is "even if you were not tipplers or drunkards"; kept as printed.
- **"The senses' neglect of your soul's concern"** (item 6): printed "The senses' neglect" in the source. It may be a misprint for "senseless neglect," which would fit the sentence better; we have not checked another edition, so the printed reading is kept.
- **"chapter 5, part 1"** (cause 4): Baxter's cross-reference, presumably to his treatment of time-wasting earlier in the *Directory*; we have not checked which section it points to.
- **Plutarch; Diogenes:** the anecdotes are Baxter's; we have not traced their sources.
- **Note 434:** Gregory is Pope Gregory I, and the letter quoted is his advice on adapting heathen festivals in England. Baxter's reference ("lib. x. Regist. Ep. 71") is kept as printed; modern editions may number the letter differently, and we have not checked. "Lege Acost. 1. iii." is printed with the numeral "1" where "l." (*liber*, book) is probably meant; kept as printed. Acosta is the Jesuit writer Baxter cites elsewhere on the native peoples of the Americas. "Wakes" and "church-ales" were parish festivals with drinking.
- **Note 436:** "pound" and "penny" are Baxter's English coins for the story. "Laert. in Diog." refers to Diogenes Laertius's life of Diogenes the Cynic.
