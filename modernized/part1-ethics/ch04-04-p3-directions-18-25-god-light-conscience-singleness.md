# Chapter 4: Directions 18-25 against hypocrisy (God's presence, the light, conscience, singleness of heart)

*This file begins at the start of Direction 18, partway through Baxter's "Directions against Hypocrisy" (the heading is in an earlier file). It ends at the end of Direction 25; Baxter's directions continue in the next file.*

**Direction 18. Remember the perfections of that God whom you worship, that he is a Spirit, and therefore to be worshiped in spirit and in truth; and that he is most great and terrible, and therefore to be worshiped with seriousness and reverence, and not to be dallied with, or served with toys or lifeless lip-service; and that he is most holy, pure, and jealous, and therefore to be purely worshiped; and that he is still present with you, and all things are naked and open to him with whom we have to do.**

The knowledge of God, and the remembrance of his all-seeing presence, are the most powerful means against hypocrisy. Christ himself argues from the nature of God, who is a Spirit, against the hypocritical ceremoniousness of the Samaritans and Jews, John 4:23, 24. Hypocrites offer that to God which they know a man of ordinary wisdom would scorn if they offered it to him. If a man knew their hearts as God does, would he be pleased with words, and compliments, and gestures, which are not accompanied with any suitable seriousness of the mind? Would he be pleased with affected, histrionical actions? One who sees a papist priest come out in his formalities, and there lead the people, in a language which they do not understand, to worship God by a number of ceremonies, and canting, repeated, customary words, would think he saw a stage-player acting his part, and not a wise and holy people, seriously worshiping the most holy God. And not only in worship, but in private duties, and in conversation with men, and in all your lives, the remembrance of God's presence is a powerful rebuke for all hypocrisy. It is more foolish to sin in the sight of God, because you can hide it from the world, than to steal or commit adultery in the open market-place, before the crowd, and be careful that dogs and crows do not discern it. If all the world sees you, it is not so much as if God in secret sees you. "Do not be deceived. God will not be made a fool" (Galatians 6:7).

**Direction 19. Remember how hypocrisy is hated by God; and what punishment is appointed for hypocrites.**

They are joined in torment with unbelievers. And, as wicked men's punishment is aggravated by their being condemned to the fire prepared for the devil and his angels; so the punishment of ordinary ungodly persons is aggravated by this, that their portion shall be with hypocrites and unbelievers. How often do you find the Lamb of God himself denouncing his thundering woes against the hypocritical scribes and Pharisees! How often does he inculcate to his disciples, "Do not be as the hypocrites," Matthew 6:2, 5, 16. And no wonder if hypocrites are hateful to God, when they and their services are lifeless images, and have nothing but the name and outside of Christianity, and some antique dress to set them off, and human ornaments of wit and parts; as a corpse is more dressed with flowers than the living, as needing those ceremonies for want of life to keep them sweet: and a carrion is not lovable to God. And the hypocrite puts a scorn on God; as if he thought that God were like the heathen's idols, that have eyes and do not see, and could not discern the secret dissemblings of his heart; or as if he were like fools and children that are pleased with fair words and little toys. God must needs hate such abuse as this.

**Direction 20. Come into the light, that your hearts and lives may be thoroughly known to you.**

Love the most searching, faithful ministry and books; and be thankful to reprovers and plain-dealing friends. Darkness is it that cherishes deceit: it is the office of the light to manifest. Justly do those wretches perish in their hypocrisy, who will not endure the light which would undeceive them; but flee from a plain and powerful ministry, and hate plain reproof, and set themselves by excuses and cavils to defend their own deceit. [154]

**Direction 21. Be very diligent in the examining of your hearts and all your actions by the word of God, and call yourselves often to a strict account.**

Deceit and guilt will not endure strict examination. The word of God is living and powerful, revealing the thoughts and imaginations of the heart. There is no hypocrite but might be delivered from his own deceits, if by the assistance of an able guide he would faithfully go on in the work of self-testing, without partiality or sloth.

**Direction 22. Live continually as one that is going to be judged at the bar of God, where all hypocrisy will be exposed and shamed, and hypocrites condemned by the all-knowing God.**

One thought of our appearing before the Lord, and of the day of his impartial judgment, one would think should make men walk as in the light, and teach them to understand that the sun is not eclipsed as often as they wink, nor is it night because they draw the curtains. What a shame will it be to have all your dissimulation laid open before all the world! "Be on your guard against the yeast of the Pharisees, which is hypocrisy. Nothing is hidden that will not be revealed, and nothing is secret that will not be made known. So then whatever you have said in the dark will be heard in the light, and what you have whispered in private rooms will be proclaimed from the housetops" (Luke 12:1-3).

**Direction 23. Do not think that you avoid hypocrisy by changing the expressions of it; but see that you do not run into a more subtle kind, while you avoid a grosser.**

There is no outward way of worshiping God, nor any opinion in religion, so sound, but a hypocrite can make a cloak of it. You see an ignorant, ridiculous hypocrite, such as Bishop Hall describes in his character, that can pray up to a pillar, when his heart does not know what his tongue is doing; that babbles over a few words to God while he is dressing or washing him, and talking between to the bystanders; who offers to God the sacrifice of a fool, and does not know that he does evil, Ecclesiastes 5:1; that serves God with toys and antic gestures, and saying over certain words which were never acquainted with the feeling of his heart, nor scarcely with his understanding. And to avoid his hypocrisy, perhaps, you can merrily deride him, and make a formal popish hypocrite the subject of your jests; and you can yourselves, with good understanding, pour out yourselves many hours together in orderly and fitting expressions of prayer: but remember that many a hypocrite makes himself a cloak of as good stuff as this; and that as pride has more advantage to work upon your greater knowledge and better parts, so hypocrisy is but the offspring of pride. All this, without a heart entirely devoted to God, is but a carcass better dressed; as the rich have more elaborate monuments than the poor. There is no outside thing in which a hypocrite may not seem excellent.

**Direction 24. Be true to conscience, and listen diligently to all it says, and be often treating with it, and daily conversant and well acquainted with it.** [155]

Hypocrites bear little reverence to their consciences: they make so often and so grossly bold with them, that conscience is deposed from its office for the present, and silenced by them, lest it should gall them by preaching to them those hard sayings which they cannot bear: and perhaps at last it is seared or bribed to take part with sin. But usually a hypocrite has a secret judge within him which condemns him. Be careful how you use your conscience, as you love your peace and happiness. Next to Christ, it must be your best friend, or your greatest enemy: palliate it however you will at present, if you wound it, it will smart at last. And it is easier to bear poverty, or shame, or torment, than to bear its wounds, Proverbs 18:14.

1. Mark the very principles and former judgment of your consciences; and if they are changed, know what changed them.

2. Listen to all the secret counsel and reproofs of conscience, especially when it speaks often and terribly; do not turn it off without a hearing; indeed, know the reason of its very scruples and doubts.

3. When it is sick and disquieted, know what the matter is, Psalm 53:5, and vomit up the matter that justly disquiets it, whatever it costs you; and be sure you go to the bottom, and do not leave the root behind.

4. Open your consciences to some able, trusty guide when it is necessary, though it costs you shame. An over-tender avoiding of such shame is the hypocrite's sin and folly. Counsel is safe in matters of such importance.

5. Prefer conscience before all men, however great: none is above it but God. It is God's messenger, when it is conscience indeed: remember what it says to you, and from whom, and for what end. Let friends, and neighbors, and company, and business, and profit, and amusements, and honor stand by, and all give way while conscience speaks; for it will be a better friend to you than any of these, if you use it as a friend. It would have been better to Judas than his thirty pieces were.

6. Yet see that it is well informed, and see its commission, for it is not above God; nor is it masterless or lawless.

7. Do not converse with it only in a crowd, but in secret, Psalm 4:4.

8. Keep it awake; and keep it among awakening means and company: it will much sooner fall asleep in an ale-house, or a play-house, or among the foolish and profane, than at a lively sermon, or prayers, or reverent talk of God.

If I could but get conscience awakened to perform its office, and preach over all this that I have said in secret, it would ferret the hypocrite out of his self-deceit. Go, conscience, and search that deceitful heart, and speak to it in the name of God: ask that hypocrite whether conversion ever made him a new creature, and whether his soul and all that he has is entirely devoted to God? and whether his hopes and treasures are laid up in heaven, and his heart is there? and whether he subjects all his worldly interest to the will of God, and the interest of his soul? and whether his greatest work is about his heart, and to approve himself to God? and whether he makes an impartial, diligent inquiry after the truth, with a desire to receive it at the dearest rates? Tell him that a proud self-flattery may now make him justify or extenuate his sins, and take his formalities, and lip-service, and abuse of God for true devotion, and hate every man that would detect his hypocrisy, and convert him by bringing in the light; but a light will shortly appear to his soul, which he shall not resist. And then let him stand to his justification if he can; and let him then make it good that he gave up himself in sincerity, simplicity, and self-denial, to his God.

**Direction 25. Remember that hypocrisy lies much in doubling, and in a dividing heart and life: see therefore that you serve God in singleness of heart, or simplicity and integrity, as being his alone.**

Do not think of serving God and mammon: a deep reserve at the heart for the world, while they seem to give up themselves in covenant to God, is the grand character of a hypocrite. Live as those that have one Lord and one Master, that all power stoops to; and one end or scope, to which all other are but means; and one work of absolute necessity to do; and one kingdom to seek first, and with greatest care and diligence to make sure of; and that have your hearts and faces always one way; and that agree with yourselves in what you think, and say, and do. [156] A double heart and a double tongue is the fashion of the hypocrite, Psalm 12:2; 1 Timothy 3:8. He has a heart for the world, and pride, and lust, which must seem sometimes to be lifted up to ask forgiveness, that he may sin with quietness and hope of salvation: you would not think when you see him drop his beads, or lift up his hands and eyes, and seem devoutly to say his prayers, how lately he came from a tavern, or a whore, or a lie, or from scorning at serious godliness. As Bishop Hall says, he seems to serve that God at church on holy-days, whom he neglects at home; and bows at the name of Jesus, and swears profanely by the name of God. Remember that there is but one God, one heaven for us, one happiness, and one way; [157] and this one is of such moment, as calls for all the intention and attention of our souls, and is enough to satisfy us, and should be enough to call us off from all that would divert us. A divided heart is a false and self-deceiving heart. Are there two Gods? or is Christ divided? [158] While you grasp at both (God and the world) you will certainly lose one, and it is likely you will lose both. To have two Gods, two rules, two heavens, is to have no God, no true rule, no heaven or happiness at all. Do not halt therefore between two opinions: if God is God, obey him and love him; if heaven is heaven, be sure it is first sought. But if your belly is your god, and the world is your heaven, then serve and seek them, and make your best of them.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[154] Permanent tepidi, ignavi, negligentes, vani, leves, voluptuosi, delicati; commoda corpores superflua sectantur, suum compendium in omnibus quærunt, ubicunque honorem et existimationem nominis sui integra servare possunt: intus propriæ voluntati pertinaciter addicti, irresignati, minime abnegati, superbi, curiosi, et contumaces sunt in omnibus, licet externe coram hominibus bene morati videantur. In tentationibus impatientes, amari, procaces, iracundi, tristes, aliis molesti, verbis tamen ingenioque scioli,--In prosperis nimium elati et hilares: in adversis, nimium turbati sunt et pusillanimes: aliorum temerarii sunt judices: aliorum vitia accuratissime perscrutari, de aliorum defectibus frequenter garrire, ac gloriari egregium putant. Ex istis et similibus operibus facillime cognosci poterunt: nam moribus gestibusque suis seu sorex quispiam suopte semet indicio produnt. Thauler. flor. p. 65, 56. (English, rendered loosely: They remain lukewarm, idle, careless, empty, frivolous, pleasure-loving, soft; they pursue needless bodily comforts, seek their own advantage in everything, wherever they can keep the honor and reputation of their name unharmed; inwardly they are stubbornly attached to their own will, unresigned, not at all self-denying, proud, meddlesome, and obstinate in everything, although outwardly before men they seem well-behaved. In temptations they are impatient, bitter, forward, irritable, gloomy, a trouble to others, yet clever-seeming [or smattering, "scioli"] in words and wit.--In prosperity they are too elated and merry; in adversity too disturbed and fainthearted; they are rash judges of others; to examine others' faults most carefully, to chatter often about others' failings, and to boast, they think admirable. By these and similar works they can very easily be recognized: for by their manners and gestures, like a shrew-mouse, they give themselves away by their own tell-tale sign. Tauler, *Flor.* [probably a collection of his sermons; not confirmed], pp. 65, 56.)

[155] Quid prodest recondere se et oculos hominum auresque vitare? Bona conscientia turbam advocat; mala autem in solitudine anxia et sollicita est. Si honesta sunt quæ facis, omnes sciant: si turpia, quid refert neminem scire, cum tu scias: O te miserum si contemnis hunc testem. Sen. Ep. 96. Matt. xxiii. 13-15, 23, 25, 27, 29. (English: What does it profit to hide oneself and to avoid the eyes and ears of men? A good conscience calls the crowd to it; but a bad one in solitude is anxious and troubled. If what you do is honorable, let everyone know it; if shameful, what does it matter that no one knows, when you know? O wretched you, if you despise this witness. Seneca, Epistle 96. Matthew 23:13-15, 23, 25, 27, 29.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). Every verse in quotation marks was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Heading layout:** in the source, each Direction's heading sentence runs straight into its explanation as one paragraph (Directions 18, 21, 24 and 25 especially). We bold the first sentence as the heading and start the explanation on a new line; no words are moved. Direction 18's bold sentence is long because Baxter's first sentence is long. In Direction 24, the numbered points 1-8 are printed run-on in one paragraph in the source; we set each on its own line, keeping Baxter's numbers and order.
- **Galatians 6:7:** Baxter's "Be not deceived, God is not mocked" is the King James. NET reads "Do not be deceived. God will not be made a fool." The sense he uses (God cannot be mocked by outward show) is close, but "made a fool" is a little different in tone from "mocked."
- **John 4:23, 24; Matthew 6:2, 5, 16:** Baxter cites John 4:23, 24 by reference only. His unmarked words in the Direction 18 heading, "worshiped in spirit and in truth," echo John 4:24 (NET: "must worship in spirit and truth"); we kept his wording. Baxter's "Be not as the hypocrites" (Matthew 6) is his own short paraphrase, so we keep his wording, modernized. NET Matthew 6:5 reads "do not be like the hypocrites."
- **Luke 12:1-3:** NET wording. Baxter's King James "leaven" is "yeast" in NET (same thing); "spoken in the ear in closets" is NET "whispered in private rooms"; "house-tops" is "housetops." We placed the reference after the quotation per our convention, though Baxter printed it before.
- **Ecclesiastes 5:1:** Baxter prints "Eccles. v. 2." The words he alludes to ("the sacrifice of a fool ... knoweth not that he doth evil") are in verse 1 in the NET (numbered 5:1, with Hebrew 4:17), and, as far as we can tell, in the King James verse 1 too. We corrected the reference, but we are not certain of the correction: verse 2 (NET 5:2, "Do not be rash with your mouth") is also about careless words before God, so Baxter may have meant it. His wording is an allusion, not a quotation, so we kept it, modernized. NET reads "draw near to listen rather than to offer a sacrifice like fools, for they do not realize that they are doing wrong."
- **Psalm 12:2; 1 Timothy 3:8:** Baxter cites these for "a double heart and a double tongue" (King James "with a double heart" and "doubletongued"). NET Psalm 12:2 reads "they flatter and deceive" and 1 Timothy 3:8 reads "not two-faced." The idea of duplicity is present, but the NET does not use "double heart" or "double tongue." Not quoted by Baxter; references only.
- **Proverbs 18:14; Psalm 53:5; Psalm 4:4:** references only. NET Proverbs 18:14 has "who can bear a crushed spirit?" (King James "wounded spirit"); Baxter's "its wounds" applies this to conscience. NET Psalm 53:5 (they are terrified "even by things that do not normally cause fear") is what he cites for a troubled conscience. NET Psalm 4:4 reads "Meditate as you lie in bed" (King James "commune with your own heart"); Baxter takes it as speaking with conscience in secret.
- **Hebrews 4:12 (unmarked allusion), Direction 21:** "quick and powerful, discovering the thoughts and imaginations of the heart." NET reads "living and active ... able to judge the desires and thoughts of the heart." We rendered Baxter's "quick" as "living" and "discovering" as "revealing"; NET's "judge" is a somewhat different emphasis.
- **Other unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "the fire prepared for the devil and his angels" (Matthew 25:41); woes on the scribes and Pharisees (Matthew 23); idols that "have eyes and see not" (probably Psalm 115:5 or 135:16); "serving God and mammon" (Matthew 6:24); "one thing needful" and "seek first" (Luke 10:42; Matthew 6:33, which Baxter's own note 156 cites); "halt between two opinions" (1 Kings 18:21; the NET there reads "paralyzed by indecision"); Judas's thirty pieces (Matthew 26:15); "Are there two Gods? or is Christ divided?" (1 Corinthians 1:13, cited in note 158).
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): worshipped, worshipping: worshiped, worshiping; discern it not: do not discern it; amiable: lovable; opened (hypocrisy "opened and shamed"): exposed; discovering: revealing; quick: living; self-trying: self-testing; standers-by: bystanders; knoweth ... doth: knows ... does; scarce: scarcely; meet (expressions): fitting; curious (monuments): elaborate; hearken: listen; at the present: for the present; Take heed: Be careful; sports: amusements; neighbours, honour: neighbors, honor; give place: give way; discourse (of God): talk; yea: indeed; it is like: it is likely; Halt not: Do not halt; thy: your; christianity: Christianity. Baxter's subjunctives ("if God be God," "whether his hopes be laid up") become ordinary indicatives ("if God is God," "whether his hopes are laid up"); "whatever it cost you" becomes "whatever it costs you."
- **Kept as Baxter wrote them:** *toys* (trifles), *canting* (insincere, singsong speech), *histrionical* (stagey, theatrical), *antic* (grotesque), *parts* (natural talents), *palliate* (to soothe over without curing), *extenuate* (to make light of), *treating with* (conferring or negotiating with), *conversant*, *character* (in "the grand character of a hypocrite," meaning mark or distinguishing sign), *scope* (aim), *moment* (importance), *intention* (here, concentrated purpose), *dissemblings*, *cavils*, *drop his beads* (to tell rosary beads). *Papist* and *popish* are Baxter's words for Roman Catholic; the polemic against the priest's ceremonies in Direction 18, and against the "formal popish hypocrite" in Direction 23, is his own and kept as written.
- **Bishop Hall** (Directions 23, 25): probably Joseph Hall, whose *Characters of Vertues and Vices* includes a portrait of the hypocrite; we have not checked the passages Baxter has in mind.
- **Note 154 (Tauler):** the Latin is a catalog of faults of those who seem good outwardly. "Scioli" means smatterers or pretenders to knowledge, and the last clause ("like a shrew-mouse") may allude to a saying that a shrew-mouse gives itself away by its squeak, but we have not confirmed that; both rendered loosely. "Thauler" is probably the medieval preacher Johannes Tauler; "flor." is probably a Latin collection of his writings, which we have not identified. The "p. 65, 56" is as printed.
- **Note 155 (Seneca):** Baxter cites Seneca, Epistle 96. We have not confirmed that these words are from that letter (they may be from another of Seneca's letters). The Latin is printed as in the source; draft translation above. Matthew 23:13-15, 23, 25, 27, 29 are the "woes" against hypocritical scribes and Pharisees.
- **Notes 156-158:** lists of references, converted to modern form and not checked against the verses, except that they are carried over as Baxter printed them.
- **"Is God" / "is heaven"** (Direction 25, last paragraph): the Elijah-style challenge; Baxter's "be sure it be first sought" is rendered "be sure it is first sought," keeping his order.
