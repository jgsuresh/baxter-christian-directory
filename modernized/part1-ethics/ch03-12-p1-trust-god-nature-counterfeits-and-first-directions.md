# Chapter 3: Grand Direction XII (part 1 of 2)

**Grand Direction 12. Trust God with that soul and body which you have delivered up and dedicated to him; and quiet your mind in his love and faithfulness, whatever shall appear to you, or happen to you in the world.**

I shall here briefly show you, 1. What is the nature of this trust in God. [118] 2. What are the contraries to it. 3. What are the counterfeits of it. 4. The usefulness of it. And then, 5, I shall give you some directions how to attain and exercise it.

1. To trust in God, is, upon the apprehension of the all-sufficiency, goodness, and faithfulness of God, to quiet our hearts in the expectation of the safety or benefits from him which we desire, rejecting the cares, and fears, and griefs that would disquiet them, if they had not the refuge of these hopes. [119] It contains in it a believing of the word or nature of God, or judging it to be a sufficient ground of our security and expectation: and then security and expectation built upon that ground, make up the rest of the nature of trust. Looking for the benefit, and finding a complacency and quietness of mind in the ground discovered, and ceasing all other cares and fears, which would else disquiet us. Aquinas and other scholastic theologians often call affiance, *spes roborata*, a confirmed hope. There is a twofold trust in God: one is, for that which he has not promised to do, but yet we think that we find reason sufficient, from his nature itself, and relations, to expect: this may be more or less certain and strong, as our inference of the will of God, from his nature, is more or less sure and clear. The other is, when we have not only God's nature, but his promise also to trust upon: and this gives us a certainty, if we certainly understand his promise. To the last sort I may assign that trust in God for particular benefits, when we have only a promise in general, which does not make the particulars known and certain to us: as the promise, that all shall work together for our good, does give us but a probability of health or outward protection and deliverances, because we are uncertain how far they are for our good. All that is promised is sure; but whether this or that be good for us, must be otherwise known. But those general promises which contain particulars as surely known as the promise itself, do make every one of the particular benefits as sure, by promise, as the general: as, the promise of the pardon of all our sins, assures us of the pardon of every sin in particular. Where there is a promise, we trust God's faithfulness as well as his nature; but where there is none, we trust his nature only. As a child quietly trusts his parents, without a promise, that they will not kill, or torment, or forsake him. But because man is apt to make false inferences of God's will from his nature, he has given us such clear expressions of it in his word, as may bring us above uncertain probabilities, and are sufficient for faith to ground upon (supposing God's properties) for our direction and peace. And it is certain that all inferences of God's will which are contrary to his word, are the errors of the one inferring.

In what I have said in this direction, I desire you chiefly to observe these three things: 1. That God's nature and love are the sufficient, general security to the soul. 2. That his promise is the sufficient, particular security. 3. And that our unfeigned self-dedication to him, is our sufficient evidence of our claim to his love and covenant, which may warrant our special trust and expectations.

II. The contraries to trust in God, are: 1. Privative: not trusting him: not seeing the ground of just security in his love and promise: not believing what is seen: not ceasing disquietness and distrustful cares and fears. 2. Positive distrust: supposing the all-sufficiency, goodness, and promises of God, are not sufficient grounds of our expectation and security; and thereupon disquieting our minds with sinful fears, and griefs, and cares, and scheming efforts for ourselves some other way. And this has various degrees: in some it is predominant; in others not. 3. Opposite or adverse: when we trust ourselves, or friends, or wealth, or something else instead of God, either against him, without him, or in coordination with him.

III. The counterfeits of this trust are these: 1. When indeed we trust in our cleverness, or power, or schemes, or friends, or in some means or creatures only, or in coordination with God; but claim and think that we do it but in subordination to him, and that our primary trust is in him alone. The detection of this is by trying how we can trust God alone, when he gives us a promise and no probable means. 2. Claiming to trust God alone in the neglect of those means which he has appointed us to use, and in the neglect of those duties which he has made the condition of his promises; and this trust is but a self-deceiving cover for sin and sloth. 3. Claiming to trust God in the use of self-devised, sinful means; when he has promised a blessing to no such means, but threatened them with a curse. 4. Thinking we trust God, when it is some false revelation of the devil, or some delusion of deceivers, or some dream, or fancy, or brain-sick, proud notion of our own, which indeed we believe, and ground our trust upon: as those do that are deluded by false prophets and false teachers, and fantastical fancies of their corrupted imaginations. 6. When men in presumption and carnal security will rashly venture their souls in the darkness of uncertainty, (as well as in the neglect of a holy life,) and cast away all the sense of their miserable state; and all the necessary fear and care that tended to their recovery; and persuade themselves that they are in no great danger, or that their care will do no good, and call all this a trusting God with their salvation. 7. A claiming to trust God for that which is contrary to his nature: as to love the wicked with complacency, or to take them into heaven. 8. A claiming to trust God for that which is contrary to his word: as to save the unregenerate and unholy; and so "not believing him" itself, is taken for a believing in him, or trusting him. 9. Claiming to believe and trust him for that which neither his nature or his word ever declared to be his will, in matters which he has kept secret, or never gave us any revelation of; such is that which some call a particular faith: as to believe in prayer that some particular never promised shall be granted, because we ask it, or because we feel a strong persuasion that it will be so.

*In the margin: Of particular faith.*

*Question.* But is not such a particular faith and trust divine and solid?--*Answer.* To expect any particular mercy which God's nature, or word, or works tell us that he will give, is sound and warrantable: and to expect any particular thing which by inspiration, prophecy, or true extraordinary revelation shall be made known to us; for this is a word of God: but all other belief and expectation is but self-promising and self-deceiving. And wise men will not easily take themselves for prophets, nor take anything for an inspiration, or divine, extraordinary revelation, which does not bring the testimony of cogent evidence.

IV. There are three great uses and benefits of this trust in God, which highly commend it to us, and make it necessary. 1. It is necessary to our acknowledgment and honoring of God. It is a heartfelt, practical confession of his power, and wisdom, and goodness, and truth: for where any one of these is wanting, there is no ground of rational trust. And the greater the danger or assault against us is, the more God is acknowledged and honored by our trust; for then we declare, that no creature or impediment can disappoint his will: but that his power is above all power, and his wisdom above all wisdom, and his goodness and fidelity constant and invincible. Whereas distrust is a denying of God in some of his attributes, or a suspecting of him. 2. It is necessary to ourselves, for the quiet, and peace, and comfort of our minds, which else will be left unavoidably to continual disquietness and pain, by vexatious fears, and griefs, and cares, unless stupidity or deceit should ease them. 3. It is necessary to prevent the errors and sinful failures of our lives. For if we do not trust in God, we shall spend all our thoughts and labors in the use of sinful means; we shall be trusting idolatrously to the creature, and we shall be scheming for ourselves by lies or any unlawful means, and lose ourselves by saving ourselves, as from God, or without God.

Hence it is, that trust in God is so frequently and earnestly commanded in the Scriptures, and such blessings promised to it, as if it were the sum of godliness and religion. Jeremiah 17:5, 7, "I will put a curse on people who trust in mere human beings, who depend on mere flesh and blood for their strength, and whose hearts have turned away from the Lord. ... My blessing is on those people who trust in me, who put their confidence in me." Proverbs 16:20, "blessed is the one who trusts in the Lord." Psalm 2:12, "How blessed are all who take shelter in him!" So Psalm 84:12; 34:8, "Taste and see that the Lord is good. How blessed is the one who takes shelter in him." See Psalm 32:10; 57:1; 86:2; 22:4, 5, 8. Safety, stability, comfort, salvation, all mercies are promised to them that trust in God, Psalm 34:22; 37:3, 5, 40; 91:2, 4; 125:1; Isaiah 50:10. So faith in Christ is called trust, Matthew 12:21; Ephesians 1:12, 13. And idolaters and worldlings are described by trusting in their idols and their wealth, Psalm 115:8; 135:18; Amos 6:1; Mark 10:24; Proverbs 11:28; 28:26.

**Directions for a quieting and comforting Trust in God.**

**Direction 1. Let your soul retain the deepest impression of the almightiness, wisdom, goodness, and faithfulness of God, and how certainly all persons, things, and events are in his power; and how powerless all the world is to resist him, and that nothing can hurt you but by his consent.**--The principal means for a confirmed confidence in God is to know him, and to know that all things that we can fear are nothing, and can do nothing, but by his command, and motion, or permission. I am not afraid of a bird or a worm, because I know it is too weak for me: and if I rightly apprehend how much all creatures are too weak for God, and how sufficient God is to deliver me, his trust would quiet me. "Don't be afraid, for I am with you! Don't be frightened, for I am your God! I strengthen you--yes, I help you--yes, I uphold you with my victorious right hand!" (Isaiah 41:10). So verses 13, 14; 43:1; 44:2, 8. "Your loyal followers trust in you" (Psalm 9:10). "Listen to me, you who know what is right, you people who are aware of my law. Don't be afraid of the insults of men; don't be discouraged because of their abuse. For a moth will eat away at them like clothes; a clothes moth will devour them like wool" (Isaiah 51:7-8).

**Direction 2. Work for a sound and clear understanding of the promises of God, that you may know how far he calls you to trust in him.**--For to think that he promises what he does not, is not to trust him, but to deceive yourself; and to think that he does not promise what indeed he does, is to cast away the ground of trust.

**Direction 3. Do not yield to the tempter, who would either entice you into terrifying guilt, and blot out your evidences, or else hide them from you, and keep you doubtful and suspicious of the love of God.**--For almost all that the distrustful soul has to say for itself, to justify its distrust, is, I am not sure that the promises are mine. Remember always, that a heart dedicated to God, or consenting to his covenant, is your fullest evidence; and do not allow this to be hidden or blotted out. Willful sin and guiltiness breeds fears, and will interrupt your trust and quiet till it is forsaken.

**Direction 4. Remember the grounds of confidence and quietness which God has given you in his Son, his covenant, his Spirit, his sacraments, and your own and others' manifold experiences.** I name them all together, because I would have you set them all together before your eyes. Will he not give you "all things with him," that has "given you his Son?" (Romans 8:32). Is not Christ a sufficient guarantor and encourager? Are not his covenant, promise, and oath sufficient security for you? "In the same way God wanted to demonstrate more clearly to the heirs of the promise that his purpose was unchangeable, and so he intervened with an oath, so that we who have found refuge in him may find strong encouragement ... through two unchangeable things, since it is impossible for God to lie" (Hebrews 6:17-18). And are not the heavenly seal and earnest of his Spirit sufficient to confirm us? 2 Corinthians 1:22; and 5:5; Ephesians 1:13, 14, and 4:30. And have you tried God so often, and yet cannot you trust him? Our frequent experiences, though the least of all these helps of trust, are very powerful, because they are near us, and almost satisfy sense itself; when all our bones say, "O Lord, who can compare to you? You rescue the oppressed from those who try to overpower them" etc. (Psalm 35:10).

**Direction 5. Consider the greatness of the sin of distrust; how it denies God in his attributes, and usually supposes the creature to be above him.**--Either you doubt of, or deny his power to help you, or his wisdom as deficient in making his promises, or finding out the means of your deliverance, or his goodness and love, as if he would deceive you, and so his truth and faithfulness in his promises. And if you fear a man, how great soever, when God calls you to trust him for your help, what do you but say, This man is more powerful than God? or, God cannot deliver me out of his hands? If it be want, or sickness, or death which you fear, what do you but say in your heart, that God either does not know what is best for you so well as you know yourself, or else is not powerful or gracious enough to give it? nor true enough to keep his promise? "The one who does not believe God has made him a liar" (1 John 5:10-11).

## Translator's notes

- **Direction numbering.** In the source, the "Direct." items inside this Grand Direction restart at I. They are rendered "Direction 1." and so on. The five points announced at the start of the Grand Direction run as 1. (nature), II., III., IV., and the fifth (the directions) begins under the heading "Directions for a quieting and comforting Trust in God." Baxter's own labels "1., II., III., IV." are kept as printed.
- **Counterfeits list.** The source goes from "4." straight to "6." (no 5). We keep Baxter's numbers. The printed text may have lost an item; we cannot tell.
- **Romans 8:32 (Direction 4).** Baxter's quoted words "all things with him" and "given you his Son" are his own King James-style blend and paraphrase, so we keep his words (modernized). The NET reads: "he who did not spare his own Son, but gave him up for us all--how will he not also, along with him, freely give us all things?" The sense Baxter relies on is the same.
- **NET flags where the wording changes Baxter's sense (trust).**
  - **Jeremiah 17:5, 7.** The King James has "Cursed be the man that trusteth in man, and maketh flesh his arm" and "Blessed is the man that trusteth in the Lord, and whose hope the Lord is." The NET has God speaking in the first person ("I will put a curse...", "My blessing is on...") and drops "maketh flesh his arm" and "whose hope the Lord is." Baxter's point (trust placed in people against trust in God) is intact, but his quoted phrase "hope" is not in the NET here. The ellipsis marks the skipped words between verses 5 and 7, as in Baxter.
  - **Proverbs 16:20.** Baxter quotes only the second half of the verse, as in the King James ("Whoso trusteth in the Lord, happy is he"). The NET's first half reads differently ("The one who deals wisely in a matter will find success"). We quote only the second half, which contains the trust Baxter wants.
  - **Psalm 2:12 and Psalm 34:8.** The King James has "put their trust in him" and "trusteth in him." The NET has "take shelter in him" and "takes shelter in him." The sense is close (refuge as a picture of trust), but the word "trust," which Baxter is cataloguing, is not there. Psalm 84:12 in the NET does say "trust."
  - **Psalm 9:10.** Baxter quotes "They that know thy name will put their trust in thee." The NET reads "Your loyal followers trust in you," without "know thy name." Baxter leans on knowing God as the root of trust (his Direction 1); the quoted words "know thy name" are not in the NET, so we quote only the part the NET has.
  - **Isaiah 51:7-8.** Baxter's "the people in whose heart is my law" becomes "you people who are aware of my law" in the NET; "reproach of men... revilings" becomes "insults of men... abuse"; and "the worm shall eat them like wool" becomes "a clothes moth will devour them like wool." The NET's second half of verse 8 ("But the vindication I provide will be permanent...") is not quoted by Baxter and is omitted here.
  - **Hebrews 6:17-18.** Baxter's "immutability of his counsel" is "his purpose was unchangeable" in the NET, "two immutable things" is "two unchangeable things," and "strong consolation" is "strong encouragement." The NET also has "we who have found refuge in him" (not in the King James wording Baxter quotes) and "hold fast to the hope set before us," which is skipped with an ellipsis; Baxter's quotation runs on without it.
  - **Psalm 35:10.** Baxter has "all my bones" saying; the NET has "With all my strength I will say." Baxter's picture of the whole body speaking is lost in the NET wording. We keep Baxter's "all our bones say" in his own sentence and quote the NET only for the words of praise.
  - **1 John 5:10-11.** Baxter paraphrases "He that believeth not, makes God a liar" in the present tense. The NET (verse 10, which it puts in parentheses) reads "the one who does not believe God has made him a liar." Baxter cites verses 10 and 11 but the words are in 10; verse 11 has the "testimony" that God has given us eternal life in his Son.
  - **Matthew 12:21; Ephesians 1:12.** Baxter lists them as showing that faith in Christ is called "trust." The NET has "hope" ("the Gentiles will hope," "set our hope on Christ"). The King James word is "trust." This weakens the proof-text for the word, though not for the idea of faith as reliance on Christ. We have not checked every other verse in his lists.
  - **Isaiah 50:10.** The source prints "Isa. 1. 10," which is probably a misprint for Isaiah 50:10 (Roman numeral L read as 1). Isaiah 1:10 does not speak of trust, whereas Isaiah 50:10 does ("should trust in the name of the Lord"). We use 50:10 and note the correction.
- **Unmarked allusion** (kept in Baxter's wording): "all shall work together for our good" (probably Romans 8:28; the reference is not in Baxter's text).
- **Reference lists** (Psalms, Isaiah, Amos, Mark, Proverbs, 2 Corinthians, Ephesians): converted to modern form. We have not checked each verse in these lists against the NET.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): **befall** to "happen to"; **contains in it a crediting** to "contains a believing"; **school-men** to "scholastic theologians"; **collection(s), collector** (meaning inference) to "inference(s), the one inferring"; **shifts / shifting endeavours** (devices; scheming) to "schemes / scheming efforts"; **wit** to "cleverness"; **reduce** (assign) to "assign"; **ascertaineth** to "assures"; **cordial** (heartfelt) to "heartfelt"; **miscarriage(s)** to "failures"; **undertaker** (one who stands surety) to "guarantor"; **impotent** to "powerless"; **blot** to "blot out"; **wilful** to "willful"; **conceit** to "notion"; **pretending** to "claiming"; **interest in** (right to, share in) to "claim to"; **government and peace** to "direction and peace"; **Labour** to "Work"; **co-ordination** to "coordination."
- **Terms kept:** **earnest** (a first installment or pledge; "the seal and earnest of his Spirit"); **apprehension / apprehend** (grasp, understanding); **affiance** (reliance, trust placed in someone); **complacency** (settled satisfaction or delight in something; "to love the wicked with complacency" means to love them with approval or delight); **stupidity** (numbness of mind, not low intelligence); **carnal security** (careless confidence); **brain-sick**; **evidences** (signs that show one belongs to God); **sense** (the physical senses).
- ***spes roborata*:** Latin as printed. (English, draft: "strengthened hope," which Baxter glosses as "a confirmed hope.") Baxter attributes the term to Aquinas and other scholastic theologians; we have not checked that attribution.
- **Direction 1 ("his trust would quiet me"):** left as Baxter wrote; the sense is that trust in him would quiet me.
- **Question and Answer on "particular faith":** the source has "Quest." and "Answ."; rendered *Question* and *Answer*.
- **Direct. VI** and the sidenote "To trust in God." at its head fall just outside this range (the next file begins there).
- **Peucerus** (note 119): Baxter's own spelling; we have not identified him with certainty. **Dr. Barlow** (note 118): named by Baxter; we say nothing further about him.
