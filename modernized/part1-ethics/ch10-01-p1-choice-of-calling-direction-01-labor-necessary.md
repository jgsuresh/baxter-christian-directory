# Chapter 10: Part 1, Title 1, the right choice of a calling (Direction 1)

**Chapter 10. Directions for the government of the body.**

**Part 1.**

**Directions about our labor and callings.**

**Title 1. Directions for the right choice of our calling and ordinary labor.**

I have already spoken of Christian works, and the duty of our callings, Chapter 3, Grand Direction 10; and am now only to add these few directions, for the right choosing of your callings: for of the using of them I must speak more later.

**Direction 1. Understand how necessary a life of labor is, and the reasons of the necessity.**

*In the margin: Is labor necessary to all?*

*Question 1.* Is labor necessary to all? or to whom, if not to all? *Answer.* It is necessary (as a duty) to all that are able to perform it: but to the unable it is not necessary; as to infants, and sick persons, or deranged persons, that cannot do it, or to prisoners, or any that are restrained or hindered unavoidably by others, or to people that are disabled by age, or by anything that makes it naturally impossible.

*In the margin: What labor is necessary?*

*Question 2.* What labor is it that is necessary? *Answer.* Some labor that shall employ the faculties of the soul and body, and be profitable, as far as may be, to others and ourselves. But the same kind of labor is not necessary for all.

In some labors, the mind is more employed than the body; as in the labors of a magistrate, a minister, a physician, a lawyer, etc.; though some in these may have much bodily labor also.

The labor of some is almost only of the mind: as, 1. Of students in divinity, philosophy, law, medicine, etc. who are but preparing themselves for a calling. 2. Of some ministers, or other godly persons, who by the iniquity of the place or times where they live, may for a season be disabled from appearing among men, and laboring for any except by the mind; being imprisoned, or driven into solitude, or otherwise made incapable. 3. Of men that have some extraordinary necessity for a season, to converse with God and themselves alone; as, men that are near death, and have need to lay aside all other labors to prepare themselves. Though, usually, even they that are near death should labor for the good of others to the last; and in so doing they profit and prepare themselves.

The labor of some others is more of the body than the mind; as, most tradesmen and day-laborers.

And the labor of some is equally of the body and mind; as, some painstaking ministers, and physicians, scribes, and craftsmen of more ingenious professions, as watchmakers, printers, builders, etc.: some of these are fittest for one man, and some for another. [567]

*In the margin: Will religion excuse from labor?*

*Question 3.* May not religion excuse men from all other labor, except prayer and contemplation? [568] *Answer.* Religion is our obligation to obey God. God binds us to do all the good we can to others. Some men that have ability, opportunity, and a call, may be excused by religion from worldly labors, as ministers; but not from such spiritual labors for others which they can perform. He that under pretext of religion withdraws from company, and refrains from doing good to others, and only lives to himself, and his own soul, makes religion a pretext against charity, and the works of charity, which are a great part of religion; for "Pure and undefiled religion before God the Father is this: to care for orphans and widows in their adversity and to keep oneself unstained by the world" (James 1:27). Even when sickness, imprisonment, or persecution disables us from doing any more for others, we must pray for them. But while we can do more, we must.

*In the margin: Will not riches excuse?*

*Question 4.* Will not riches excuse one from laboring in a calling? *Answer.* No; but rather bind them to it the more; for he that has most wages from God, should do him most work. Though they have no outward need to urge them, they have as great a necessity of obeying God, and doing good to others, as any other men have that are poor.

*In the margin: Why labor is necessary.*

*Question 5.* Why is labor thus necessary to all that are able? *Answer.* 1. God has strictly commanded it to all; and his command is reason enough to us: 2 Thessalonians 3:10–12, "For even when we were with you, we used to give you this command: 'If anyone is not willing to work, neither should he eat.' For we hear that some among you are living an undisciplined life, not doing their own work but meddling in the work of others. Now such people we command and urge in the Lord Jesus Christ to work quietly and so provide their own food to eat." See verses 6, 14; 1 Thessalonians 4:11, "But we urge you, brothers and sisters, ... to aspire to lead a quiet life, to attend to your own business, and to work with your own hands, as we commanded you. In this way you will live a decent life (or decently) before outsiders and not be in need." Genesis 3:19, "By the sweat of your brow you will eat food until you return to the ground." And in the fourth commandment, "For six days you may labor." [569] So Ephesians 4:28; Proverbs 31:32, 33.

2. Naturally action is the end of all our powers; and the power would be worthless, except in respect to the act: to be able to understand, to read, to write, to go, etc. would be worth little, if it were not that we may do the things that we are enabled to.

3. It is for action that God maintains us and our abilities: work is the moral as well as the natural end of power. It is the act by the power that is commanded us.

4. It is action that God is most served and honored by: not so much by our being able to do good, but by our doing it. Who will keep a servant that is able to work and will not? Will his mere ability answer your expectation?

5. The public welfare, or the good of many, is to be valued above our own. Every man therefore is bound to do all the good he can to others, especially for the church and commonwealth. And this is not done by idleness, but by labor. As the bees labor to replenish their hive, so man, being a sociable creature, must labor for the good of the society which he belongs to, in which his own is contained as a part.

6. Labor is necessary for the preservation of the faculties of the mind. (1.) The labor of the mind is necessary for this, because unexercised abilities will decay; as iron not used will be consumed with rust. Idleness makes men fools and dullards, and spoils that little ability which they have. (2.) And the exercise of the body is ordinarily necessary, because of the mind's dependence on the body, and acting according to its temperament and disposition: it is exceedingly helped or hindered by the body.

7. Labor is needful to our health and life: the body itself will quickly fall into mortal diseases without it (except in some very few persons of extraordinary soundness). [570] Next to abstinence, labor is the chief preserver of health. It stirs up the natural heat and spirits, which perform the chief functions for the life of man: it is the proper bellows for this vital fire: it helps all the digestions of nature: it thins that which is too thick: it purifies that which begins to corrupt: it opens obstructions: it keeps the mass of blood and other nutritious humors in their proper temperament, fit for motion, circulation, and nutrition; it helps them all in the discharge of their natural functions: it helps the parts to attract each one its proper nourishment, and promotes every fermentation and assimilation by which nature maintains the transitory, ever-consuming oil and mass: it excels art in the preparation, alteration, and expulsion of all the waste matter, which being retained would be the matter of manifold diseases; and powerfully fights against all the enemies of health. In a word, it incomparably excels the help of the most skillful physicians and excellent medicines in the world, for the preventing of most diseases incident to man: (and consequently to the benefit of the soul itself, which cheerfully uses a cheerful and well-tempered body; and uses a languishing, sickly body, as the rider uses a tired horse, or as we use a sick or lazy servant, or a blunted knife, or a clock or watch that is out of order). I speak all this of bodily labor, which is necessary to the body, and consequently to the mind; for lack of which abundance grow melancholy, and abundance grow sluggish and good for nothing, and abundance cherish filthy lusts, and millions yearly turn to earth before their time. For lack of bodily labor, a multitude of the idle gentry, and rich people, and young people that are slothful, heap up in the secret receptacles of the body a dunghill of undigested and waste filth, and corrupt all the mass of humors which should be the fuel and oil of life, and die by thousands of untimely deaths (of fevers, palsies, convulsions, strokes, dropsies, consumptions, gout, etc.) more miserably than if thieves had murdered them by the highway, because it is their own doing, and by their sloth they kill themselves. For lack of bodily exercise and labor interposed, abundance of students and sedentary persons fill themselves with diseases, and hasten their death, and causelessly blame their hard studies for that which was caused by their bodily sloth. The hardest studies will do little harm to most men, if they do but by suitable, interposed bodily labor keep all the humors in their just temperament; when by a sluggish walk now and then, instead of labor and sweat, they defraud themselves. If the world knew but the benefit of temperance and labor to the maintaining of man's health and life, and the harms of excess of food and drink, and idleness, the love of health and life would do that with them, which God's authority will not do.

8. Labor and diligence keep the mind upon a lawful employment, and therefore keep out many dangerous temptations, and keep the thoughts from emptiness and sin; and also keeps out empty words, and preserves the soul from many sins, which a life of idleness and sloth cherishes. It helps even unlearned persons more effectively to restrain their thoughts and words from sin, than the greatest knowledge and diligent watchfulness can do in an idle kind of life.

9. Diligent labor mortifies the flesh, and keeps down its self-indulgent inclinations, and subdues that pride, and lust, and brutish indulgence in physical pleasure which is cherished by an idle life.

10. Lastly, it is God's appointed means for the getting of our daily bread; and as it is a more real honor to get our bread ourselves, than to receive it by the gift of our friends or parents, so is it more comforting to a well-informed mind. We may best believe that we have our food and provisions in mercy, and that they shall be blessed to us, when we have them in God's appointed way; who has said, "If anyone is not willing to work, neither should he eat," 2 Thessalonians 3.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized; Scripture references put in modern form). None of these notes contains Latin or Greek.

[567] See 1 Corinthians 9:6; 2 Corinthians 6:1; 1 Corinthians 16:10; 2 Timothy 2:15.

[568] See before, Chapter 6, Title 4 of this: and in my *Treatise of the Divine Life*, part 3.

[569] Ezekiel 46:1; Deuteronomy 16:15; 2:7; Exodus 34:21.

[570] Socrates was greatly devoted to the exercise of his body, as necessary to the health of body and mind. Laertius. Plutarch, out of Plato, says that soul and body should be equally exercised together, and driven on as two horses in a coach, and not either of them outrun the other. *Precepts of Health*.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Headings:** Chapter 10 ("Directions for the Government of the Body") is the last chapter of Part 1 of the *Directory*, and is divided into Parts; this file begins its Part 1. **Title:** Baxter's "Tit." is short for "Title," meaning a heading or division within a Part. Title 1 is followed later by Titles 2 and 3.
- **calling:** Baxter's word for one's vocation or occupation, the regular work a person is set to. Kept throughout. At the start of Direction 2 (next file) he defines it as "a stated ordinary course of labor."
- **Cross-references not checked:** "Chapter 3, Grand Direction 10" (Baxter's "chap. iii. grand direct. x") in the opening paragraph; in note 568, "Chapter 6, Title 4 of this" (Baxter's "of this," probably meaning this Part of the *Directory*) and his *Treatise of the Divine Life*, part 3. We have not confirmed these.
- **James 1:27:** NET wording. The King James Version, which Baxter quotes, has "to visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction"; the NET's "to care for orphans and widows" keeps his point (religion includes active charity to others).
- **2 Thessalonians 3:10–12:** NET wording. Where the King James has "walk among you disorderly, working not at all, but are busybodies," the NET has "living an undisciplined life, not doing their own work but meddling in the work of others." The NET's "If anyone is not willing to work" matches Baxter's use: the rule is aimed at those who will not work, not those who cannot (compare his Question 1).
- **1 Thessalonians 4:11:** Baxter cites verse 11, but his quotation begins with the end of 4:10 ("We beseech you, brethren") and runs through 4:12; his "--" marks the gap, rendered here as "...". NET wording throughout. Baxter's parenthesis "(or decently)" is his own alternative to the King James "walk honestly"; the NET already reads "live a decent life," so his gloss now repeats the NET. We keep it because it is his.
- **Genesis 3:19:** NET wording, quoted only as far as Baxter quotes. The King James has "In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread."
- **"For six days you may labor"** (the fourth commandment): Baxter gives no reference; the words are in Exodus 20:9 (also Deuteronomy 5:13), and we have used Exodus 20:9 (reference not in Baxter's text). **NET changes the sense here:** the King James "Six days shalt thou labour" reads as a command, and Baxter cites it as one of God's commands to work; the NET's "you may labor" reads as a permission (Deuteronomy 5:13 in the NET, "You are to work and do all your tasks in six days," is closer to Baxter's sense).
- **Proverbs 31:32, 33:** as printed. Proverbs 31 has only 31 verses, so the reference is wrong in the source; we are not sure what Baxter intended (perhaps verses on the diligent wife earlier in the chapter, such as 31:13 or 31:27), so we keep his reference.
- **2 Thessalonians 3** (end of point 10): Baxter's text gives only the chapter; the quoted words are 3:10, and we use the NET wording of that verse as above.
- **Unmarked allusion:** "our daily bread" (point 10) echoes the Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:11).
- **divinity:** Baxter's term for theology, kept. **medicine:** his "physic."
- **deranged persons:** his "distracted persons," meaning those out of their minds.
- **painstaking ministers:** his "painful," meaning taking pains, hard-working. **craftsmen:** his "artificers."
- **withdraws from company:** his "withdraweth from converse," meaning dealings and society with other people. **refrains from doing good:** his "forbeareth to do good." **pretext:** his "pretence." **outward need:** his "outward want." **add:** his "subjoin." **later:** his "anon."
- **the power would be worthless:** his "the power were vain." **to go:** kept, meaning to walk or move about.
- **Point 7 and the medicine of Baxter's day:** this passage rests on the humoral medicine of the seventeenth century (natural heat and "spirits," the humors and their "temperament," the "digestions" or "concoctions" of nature). These are his claims, kept as his, not modern medical teaching. Word swaps: **temperament** (his "temperature" in point 6, his "temperament" in point 7: the balance or constitution of the body and its humors); **functions** (his "offices"); **digestions** (his "concoctions"); **thins that which is too thick** (his "attenuateth that which is too gross"); **nourishment** (his "nutriment"); **ever-consuming** (his "still-consuming," meaning always being used up); **waste** (his "excrementitious"); **undigested** (his "unconcocted"); **corrupt** (his "vitiate"); **strokes** (his "apoplexies"); **suitable** (his "convenient"); **harms** (his "mischiefs"); **food** (his "meat"). **dropsies** (swelling from fluid) and **consumptions** (wasting diseases, often what is now called tuberculosis) are kept. **art** here means human skill, that is, medicine.
- **abundance:** kept in Baxter's sense of "a great many" (people).
- **melancholy:** kept; see GLOSSARY.md.
- **emptiness; empty words** (point 8): his "vanity" and "vain words."
- **mortifies:** kept (to put to death the sinful desires). **flesh:** kept; see GLOSSARY.md. **self-indulgent inclinations:** his "luxurious inclinations"; in his day "luxurious" meant given to excess and lust, not merely costly. **keeps down:** his "keepeth under." **indulgence in physical pleasure:** his "sensuality."
- **comforting:** his "comfortable."
- **Note 570:** "Laert." is probably Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of the Philosophers*, and "Prec. of Health" probably Plutarch's *Precepts of Health* (in the *Moralia*); we have not checked the passages. **greatly devoted to:** his "mightily addicted to." **outrun:** his "overgo."
- **Range end:** this file ends at the close of Direction 1; Direction 2 (the need of a stated calling) begins the next file.
