# Chapter 6: Title 4, Questions 1-4 (the contemplative and the active life)

**Title 4. The difference between a contemplative life, and an obedient, active life, with directions concerning them.**

This task will be best performed by answering those questions which here need a solution.

*In the margin: What is a contemplative life.*

*Question 1.* What is a contemplative life? and what is an active, obediential life?

*Answer.* Every active Christian is bound to something of contemplation; and all contemplative persons are bound to obedience to God, and to so much of action as may answer their abilities and opportunities. But yet some are much more called to the one, and some to the other; and we name it from that which is most eminent and the chief. We call that a contemplative life, when a man's state and calling allows and requires him to make the exercises of his mind on things sublime and holy, and the affecting of his heart with them to be his principal business, which takes up the most of his time. And we call that an active, obediential life, when a man's state and calling requires him to spend the chief part of his time in some external labor or vocation, tending to the good of ourselves and others. As craftsmen, tradesmen, farmers, laborers, physicians, lawyers, pastors and preachers of the gospel, soldiers and magistrates, all live an active life, which should be a life of obedience to God. Though among these, some have much more time for contemplation than others. And some few there are that are exempt from both these, and are called to live a passive, obediential life; that is, such a life in which their obedient bearing of the cross, and patient suffering, and submission to the chastising or trying will of God, is the most eminent and principal service they can do him, above contemplation or action.

*Question 2.* Must every man do his best to cast off all worldly and external labors, and to retire himself to a contemplative life as the most excellent?

*Answer.* No: no man should do so without a special necessity or call; for there are general precepts on all that are able, that we live to the benefit of others, and prefer the common good, and as we have opportunity do good to all men, and love our neighbors as ourselves, and do as we would be done by (which will put us to much action), and that we labor before we eat. [309] And for a man unnecessarily to cast off all the service of his life, in which he may be profitable to others, is a burying or hiding of his master's talents, and a neglect of charity, and a sinning greatly against the law of love. As we have bodies, so they must have their work, as well as our souls.

*In the margin: Who are called to a contemplative life.*

*Question 3.* Is a life of contemplation then lawful to any man? and to whom?

*Answer.* It is lawful, and a duty, and a great mercy to some, to live almost wholly, yes, altogether, in contemplation and prayer, and such holy exercises. And that in these cases following: 1. In case age has disabled a man from being serviceable to others by an active life: and when a man has already spent his days and strength in doing all the good he can; and being now disabled, has special reason to make good use of the rest of his (decrepit) age, in more than ordinary preparations for his death, and in holy communion with God. 2. So also when we are disabled by sickness. 3. And when imprisonment restrains us from an active life, or profiting others. 4. And when persecution forces Christians to retire into solitudes and deserts, to reserve themselves for better times and places; or when prudence tells them that their prayers in solitude may do more good than at that time their martyrdom would be likely to do. 5. When a student is preparing himself for the ministry, or other active life, to which a contemplative life is the way. 6. When poverty, or wars, or the rage of enemies disables a man from all public dealings, and drives him into solitude by unavoidable necessity. 7. When the number of those that are fit for action is so sufficient, and the gifts of the person are so insufficient, and so the need and use of them in an active life so small, that, all things considered, holy, impartial prudence tells him that the good which he could do to others by an active life is not likely to outweigh the losses which he would himself receive, and the good which his very example of a holy and heavenly life might do, and his occasional counsels, and precepts, and resolutions, to those who come to him for advice, being drawn by the esteem of his holy life: in this case, it is lawful to give oneself up to a contemplative life; for that which contributes most to his own good and to others is beyond doubt lawful and a duty. "Anna ... never left the temple, worshiping with fasting and prayer night and day" (Luke 2:36–37). [310] Whether the meaning is that she strictly kept the hours of prayer in the temple, and the fasting twice a week, or frequently, or whether she took up her dwelling in the houses of some of the officers of the temple, devoting herself to the service of the temple; it is plain that either way she did something besides praying and fasting; even as the widows under the gospel who were also to continue "in ... pleas and prayers night and day" (1 Timothy 5:5), and yet were employed in the service of the church, in overseeing the younger, and teaching them to be sober, etc. (Titus 2:4), which is an active life. But however Anna's practice is explained, if this much that I have granted would please the monastics, we would not differ with them.

*Question 4.* How far are those in an active life to use contemplation?

*In the margin: How far contemplation is necessary.*

*Answer.* With very great difference. 1. According to the difference of their callings in the world, and the offices in which they are ordinarily to serve God. 2. And according to the difference of their abilities and fitness for contemplation or for action. 3. According to the difference of their particular opportunities. 4. According to the difference of the necessities of others which may require their help. 5. And of their own necessities of action or contemplation. Which I shall more particularly determine in certain rules.

1. Every Christian must use so much contemplation as is necessary to the loving of God above all, and to the worshiping of him in spirit and in truth, and to a heavenly mind and conduct, and to his due preparation for death and judgment, and to the referring of all his common works to the glory and pleasing of God, that "Holiness to the Lord" may be written upon all, and all that he has may be sanctified, or devoted with himself to God.

2. The calling of a minister of the gospel is so perfectly made up of contemplation and action (though action names it, as being the end and chief), yet he must be excellent in both. If they are not excellent in contemplation, they will not be fit to stand so much nearer to God than the people do; and to sanctify him when they draw near him, and glorify him before all the people: nor will they be fit for the opening of the heavenly mysteries, and working that on the people's hearts which never was on their own. And if they are not excellent in an active life, they will betray the people's souls, and never go through that painstaking diligence, and preaching in season, and out of season, publicly, and from house to house, day and night with tears, which Paul commands them, Acts 20; and the Epistles to Timothy.

3. The work of a magistrate, a lawyer, a physician, and the like, is principally in doing good in their respective callings, which must not be neglected for contemplation. Yet so that all these, and all others, must allow God's service and holy thoughts their due place, in the beginning, and middle, and end of all their actions. As magistrates must read and meditate, day and night, in the word of God, Joshua 1:8, 10. So the eunuch, Cornelius, etc. Acts 8 and 10.

4. Some persons in the same calling, whose callings are not so urgent on them by any necessities of themselves or others, and who may have more free time, must gladly take it for the good of their souls, in the use of contemplation, and other holy duties. And others that are under greater necessities, urgencies, obligations, or cannot be spared from the service of others (as physicians, lawyers, etc.) must be less in contemplation, and prefer the greatest good.

5. Public necessities or service may with some be so great as to dispense with all private duty both of prayer and contemplation (except short silent prayers) for some days together. So in wars it often happens that necessity forbids all set or solemn holy service for many days together (even on the Lord's day). So a physician may sometimes be tied to such close attendance on his patients as will not allow him time for a set prayer. So sometimes a preacher may be so taken up in preaching, and exhorting, and resolving people's weighty doubts, that they will scarcely have time for private duties for some days together (though such happy impediments are rare). In these cases to do the lesser is a sin, when the greater is neglected.

6. Servants, who are not masters of their time, must be faithful in employing it to their masters' service, and take none for holy duty from that part which they should work in; but rather from their rest, so far as they are able; intermixing meditations with their labors when they can: but redeeming such time as is allowed them the more diligently, because their opportunities are so rare and short.

7. The Lord's day (excepting works of necessity), and such free times as do not hinder other work (as when they travel on the way, or work, or wake in the night, etc.), are every man's own time, which he is not to transfer to another's service, but to reserve and use for the service of God, and for his soul, in holy duties.

8. Some persons cannot bear much contemplation, especially melancholy and weak-headed people; and such must serve God so much the more in other duties which they are able to do; and must not tire out and distract themselves with striving to do that which they are not able to undergo. But others feel no harm by it at all, as I can speak by my own experience: my weakness and decay of spirits inclining me most to a dullness of mind, I find that the most stirring serious studies and contemplations, in the greatest solitude, are so far from hurting me by any lessening of health, or cheerfulness, or serenity of mind, that they seem rather a help to all. Those that can thus bear long solitude and contemplation ought to be the more exercised in it, except when greater duties must take precedence. But to melancholy persons it is to be avoided as a hurt.

9. To the same persons, sometimes their own necessities require contemplation most, and sometimes action; and so that which is at one time a duty may at another time be none.

10. A mere sinful reluctance is not to be indulged. A diseased disability (such as comes from melancholy, weak-headedness, or decay of memory) must be endured, and not too much blamed; when Christ excused worse in his disciples, saying, "The spirit is willing, but the flesh is weak." But a sinful reluctance in cases of absolute necessity is not at all to be endured, but striven against with all your power, whatever it costs you: as to bring yourselves to so much serious consideration as is necessary to your repentance and sincere faith, and godly conduct, this must be done whatever follows; though the devil persuades you that it will make you melancholy or mad; for without it, you are far worse than mad.

11. The most desirable life, to those that have their choice, is that which joins together contemplation and action; so that there shall be suitable leisure for the most high and serious contemplation, and this put to use to fit us for the greatest and most profitable action. And such is the life of a faithful minister of Christ: and therefore no sort of men on earth are more obliged to thankfulness than they.

12. Servants, and poor men, and diseased men, and others that are called off from much contemplation, and employed in a life of obedient action, yes, or suffering, by the providence of God, and not by their own sinful choice, must understand that their labor and patience is the way of their acceptable attendance upon God, in the spending of most of their time. And though it is madness in those that hope God will accept their labors instead of true faith and repentance, and a godly life (for these must go together, and do not hinder each other); yet, instead of such further contemplations as are not necessary to the being of a godly life, a true Christian may believe that his obedient labors and sufferings shall be accepted. If you set one servant to add up an account, and another to sweep your chimney or gutters, you will not accept the former and reject the latter for the difference of their works; but you will rather think that he has most merited your acceptance who yielded without grudging to the humblest service. And doubtless it is a heightening of acceptable obedience when we readily and willingly serve God in the lowest, most menial work. He is too fine to serve him who says, I will serve you in the magistracy or ministry, but not at plow or cart, or any such drudgery. [311] And if you are but in God's way, he can make your very obedience a state of greater holiness and safety than if you had spent all that time in the study of holy things, as you see many ungodly ministers do all their lifetime, and are never the better for it. It is not the quality of the work, but God's blessing, that makes it do you good. Nor is he most beloved by God who has rolled over the greatest number of good thoughts in his mind, or of good words in his mouth, no, nor he that has stirred up the strongest passions about these things; but he that loves God and heaven best, and hates sin most, and whose will is most confirmed for holiness of life. He that goes about his labor in obedience to God may have as much comfort as another that is meditating or praying. But neither labor nor prayer is a matter of comfort to an ungodly, carnal heart.

Yes, if decay of memory or natural ability takes you off both action and contemplation, you may have as much acceptance, and solid comfort, in a patient bearing of the cross, and an obedient, cheerful submission to the holy will of God.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Title:** Baxter's "_Tit._" is his abbreviation for "Title," meaning a heading or main division. Chapter 6 has six such Titles; this file is the whole of Title 4.
- **Question numbering:** the source prints "Quest. 1." and then "Quest. II.", "III.", "IV."; we use plain numbers throughout.
- **Luke 2:36–37:** NET wording, with "Anna" put for the NET's "She," since Baxter names her as the subject. The King James has "departed not from the temple, but served God with fastings and prayers night and day"; the NET has "never left the temple, worshiping with fasting and prayer night and day." The sense Baxter discusses (whether she literally stayed in the temple) is the same in both.
- **1 Timothy 5:5:** Baxter's "continue in prayer and supplication night and day" follows the King James ("continueth in supplications and prayers night and day"). We put the NET phrase "pleas and prayers night and day" in the quotation, with "continue" left outside it because the NET verse is singular ("continues in her pleas and prayers").
- **Titus 2:4 (flag):** Baxter's "teaching them to be sober" follows the King James ("teach the young women to be sober"). The NET reads "they will train the younger women to love their husbands," with no word "sober" (it folds that sense into "train"). Baxter is not quoting, so his wording is kept.
- **Joshua 1:8, 10 (corrected reference; flag):** the source prints "John i. 8, 10," which does not fit; the words "meditate ... day and night" are from Joshua 1:8, so we read Joshua. We are unsure what Baxter meant by "10" (Joshua 1:10 is Joshua's command to the officers) and have kept it. The King James says "thou shalt meditate therein day and night"; the NET says "You must memorize it day and night." Baxter's point is about meditation, which the NET wording does not carry.
- **Matthew 26:41:** "The spirit is willing, but the flesh is weak" is the NET wording as well (it is also the King James). Baxter gives no reference; the reference is added here only, not in his text (Mark 14:38 is parallel).
- **"Holiness to the Lord"** (rule 1): Baxter gives no reference. The words are the inscription on the high priest's gold plate (Exodus 28:36, where the NET also reads 'Holiness to the Lord') and on the horses' bells in Zechariah 14:20 (NET "Holy to the Lord"). Reference added here only.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "as we have opportunity do good to all men" (Galatians 6:10, which his note 309 cites); "labor before we eat" (2 Thessalonians 3:10, compare note 309); "love our neighbors as ourselves" (Matthew 22:39); "do as we would be done by" (Matthew 7:12); "burying or hiding his master's talents" (Matthew 25:25); "in spirit and in truth" (John 4:24); "sanctify him when they draw near him, and glorify him before all the people" (Leviticus 10:3, where the NET reads "Among the ones close to me I will show myself holy, and in the presence of all the people I will be honored"); "in season, and out of season" (2 Timothy 4:2, NET "whether it is convenient or not"); "publicly, and from house to house" and "day and night with tears" (Acts 20:20, 31); "the eunuch, Cornelius" (Acts 8:26–39; Acts 10); "redeeming such time" (compare Ephesians 5:16 and Colossians 4:5 in the King James, "redeeming the time"; the NET has "taking advantage of every opportunity" and "making the most of the opportunities").
- **Acts 20; and the Epistles to Timothy:** Baxter's "Acts xx.; and Epist. Tim."
- **obediential:** kept as Baxter's word; it means "of obedience," a life given chiefly to obeying God in outward work.
- **we name it:** Baxter's "we denominate." **names it** (rule 2): his "denominate it." **made up of:** his "mixed of." Rule 2 begins "so ... mixed" and continues "yet he must," a construction that does not quite finish; kept as printed.
- **craftsmen; farmers:** Baxter's "artificers; husbandmen."
- **put us to much action:** his "put us upon much action." **charity:** kept; it means love.
- **make good use of:** his "improve." **put to use** (rule 11): his "improved." **suitable leisure:** his "convenient leisure."
- **public dealings:** his "public converse" (social contact and dealings with others).
- **gifts:** his "parts" (natural abilities). **outweigh:** his "countervail." **esteem:** his "estimation." **contributes most to:** his "maketh most to." **beyond doubt:** his "past doubt."
- **resolutions:** kept; in Baxter's usage, answers given to people's questions and doubts (as in "resolving people's weighty doubts," rule 5).
- **the monastics:** Baxter's term for monks and those who defend the monastic life; his point is that he grants a contemplative life only in the cases listed.
- **painstaking:** his "painful" (taking great pains).
- **respective callings:** his "several callings." **free time:** his "vacant time." **free times:** his "vacancies." **transfer:** his "alienate."
- **private duty; private duties:** his "secret duty; secret duties," meaning private devotion. **short silent prayers:** his "short mental ejaculations" (brief prayers darted up in the mind).
- **weak-headed; weak-headedness:** kept; Baxter means people whose heads (minds) are easily disordered or fatigued by long thinking.
- **melancholy:** kept (see GLOSSARY.md); Baxter's term is not a modern diagnosis.
- **harm:** his "inconvenience." **decay of spirits:** kept; "spirits" here means bodily and mental vigor. **stirring:** his "exciting" (rousing). **lessening:** his "abatement." **cheerfulness:** his "hilarity." **take precedence:** his "take place." **able to do:** his "able for."
- **reluctance:** his "backwardness" (unwillingness, not slowness of development). **blamed:** his "accused." **sincere:** his "unfeigned."
- **add up an account:** his "cast up an account." **gutters:** his "channels." **humblest service:** his "basest service." **lowest, most menial work:** his "lowest, meanest work." **a heightening of:** his "an aggravation of," which here means an increase (not an annoyance). **too fine:** kept; it means too proud or fastidious.
- **about these things:** his "hereabouts." **most beloved by God:** his "most beloved of God."
- **Dr. Hammond** (note 310): probably Henry Hammond, whose *Paraphrase and Annotations* on the New Testament Baxter seems to point to; we have not confirmed the edition or pages.
- **Petrarch, *In Vita Sua*** (note 311): "in his own Life"; probably Petrarch's autobiographical letter, but we have not checked the source. Baxter's note supports his point that some prize freedom from public affairs.
