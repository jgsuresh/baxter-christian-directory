# Chapter 1, Part 1: Direction 17

**Direction 17. That in all this you may be sincere, and not deceived by a hypocritical change, be sure that God is all your confidence, and all your hopes are placed in heaven; and that there is no secret reserve in your hearts for the world and flesh; and that you do not divide your hearts between God and the things below, nor take up with the religion of a hypocrite, which gives God what the flesh can spare.**

When the devil cannot keep you from a change and reformation, he will seek to deceive you with a superficial change and half reformation, which does not go to the root, nor recover the heart to God, nor deliver it entirely to him. If he can by a partial, deceitful change persuade you that you are truly renewed and sanctified, and fix you there that you go no further, you are as surely his as if you had continued in your grosser sins. And of all others, this is the most common and dangerous cheat of souls, when they think to halve it between God and the world, and to secure their fleshly advantage of pleasure and prosperity, and their salvation too; and so they insist on serving God and mammon.

*In the margin: The full description of a false conversion, and of a hypocrite.*

This is the true character of a self-deceiving hypocrite. [36] He is neither so fully persuaded of the certain truth of the Scripture and the life to come, nor yet so mortified to the flesh and the world, as to take the joys of heaven for his whole portion, and to subject all his worldly prosperity and hopes to it, and to part with all things in this world when it is necessary to the securing of his salvation: and therefore he will not lose his hold of present things, nor forsake his worldly interests for Christ, as long as he can keep them. Nor will he be any further religious than is consistent with his bodily welfare; resolving never to be undone by his godliness; but in the first place to save himself, and his prosperity in the world, as long as he can: and therefore he is truly a carnal, worldly-minded man; being named from what is predominant in him. And yet, because he knows that he must die, and for all he knows he may then find, against his will, that there is another life which he must enter upon; lest the gospel should prove true, he must have some religion: and therefore he will take up as much as will be consistent with his temporal welfare, hoping that he may have both that and heaven hereafter; and he will be as religious as the predominant interest of the flesh will give him leave. He is resolved rather to risk his soul than to be here undone: and that is his first principle. But he is resolved to be as godly as will be consistent with a worldly, fleshly life: that is his second principle. And he will hope for heaven as the end of such a way as this: that is his third. Therefore he will place most of his religion in those things which are most consistent with worldliness and carnality, and will not cost his flesh too dearly; as in being of this or that opinion, church, or party (whether papist, Protestant, or some smaller party), in adhering to that party and being zealous for them, in acquiring and using such talents and gifts as may make him highly esteemed by others; and in doing such good works as cost him not too dearly; and in refraining from such sins as would procure his disgrace and shame, and cost his flesh more to commit them than to refrain from them; and such other things as his flesh can spare: this is his fourth principle. And he is resolved, when trial calls him to part with God and his conscience, or with the world, that he will rather let go God and conscience, and venture upon the pains hereafter, which he thinks to be uncertain, than run upon a certain calamity or ruin here; at least, he has no resolution to the contrary which will carry him through in a day of trial: this is his fifth principle. And his sixth principle is, That yet he will not torment himself, or blot his name, with confessing himself a time-serving worldling, resolved to turn any way to save himself. And therefore he will be sure to believe nothing to be truth and duty that is dangerous; but will furnish himself with arguments to prove that it is not the will of God; and that sin is no sin: indeed, perhaps, conscience and duty shall be pleaded for his sin: it shall be out of tenderness, and piety, and charity to others that he will sin; and will charge them to be the sinners who do not comply, and do not act wickedly as well as he. He will be one that shall first make a controversy of every sin which his flesh calls necessary, and of every duty which his flesh counts intolerably costly; and then, when it is a controversy, and many reputed wise, and some reputed good, are on his side, he thinks he is on equal terms with the most honest and sincere: he has got a burrow for his conscience and his reputation: he will not believe himself to be a hypocrite, and no one else must think him one, lest they be uncharitable; for then the censure must fall on the whole party; and then it is sufficient to defend his reputation of piety to say, Though we differ in opinion, we must not differ in affection, and must not condemn each other for such differences (a very great truth where rightly applied). But what is it, O hypocrite, that makes you differ in cases where your flesh has a stake, rather than in any other? and why were you never of that mind until now that your worldly interest requires it? and how does it come to pass that you are always on the self-saving opinion? and where does it come from that you consult only with those that are of the opinion which you desire should be true, and either not at all, or partially and slightly, with those that are against it? Were you ever conscious to yourself that you have counted what it might cost you to be saved, and reckoned on the worst, and resolved in the strength of grace to go through all? Did you ever engage in much of the self-denying part of religion, or any duties that would cost you dearly? May not your conscience tell you that you never did believe that you should suffer much for your religion; that is, you had a secret purpose to avoid it?

O sirs! take warning from the mouth of Christ, who has so often and plainly warned you of this sin and danger! and told you how necessary self-denial and a suffering disposition are to all that are his disciples; and that the worldly, fleshly principle predominant in the hypocrite is manifest by his self-saving course: he must take up his cross, and follow him in a conformity to his sufferings, who will indeed be his disciple. We must suffer with him if we will reign with him (Romans 8:17–18). "The seed sown on rocky ground is the person who hears the word and immediately receives it with joy. But he has no root in himself and does not endure; when trouble or persecution comes because of the word, immediately he falls away. The seed sown among thorns is the person who hears the word, but worldly cares and the seductiveness of wealth choke the word, so it produces nothing" (Matthew 13:20–22). If you have not taken heaven for your part, and are not resolved to let go all that would keep you from it, I must say to your conscience, as Christ to one of your predecessors, "One thing you still lack" (Luke 18:22), and such a one as you will find of flat necessity to your salvation. And it is likely some trying time, even in this life, will expose your hypocrisy, and make you, as it did him, "become very sad" for your riches' sake (verse 23). If godliness with contentment does not seem sufficient gain to you, you will make your gain go instead of godliness; that is, your gain shall be next to your heart, and have the precedence which godliness should have, and your gain shall choose your religion for you, and overrule your conscience, and sway your life.

O sirs! take warning by the apostates, and time-serving hypocrites, who have looked behind them, and, with Demas, have forsaken their duty for the world, and are set up by justice as pillars of salt, for your warning and remembrance. And if ever you would make sure work in turning to God, and escape the too-late repentance of the hypocrite, see that you go to the root, and resign the world to the will of God; and reckon what it may cost you to be followers of Christ; and look for no portion but the favor of God and eternal life; and see that there is no secret reserve in your hearts for your worldly interest or prosperity; and do not think of halving it between God and the world, nor making your religion compliant with the desires and interests of the flesh. Take God as enough for you, indeed, as all, or else you do not take him as your God.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source, except that Baxter's English is modernized.

[36] Whereas there are two great and grievous sorts of trouble raised, one in the churches at the trial of members, and another in men's consciences in examining their states, about this question, How to know true conversion or sanctification? I must tell them in both these troubles, plainly, that Christianity is but one thing, the same in all ages, which is their consent to the baptismal covenant: and there is no such way to resolve this question as to write or set before you the covenant of baptism in its proper sense, and then ask your hearts whether you sincerely and resolutely consent. He that consents truly is converted and justified; and he that professes consent is to be received into the church by baptism (if his parents' consent did not bring him in before, which he must nevertheless do himself when of age).

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Matthew 13:20–22:** NET wording. Baxter's "stony places" is "rocky ground," and "the care of this world, and the deceitfulness of riches" is "worldly cares and the seductiveness of wealth."
- **Luke 18:22–23:** Baxter's "Yet lackest thou one thing" is "One thing you still lack." Baxter's "go away sorrowful" for verse 23 is "became very sad" in the NET; we quote the NET's "become very sad" in the sentence.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording, modernized): "serve God and mammon" (Matthew 6:24); "take up his cross" (Matthew 16:24); "godliness with contentment" (1 Timothy 6:6); "pillars of salt" and "looked behind them" (Genesis 19:26); "Demas, for the world forsaken" (2 Timothy 4:10).
- **interest(s)** (throughout): kept in its older sense of stake, vested advantage, or what one stands to gain or lose, as in "his worldly interests." Baxter's "predominant interest of the flesh" means the controlling stake of his bodily desires.
- **principle** (the six "principles" of the hypocrite): Baxter's word for a guiding rule or governing assumption.
- **time-serving:** Baxter's "temporizing," meaning adapting one's beliefs to what is convenient. **papist:** kept; it is Baxter's usual (and unfriendly) word for a Roman Catholic.
- **mortified:** kept in its theological sense (put to death). **named from:** Baxter's "denominated from," meaning called by what is dominant in him.
- **talents and gifts:** Baxter's "parts and gifts," where "parts" meant natural abilities. **reputation:** his "credit." **intolerably costly:** his "intolerably dear."
- **engage in:** Baxter's "meddle with." **advantage:** his "interest of pleasure." **insist on serving:** his "will needs serve." **risk:** his "venture."
- **burrow:** left as written; a hiding place.
- **"of all others"** (paragraph 1): the sense is "of all other deceits." Left in modern form as "of all others."
- **margin heading** (paragraph 2): Baxter's note that the next paragraph is a full description of a false conversion, with six marks, which he calls his "principles." They are listed in order in the paragraph.
- **Note [36]:** about the two kinds of trouble Baxter sees, over how churches should test applicants for membership and how individuals should test themselves. His answer in both cases is the baptismal covenant.
