# Chapter 1, Part 1: Direction 7

**Direction 7. If you would be converted and saved, do not be a malicious or peevish enemy to those that would convert and save you: do not be angry with them that tell you of your sin or duty, as if they did you wrong or hurt.**

God works by instruments: when he intends to convert a Cornelius, a Peter must be sent for, and willingly heard. When he intends to recall and save a sinner, he has usually some public minister or private friend who shall be a messenger of that searching and convincing truth which is fit to awaken them, enlighten them, and recover them. If God furnishes these his instruments with compassion for your souls, and willingness to instruct you, and you will take them for your enemies, and peevishly quarrel with them, and contradict them, and perhaps reproach them, and do them harm for their goodwill, what an inhuman, barbarous course of ingratitude is this! Will you be angry with men for endeavoring to save you from the fire of hell? Do they endeavor to make any gain or advantage by you? or only to help your souls to heaven? Indeed, if their endeavors did serve any ambitious design of their own, to bring the world (as the pope and his clergy would do) under their own jurisdiction, you would have had reason then to suspect their fraud. But the truth is, Christ has purposely appointed his greatest church officers to be but ministers, even the servants of all, to rule and save men as volunteers, without any coercive power, by the management of his powerful word upon their consciences; and to plead with and entreat the poorest of the flock, as those that are not lords over God's heritage, nor masters of their faith, but their servants in Christ, and helpers of their joy; so that whenever we deliver our message to them, they may see that we do not exercise dominion over them, and aim at no worldly honors, or gain, or advantage to ourselves, but at the mere conversion and saving of their souls. Whereas, if he had allowed us to exercise authority as the kings of the Gentiles, and to be called gracious lords, and to encumber ourselves with the affairs of this life, our doctrine would have been rejected by the majority of the world, and we should always have come to them on this great disadvantage, that they would have thought we sought not them, but theirs; and that we preached not for them, but for ourselves, to make a prize of them. [25] as the Jesuits, when they attempt the conversion of the Indians, still find this their great impediment, the princes and people suppose them to put forward the gospel only as a means to subjugate them and their dominions to the pope; because they tell them that they must all be subject to the pope if they will be saved. Now when Christ has appointed a poor, self-denying, entreating ministry, against whom you can have none of these pretexts, to stoop to your feet, with the most submissive entreaties, that you would but turn to God and live, you have no excuse for your own barbarous ingratitude, if you will fly in their faces, and treat them as your enemies, and be offended with them for endeavoring to save you. You know they can hold their tithes and livings by flattering, and cold, and general preaching, as well as by more faithful dealing (if not better): you know they can get no worldly advantage by dealing so plainly with you: you know that they risk their reputation by it with such as you; and they cannot be ignorant that it is likely to expose them to your ill will and indignation.

And they are men as well as you, and therefore, undoubtedly, desire the goodwill and the good word of others, and take no pleasure in being scorned or hated: undoubtedly they break through many temptations and reluctance of the flesh before they can so far deny themselves as to work for your salvation on such terms: and seeing it is all for you, it seems to me you should be their chief encouragers; if others should oppose them, you should be for them, because they are for you. If I go with a convoy to relieve a besieged garrison, I shall expect opposition from the enemy that besieges them; but if the besieged themselves shoot at us, and treat us as enemies for risking our lives to relieve them, it is time to be gone, and let them take what they get by it.

Perhaps you think that the preacher, or private admonisher, is too plain with you; [26] but you should consider that self-love is likely to make you partial in your own cause, and therefore a more incapable judge than they. And you should consider that God has commanded them to deal plainly, and told them that otherwise the people's blood shall be required at their hands (Isaiah 58:1; Ezekiel 18). And that God best knows what medicine and diet is fittest for your disease; and that the case is of such grand importance (whether you shall live in heaven or hell forever?) that it is scarcely possible for a minister to be too plain and serious with you: and that your disease is so obstinate that gentler means have been too long frustrated, and therefore sharper must be tried; else why were you not converted by gentler dealing until now? If you fall down in a faint, or are about to be drowned, you will give leave to the bystanders to handle you a little more roughly than at another time, and will not bring your lawsuit against them for laying hands on you, or ruffling your silks or finery; if your house is on fire, you will give men leave to speak in a different manner than when they modulate their voices into a civil and complimenting tone.

It may be you think that they are censorious in judging you to be unconverted when you are not; and to be worse and in more danger than you are, and speaking harder of you than you deserve. But it is you that should be most suspicious of yourselves, and afraid, in so great a matter, of being deceived. A bystander may see more than a player: I am sure he that is awake may know more of you than you of yourselves when you are asleep.

But suppose it were as you imagine; it is his love that mistakenly attempts your good: he intends you no harm: it is your salvation that he desires; it is your damnation that he would prevent. You have cause to love him, and be thankful for his goodwill, and not to be angry with him, and reproach him for his mistakes. He is none of those that bring you into the Inquisition, and would fine, or imprison, or banish, or burn, or hang, or torment you in order to convert and save you: the worst he does is but to speak those words, which, if true, you are deeply concerned to regard; and if mistaken, can do you no hurt unless you are the cause yourself. If it is in public preaching, he speaks generally by descriptions, and not by naming names; no more of you than of others in your case; nor of you at all if you are not in that case. If he speaks privately to you, there is no witness but yourself; and therefore it is no matter of disgrace. Never, for shame, claim that you are willing to be converted and saved, when you hate those that would promote it; and are angry with everyone that tells you of your case, and could find it in your heart to stop their mouths, or do them harm.

Reproduced exactly as in the source (the Scripture references are given in modern form). A draft English translation follows the Latin passage in parentheses; it was prepared for this project and is not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check it before relying on fine points.

## Translator's notes

- **No Scripture is quoted in quotation marks in this section.** Baxter alludes to several passages: Cornelius and Peter (Acts 10); "lords over God's heritage" and "masters of their faith" (1 Peter 5:3; 2 Corinthians 1:24, where Paul says he is a "fellow worker" for their joy, the King James "helpers of your joy"); "the kings of the Gentiles" who "are called gracious lords" (Luke 22:25, where the NET reads "those in authority over them are called 'benefactors'"). Baxter lists his Scripture sources in note [25]. Scripture quotations elsewhere are from the NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Isaiah 58:1; Ezekiel 18** (paragraph 3): reproduced as printed. The watchman passage about the people's blood being required at the preacher's hand is Ezekiel 3:18–21 and 33:1–9; chapter 18 is about individual responsibility. We have not changed Baxter's reference, but it may be a misprint.
- **lowercase "as the Jesuits"** (paragraph 1): the 1846 text prints a period before note [25] and continues with a lowercase "as." The sentence is unfinished in the source. Left as printed. The point: just as the Jesuits find that rulers suspect them of serving the pope, people would suspect all ministers of seeking power and gain if they ruled over people.
- **livings** (paragraph 1): paid church positions. **tithes:** the church tax. **flattering:** Baxter's "smoothing."
- **pretexts:** Baxter's "pretences," meaning false grounds.
- **claim:** Baxter's "pretend" (last paragraph). **harm:** his "mischief." **goodwill:** his "good will."
- **bravery** (paragraph 3): Baxter's word for fine clothes, rendered "finery." **swoon:** "faint." **action:** "lawsuit."
- **the Inquisition; the pope and his clergy; the Jesuits; the Indians:** all as Baxter wrote. He contrasts Protestant ministers, who persuade, with Rome's clergy and the Inquisition, who he says compel. This is his polemic, kept as written. "The Indians" are the native peoples of the Americas, as in the note to Direction 5.
- **instruments** (paragraph 1): means, tools. **recover:** Baxter's word for bringing back to health.
