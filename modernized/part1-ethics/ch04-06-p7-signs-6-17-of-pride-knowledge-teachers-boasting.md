# Chapter 4: Signs of Pride (Signs 6-17): Knowledge, Teachers, Precedence, Contention, and Boasting

*This file continues Direction 2 ("Observe the motions and discoveries of pride"), in Baxter's list of signs of pride in common interaction, from Sign 6 to Sign 17. The numbering is Baxter's own and restarts partway through the Direction. The next file continues.*

**Sign 6.** A proud man is apt to overvalue his own knowledge, and to be much unacquainted with his ignorance: he is much more aware of what he knows, than of how much he is lacking in what he ought to know: he thinks himself fit to contradict the ablest divine, when he has scarcely so much knowledge as will save his soul. [220] If he has but some smattering to enable him to talk confidently of what he does not understand, he thinks himself fittest for the chair; and is elevated to a pugnacious courage, and thinks he is able to dispute with any man, and constantly gives himself the victory. If it is a woman who has gathered up a few recipes, she thinks herself fit to be a physician, and risk the lives of dearest friends upon her ignorant skillfulness; when seven years' study more is necessary to make such novices know how little they know, and how much is utterly unknown to them, and seven years more to give them an encouraging taste of knowledge: yet pride makes them doctors in divinity and medicine by its mandamus, without so much fuss; and as they commenced, so they practice, in the dark: and to save the labor of so long studies, can spare, and gravely deride, that knowledge, which they cannot get at cheaper rates. And no wonder, when it is the nature of pride and ignorance to cause the birth and increase of each other. It would be a wonder for an ignorant person to be humble; and when he does not know what abundance of excellent truths are still unknown to him, nor what difficulties there are in every controversy which he never saw. How many studious, learned, holy divines would go many thousand miles (if that would serve) to be well resolved of many doubts in the mysteries of providence, decrees, redemption, grace, free will, and many the like, and that after twenty or forty years' study: when I can take them a boy or a woman in the streets, who can confidently determine them all in a few words, and pity the ignorance or error of such divines, and shake the head at their blindness, and say, God has revealed them to themselves who are babes! yes, and perhaps their confidence takes dissenters for such heretical, erroneous, intolerable persons, that they look upon them as heathens and publicans, and either with the papists reproach and persecute them, or with the lesser sects divide from them, as from men who do not receive the truth: and thus pride makes as many churches as there are different opinions.

**Sign 7.** Pride makes men wonderfully partial in judging of their own virtues and vices in comparison of other men's. When the humble are complaining of their weaknesses and sinfulness, and have much ado to believe that they are anything, or to discern the sincerity of their grace; and think their prayers are as no prayers, and their duties so bad that God will not regard them; the proud think well of all they do, and are little troubled at their greater lacks. They easily see another man's failings; but the very same, or worse, they justify in themselves. Their own passions, their own overreachings or injurious dealings, their own ill words, are smoothed over as harmless things, when other men's are aggravated as intolerable crimes. Another is judged by them unfit for human societies, for less than that which they cannot endure to be themselves reproved for, and will hardly be convinced that it is any fault: so blind is pride about themselves.

**Sign 8.** Pride makes men hear their teachers as judges, when they should hear them as learners and disciples of Christ: they come not to be taught what they did not know, but to censure what they hear, and as confidently pass their judgment on it, as if their teachers lacked nothing but their instructions to teach them aright. I know that no poison is to be taken into the soul on the pretense of any man's authority, and that we must prove all things, and hold fast that which is good: but yet I know that you must be taught even to do this; and that the pastor's office is appointed by Christ as necessary to your good; and that the students who are still quarreling with their teachers, and readier to teach their masters than to learn from them, and boldly contradicting what they never understood, are too proud to become wise; and that humility and reason teach men to learn with a sense of their ignorance, and the necessity of a teacher.

**Sign 9.** A proud man is always hard to be pleased, because he has too great expectations from others: he looks for so much observance and respect, and to be humored and honored by all, that it is too hard a task for any man to please him who has much to do with him, and has any other trade to follow; he who will please him, must either have little to do with him, and come but seldom in his way, or else he must study the art of man-pleasing, compliment, and flattery, until he is ready to commence doctor in it, and must make it his trade and business, as nurses do to tend the sick, or quiet children. One look, or word, or action, will every day fall cross, and some respect or compliment will be lacking. And, as godly, humble men justly aggravate their sins from the greatness and excellency of God whom they offend; so the proud man foolishly aggravates every little wrong that is done him, and every word that is said against him, and every supposed omission or neglect of him, by the high estimation he has of himself against whom it is done.

**Sign 10.** The proud are desirous of precedence among men: to be saluted with the first, and taken by great ones into the greatest favor; and to be set in the highest place, at table, and at church; and to take the better hand. He resents those who are set above him and preferred before him, unless they are much his superiors: or, if he has the wit to avoid the disgrace of contending for such trifles, and showing the childishness of his pride to others, yet he retains a displeasure at the heart: when the humble give precedence to others, and set themselves at the lower end (Luke 14:9-10).

**Sign 11.** A proud man expects that all the good that he does be remembered, and that others do keep a register of his good works, and take notice of his learning, worth, and virtues: as their own memories are stronger here than in anything, so they think other men's should be; as if (being conscious how unfit they are for the esteem of God) they thought all were lost which is not observed and esteemed by men. As their eye is upon themselves, so they think the eye of others should be also; and that as their own, to admire the good, and not to see infirmities and evil. [221]

**Sign 12.** No man is taken for so great a friend to the proud as their admirers; whatever else they are, they love those men best, who most highly esteem them: the faults of such they can play down and easily forgive. Let them be drunkards, or whoremongers, or swearers, or otherwise ungodly, the proud man loves them according to the measure of their honoring him. If you would have his favor, let him hear that you have magnified him behind his back, and that you honor him above all other men. But if the holiest servant of God thinks meanly of him, and speaks of him but as he is; especially if he thinks they are disesteemers of him, or are against his interest and honor, all their wisdom and holiness will not reconcile him to them, if they were as wise or good as Peter or Paul. It signifies nothing to him that they are honorers of God, if he thinks they are not honorers of him. No, he will not believe or acknowledge their goodness, but take all for hypocrisy, if they do not suit his interest or honor: and all because he is an idol to himself.

**Sign 13.** A proud man is apt to domineer with insolence when he gets any advantage, and perceives himself on the higher ground. He says as Pilate to those who are in his power, "Don't you know I have the authority to release you and to crucify you?" forgetting that they "have no power at all against any, but what is given them from above" (John 19:10-11). Victories and successes lift up fools, and make them look big and forget themselves, as if their shadows were longer than before. Servants got on horseback will speak disdainfully of princes who are on foot. [222] David says, "Arrogant people do nothing but scoff at me" (Psalm 119:51). If they get into places of power by promotion they cannot bear it, but are puffed up and intoxicated as if they were not the same men they were. They deal worse with their inferiors if they do not humor them, than Balaam with his ass; when they have made them speak, their insolence cannot bear it: whereas the humble remembers how far he is equal with the lowest, and deals gently with his servants themselves, "remembering that he also has a Master in heaven" (Colossians 4:1-2; Ephesians 6:9).

**Sign 14.** A proud man is impatient of being contradicted in his speech; be it right or wrong you must say as he does, or not contradict him. Hence it is that gallants think that a man's life is little enough to atone for the wrong, if a man presumes to say, they lie. I know that children, and servants, and other inferiors must not be irreverent or immodest, in an unnecessary contradicting of the words of their superiors, but must silently give place when they cannot assent to what is said; but yet an impatience of sober and reasonable contradiction, even from an inferior or servant, is not a sign of a humble mind.

**Sign 15.** Wherever a proud man dwells, he is turbulent and impatient if he does not have his will. If he is a public person, he will set a kingdom all on fire, if things may not go as he would have them. Among the crimes of the last and perilous times, Paul numbers these; to be "lovers of themselves, ... boastful, arrogant, ... treacherous, reckless, conceited" (2 Timothy 3:2-4). If they have to do in church affairs, they will have their will and way, or they will cast all into confusion, and hinder the gospel, and turn the churches upside down. In towns and corporations they are headstrong and turbulent to have their wills. In families there shall be no peace, if everything may not go their way. They cannot yield to the judgment of another.

**Sign 16.** Proud men are hot-tempered and contentious, and cannot put up with injuries or foul words; when a humble man "gives place to wrath," and "avenges not himself," nor "resists evil;" but is meek and patient, "forbearing and forgiving," and so heaping coals of fire on his enemies' heads. [223] "With pride comes only contention" (Proverbs 13:10). "The greedy person stirs up dissension" (Proverbs 28:25). What is their wrath, their scorns, their railing and effort to vilify those who have offended them, but the foam and vomit of their pride? "A proud and arrogant person, whose name is 'Scoffer,' acts with overbearing pride" (Proverbs 21:24).

**Sign 17.** A proud man is either an open or a secret boaster. If he is ashamed to show his pride by open boasting, then he learns the skill of setting out himself, and making known his excellencies in a more concealed and more attractive way. His own commendations shall not seem the design of his speech, but to come in incidentally, or before he was aware, as if he thought of something else: or it shall seem necessary to some other end, and a thing that he is unavoidably put upon, as against his will: or he will take upon him to conceal it, but by a transparent veil, as some proud women hide their beauties: or he will join the mention of some of his infirmities, but they shall be such as he thinks no matter of disgrace, but like proud women's beauty spots, to set out the better part which they are proud of. [224] But one way or other, either by ostentation or insinuation, his work is to make known all that tends to his honor, and to see that his goodness, and wisdom, and greatness are not unknown or unobserved; and all because he must have men's approval, the hypocrite's reward: he is as buried if he is unknown. "Arrogant" and "boastful" are joined together (Romans 1:30; 2 Timothy 3:2). "Theudas" the deceiver "boasted himself to be somebody" (Acts 5:36). "Simon Magus, claiming to be someone great. All the people, from the least to the greatest, paid close attention to him, saying, 'This man is the power of God that is called "Great."'" (Acts 8:9-10). Such "loved praise from men more than praise from God" (John 12:43). But the humble has learned another kind of language; not affectedly, but from the feeling of his heart, to cry out, I am vile; I am unworthy to be called a child; my sins are more than the hairs of my head. And he hates their emptiness who by ill-timed or immoderate commendations, make an effort to stir him up to pride, and so to bring him to be vile indeed, by proclaiming him to be excellent. Much more does he abhor to praise himself, having learned, "Let another praise you, and not your own mouth; someone else, and not your own lips" (Proverbs 27:2). He praises himself by works, and not by words (Proverbs 31:31).

(English: In sharing his resources and conferring benefits he was ready and easy beyond what can be said; most averse to hunting for a little glory from a kindness, which he preferred to keep hidden: visiting Ctesibius, who was sick, when he saw that he was in want, he secretly put a purse of money under his pillow; when Ctesibius found it, he said, "This is Arcesilaus's joke." Hesychius Illustrius, as Baxter cites him.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). All NET wording was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Labels:** Baxter's "Sign VI." to "Sign XVII." are rendered "Sign 6." to "Sign 17." as in the preceding file.
- **Proverbs 28:25 (Sign 16), sense changes:** Baxter's King James reads "He that is of a proud heart stirreth up strife." The NET reads "The greedy person stirs up dissension." Baxter uses the verse as proof that pride causes strife; the NET names greed, not pride, so the verse no longer supports his point as plainly. We print the NET as the convention requires.
- **Proverbs 21:24 (Sign 16):** Baxter's "that deals in proud wrath" is the King James reading; the NET ends "acts with overbearing pride," so the word "wrath" Baxter draws on is not in the NET. His line ("Proud, haughty scorner is his name") otherwise matches the NET sense ("Scoffer"). The NET's inner quotation marks are shown as single marks.
- **Psalm 119:51 (Sign 13):** King James "The proud have had me in derision"; NET "Arrogant people do nothing but scoff at me." Same sense for Baxter's use; "proud" becomes "arrogant."
- **John 19:10-11 (Sign 13):** The first quotation uses the NET. The NET puts "release" before "crucify" (Baxter has "crucify thee, and ... release thee"), and has "authority" where the King James has "power." The second quotation ("have no power at all against any, but what is given them from above") is Baxter's own adaptation of Jesus' words to Pilate, put into the third person; we keep his words, modernized. NET: "You would have no authority over me at all, unless it was given to you from above."
- **Colossians 4:1-2; Ephesians 6:9 (Sign 13):** "remembering that he also has a Master in heaven" is Baxter's paraphrase in the third person; we keep it, modernized. NET Colossians 4:1: "because you know that you also have a master in heaven." The words quoted are in 4:1 (verse 2 is not about masters). The NET renders the King James "servants" as "slaves"; Baxter's "servants" (a household servant) is kept in his own sentence.
- **2 Timothy 3:2-4 (Sign 15):** NET, with "..." for the words Baxter skips (the NET has "lovers of money" after "lovers of themselves," and several items between "arrogant" and "treacherous"). Baxter's "boasters, proud, traitors, heady, high-minded" become "boastful, arrogant, ... treacherous, reckless, conceited." The word "heady" (King James, rash or headstrong) becomes "reckless." "the last and perilous times" is Baxter's phrase.
- **Sign 16 quotations (Romans 12:19; Matthew 5:39; Colossians 3:13):** "gives place to wrath," "avenges not himself," "resists evil," and "forbearing and forgiving" are Baxter's third-person adaptations of King James phrases; we keep them, modernized (his wording "forbearing and forgiving" is quoted, and the NET has "bearing with one another and forgiving one another"). Sense flags: NET Romans 12:19 has "give place to God's wrath" (the King James and Baxter have only "wrath," leaving open whose); NET Matthew 5:39 has "do not resist the evildoer" (not "evil"); NET Romans 12:20 has "burning coals" on the head where Baxter writes "coals of fire" (his own wording, unquoted).
- **Romans 1:30; 2 Timothy 3:2 (Sign 17):** Baxter's quoted "Proud" and "boasters" are the King James words; the NET has "arrogant" and "boastful," which we use in his quotation marks.
- **Acts 5:36 (Sign 17):** Baxter's "boasted himself to be somebody" is his own wording (King James: "boasting himself to be somebody"). NET: "claiming to be somebody." We keep his words, modernized, since the NET's participle does not fit his sentence without adding words.
- **Acts 8:9-10 (Sign 17):** NET, from "claiming" to the end of verse 10. Baxter's "gave out that himself was some great one, and the people all gave heed to him" is replaced by the NET's wording, with "Simon Magus" and a comma before it. The NET has "the power of God that is called 'Great'" where the King James has "the great power of God"; Baxter's argument (the people honored a boaster) is not affected.
- **John 12:43 (Sign 17):** NET "they loved praise from men more than praise from God." Baxter's present tense ("Such love ...") and the word "Such" are his; the NET is past tense, so we print "Such" outside the NET words.
- **Proverbs 27:2 (Sign 17):** NET "someone else" where the King James has "a stranger."
- **Proverbs 31:31 (Sign 17):** Not quoted by Baxter, only cited. In the NET the verse is about the capable wife ("let her works praise her in the city gates"); Baxter applies the principle (praise by works, not words) to anyone.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): "God has revealed them ... who are babes" (compare Matthew 11:25, probably); "heathens and publicans" (compare Matthew 18:17, probably); "prove all things, and hold fast that which is good" (1 Thessalonians 5:21, probably); "Balaam with his ass" (Numbers 22); "the hypocrite's reward" (compare Matthew 6:2, probably); "I am vile" (compare Job 40:4, probably); "I am unworthy to be called a child" (compare Luke 15:19, probably); "my sins are more than the hairs of my head" (compare Psalm 40:12, probably). We have not checked these against the text, and none is marked in Baxter's source.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): receipts, recipes; physic, medicine; ado (in "without so much ado"), fuss; practise, practice; sensible of, aware of; wanting (in "wanting of what"), lacking in; wanted, lacked; wants, lacks; wonderful, wonderfully; scarce, scarcely; gainsay, contradict; insolency, insolence; extenuate, play down; closer (way), more concealed; handsome, more attractive; upon the by, incidentally; approbation, approval; unseasonable, ill-timed; expiate, atone for; preferment, promotion; heady (in "heady and turbulent"), headstrong; upper room, highest place; precedency, precedence; passionate, hot-tempered; vanity ("their vanity"), emptiness; unreverent, irreverent; scholars, students; pretence, pretense; conjoin, join; endeavour(ing), effort. "Passions" in Sign 7 is kept.
- **Terms kept:** *mandamus* (a court's or the Crown's written order; here, pride grants itself degrees by decree); *commenced*, *commence doctor* (take a university degree; Baxter's usage); *divines* (theologians); *humored*, *humor* (indulge); *observance* (attentive deference); *aggravate* (make heavier); *gallants* (fashionable young men of the time); *corporations* (towns' governing bodies); *dissenters* (those outside the established church); *papists* (his word for Roman Catholics, and his polemic against Rome and the lesser sects is kept as he wrote it); *the chair* (the teacher's or preacher's seat); *the better hand* (the place of honor at someone's right side); *whoremongers* (kept as his blunt word); *man-pleasing* (seeking to please others in place of God). "Pugnacious," "novices," "smattering," and "overreachings" are left as written.
- **"he is as buried if he is unknown"** (Sign 17): left as Baxter wrote; the sense is that the proud man feels himself as good as dead if his worth is unknown.
- **Sign 6 and Sign 7, Baxter's remarks on women** (the woman with a few recipes; "a boy or a woman in the streets"): kept as written; they reflect his time.
- **Footnote [220]:** "Lord Bacon, Essay 52" is Baxter's citation; we have not looked up which essay this is.
- **Footnote [221]:** The Latin is printed as in the source, including "crumeunam" (probably a misprint for "crumenam," a purse). "Hesich. Illust." is Baxter's abbreviation, probably for Hesychius Illustrius (we have not confirmed this). The person named is presumably the philosopher Arcesilaus; we have not confirmed. "Cervicali" is rendered "pillow."
- **Footnote [223]:** "2 Peter 2:20" is Baxter's reference as printed; the verse is about escaping the pollutions of the world and does not obviously bear on forbearance. We keep it and have not checked whether it is a misprint.
- **Footnote [224]:** Psalm 49:6 and the other references are kept in modern form as Baxter gave them.
- **Sign 10:** "Luke xiv. 9, 10" is Baxter's reference for the humble choosing the lower place; no words from the verses are quoted.
