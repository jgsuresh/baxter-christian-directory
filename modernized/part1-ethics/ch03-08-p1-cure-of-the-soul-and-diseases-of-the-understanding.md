# Chapter 3: Grand Direction VIII (part 1 of 5)

**Grand Direction 8. Remember that you are related to Christ as the Physician of your souls, and to the Holy Spirit as your Sanctifier. Make it therefore your serious study to be cured by Christ, and cleansed by his Spirit, of all the sinful diseases and defilements of your hearts and lives.**

Though I did before speak of our believing in the Holy Spirit, and using his help for our access to God, and conversation with him; yet I put off speaking fully of the cleansing and mortifying part of his work of sanctification till now; and shall treat of it here, as it is the same as the curing work of Christ, related to us as the Physician of our souls: it being part of our subjection and obedience to him, to be ruled by him, in order to our cure. And what I shall here write against sin, in general, will be of a twofold use. The one is, to help us against the inward corruptions of our hearts, and for the outward obedience of our lives, and so to further the work of sanctification, and prevent our sinning. The other is, to help us to repentance and humiliation, habitual and actual, for the sins which are in us, and which we have already at any time committed.

The general directions for this curing and cleansing of the soul from sin are contained, for the most part, in what has already been said: and many of the particular directions also may be drawn from the sixth direction before this. I shall now add only two general directions, and many more particular ones.

**Direction 1.** The two general directions are these: 1. Know what corruptions the soul of man is naturally defiled with: and this contains the knowledge of those faculties that are the seat of these corruptions, and the knowledge of the corruptions that have tainted and perverted the several faculties.

**Direction 2.** 2. Know what sin is, in its nature or intrinsic evil, as well as in its effects.

*In the margin: How the several faculties of the soul are corrupted and diseased.*

*In the margin: In what cases a sound understanding may be ignorant.*

1. The parts or faculties to be cleansed and cured are both the superior and the inferior. (1.) The understanding, though not the first in the sin, must be first in the cure: for all that is done upon the lower faculties must be by the governing power of the will: and all that is done upon the will, according to the order of human nature, must be done by the understanding. But the understanding has its own diseases, which must be known and cured. Its malady in general is ignorance; which is not only a privation of actual knowledge, but an unreadiness also of the understanding to know the truth. A man may be deprived of some actual knowledge who has no disease in his mind that causes it: as in a case where either the object is absent, and out of reach, or there is no sufficient revelation of it, or the mind is taken up wholly with some other thing, or in case a man shuts out the thoughts of such an object, or refuses the evidence, which is the act of the will, even as a man who is not blind may yet not see a particular object, 1. In case it is out of his natural reach; 2. Or if it is night, and he lacks outside light; 3. Or in case he is wholly taken up with the observation of other things; 4. Or in case he willfully either shuts or turns away his eyes.

*In the margin: How can the understanding be the subject of sin?*

It is a very hard question to resolve, how far and in what respects the diseases of the understanding may be called sin? Because the understanding is not a free, but a necessitated faculty; and there can be no sin where there is no liberty. But to clear this, it must be considered, 1. That it is not this or that faculty that is the full and proper subject of sin, but the man: the fullness of sin being made up of the vice of both faculties, understanding and will, joined together. It is more proper to say, The man sinned, than, The intellect or will sinned, speaking as if the other were excluded. 2. *Liberium arbitrium*, free choice, belongs to the man, and not to his will only, though principally to the will. 3. Though the will only is free in itself, originally, yet the intellect is free by participation, so far as it is commanded by the will, or depends on it for the exercise of its acts. 4. Accordingly, though the understanding, in its original state and of itself, is not the subject of morality, of moral virtues or of moral vices, which are immediately and primarily in the will; yet participatively its virtues and vices are moralized, and become graces or sins, praiseworthy and rewardable, or blameworthy and punishable, as they are commanded by the will, or depend upon it.

Consider then the acts, and habits, and disposition of the understanding; and you will find that some acts, and the privation of them, are necessary, naturally, originally, and unalterably; and these are not virtues or sinful at all, as having no morality. As, to know unwillingly, as the devils do, and to believe when it cannot be resisted, though they would resist; this is no moral virtue at all, but a natural perfection only. So, 1. To be ignorant of that which is no object of knowledge, or which is naturally beyond our knowledge, as of the essence of God, is no sin at all. 2. Nor is it a sin to be ignorant of that which was never revealed, when no fault of ours hindered the revelation. 3. Nor is it a sin to be without the present, actual knowledge or consideration of one point, at that moment when our thoughts are lawfully diverted, as in greater business, or suspended, as in sleep. 4. But to be ignorant willfully is a sin, participatively in the intellect, and originally in the will. 5. And to be ignorant for lack of revelation, when we ourselves are the hinderers of that revelation, or the deserving cause of our lacking it, is our sin: because, though that ignorance is immediately necessary, and hypothetically so, yet originally and remotely it is free and voluntary.

So, as to the habits and disposition of the intellect; it is no sin to lack those which man's understanding in its entire and original nature was without. As, not to be able to know without an object, or to know an unrevealed or too distant object, or actually to know all things knowable at once. But there are defects or ill dispositions that are sinfully contracted; and though these are now immediately natural [98] and necessary, yet being originally and remotely voluntary or free, they are participatively sinful. Such is the natural man's inability or unreadiness to know the things of the Spirit when the word reveals them. This lies not in the lack of a natural faculty to know them, but, 1. Radically in the will. 2. And from there in contrary, false apprehensions with which the intellect is prepossessed, which, resisting the truth, may be called its blindness or powerlessness to know them. And 3. In a strangeness of the mind to those spiritual things with which it is utterly unacquainted.

Note here, 1. That the will may be guilty of the understanding's ignorance in two ways: either by positive aversion, prohibiting or diverting it from beholding the evidence of truth; or by a privation and holding back of that command or stirring-up which is necessary to the exercise of the acts of the understanding. This last is the commonest way of the sin in the understanding; and that may truly be called voluntary which comes from the will's neglect of its office, or suspension of its act, though there is no actual volition or nolition.

2. That the will may do more in causing a disease in the understanding than it can do in curing it. I can put out a man's eyes, but I cannot restore them.

3. That yet for all that, God has so ordered it in his gracious dispensation of the grace of the Redeemer that certain means are appointed by him for man to use, in order to obtaining his grace, for his own recovery: and so, though grace does not cure the understanding of its original, natural weakness, yet it cures it of its contracted weakness, which was voluntary in its origin, but necessary, being contracted. And as the will had a hand in causing it, so must it have, in the voluntary use of the aforesaid means, in the cure of it. So much to show you how the understanding is guilty of sin.

*In the margin: The operations and maladies of the intellect.*

Though no actual knowledge is so immediate as to be without the mediation of the senses and the imagination, yet supposing these, knowledge is distinguished into immediate and mediate. The immediate is when the being, quality, etc. of a thing, or the truth of a proposition, is known immediately in itself by its proper evidence. Mediate knowledge is when the being of a thing, or the truth of a proposition, is known by means of some other intervening thing or proposition, whose evidence affords us a light to discern it.

The understanding is much more satisfied when it can see things and truths immediately in their proper evidence. But when it cannot, it is glad of any means to help it.

The further we go in the series of means, (knowing one thing by another, and that by another, and so on,) the more unsatisfied the understanding is, as apprehending a possibility of mistake, and a difficulty in escaping mistake in the use of so many *media*.

When the evidence of one thing in its proper nature shows us another, this is to know by mere reasoning or argument.

When the medium of our knowing one thing is the credibility of another man's report, who knows it, this is (though a reasoning or argument too, yet) in particular called belief; which is strong or weak, certain or uncertain, as the evidence of the reporter's credibility is certain or uncertain, and our apprehension of it strong or weak.

In both cases, the understanding's fault is either an utter privation of the act, or of the disposition to it; or else a privation of the rectitude of the act. When it should know by the proper evidence of the thing, the privation of its act is called ignorance or nescience, and the privation of its rectitude is called error (which differ as not seeing and seeing falsely.) When it should know by testimony, the privation of its act is simple unbelief, or not believing, and the privation of its rectitude is either disbelief, when they think the reporter errs, or misbelief, when it believes a testimony that is not to be believed.

So that you see by what is said that the diseases of the mind to be cured are, 1. Mere ignorance. 2. Error; thinking truth to be falsehood, and falsehood truth. 3. Unbelief. 4. Disbelief. And 5. Misbelief.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture:** no passage is quoted in this range, so nothing was fetched from the NET Bible. The phrase "the things of the Spirit" (paragraph beginning "So, as to the habits") is an unmarked allusion, probably to 1 Corinthians 2:14, which Baxter does not cite; we have not added the reference to the text.
- **Grand Direction 8 and the Directions numbered I and II:** the source prints the two "general directions" as "Direct. I." and "Direct. II.", and the second is also headed "2." (the first is likewise headed "1." inside its paragraph). We render them as Direction 1 and Direction 2 and keep Baxter's inner "1." and "2.". They are the two general directions within the Grand Direction, not the whole book's Directions.
- **the sixth direction before this:** Baxter's own cross-reference; we have not checked which direction it points to.
- **Liberium arbitrium:** printed as in the source; the usual Latin is *liberum arbitrium* ("free choice"), so "Liberium" looks like a misprint.
- **fantasy** (Baxter's "fantasy"): the imagination, the faculty that forms and holds mental images, which in the older psychology stands between the senses and the understanding. We use "the imagination."
- **discourse / discourse or argument:** here Baxter means step-by-step reasoning, not talk. We use "reasoning."
- **Technical terms kept:** *privation* (the lack or absence of something that ought to be present); *rectitude* (correctness, rightness); *nescience* (plain not-knowing); *volition* and *nolition* (willing and refusing to will); *participatively* and "by participation" (sharing in the quality of the will, as the intellect does when the will commands it); *hypothetically* (conditionally, on the supposition of an earlier fault); *radically* (at the root); *media* (Latin plural of *medium*, the means by which one comes to know a thing); *dispensation* (the arrangement by which God administers grace). The seven kinds of ignorance, error, and disbelief at the close follow the school logic of Baxter's day.
- **word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): Holy Ghost, Holy Spirit; deferred, put off; fetched, drawn; containeth and the like, contains; undisposedness, unreadiness (his word means a lack of inclination or readiness); want (to lack), lack; extrinsic, outside; wilful, willful; fulness, fullness; conjunct, joined together; properer, more proper; laudable and rewardable, or vituperable and punishable, praiseworthy and rewardable, or blameworthy and punishable; imperate, commanded; primitively, in its original state; meritorious cause, deserving cause; disability, inability; impotency, powerlessness; forbearance, holding back; excitation, stirring-up; intervenient, intervening; &c., etc.
- **Light changes of mood and wording** (not of sentence structure): "in case the object be absent" and similar subjunctives became plain indicatives ("is absent"); "though they would" became "though they would resist" to complete the sense; "speaking exclusively as to the other" became "speaking as if the other were excluded."
- **hypothetically, naturally, and necessity:** Baxter's argument is that an ignorance can be unavoidable now ("necessary") yet culpable because its origin lay in a free act of the will; that is why he says it is "participatively sinful."
- **Maxime pars hominum morbo jactatur eodem** (note 98): the line resembles verse from the Roman satirists (it may be Horace), but Baxter gives no author and we have not confirmed one. The printed "Maxime" may be a variant of "Maxima."
- **natural man** (a technical Pauline-Reformation term for a person not renewed by the Spirit); kept as written.
