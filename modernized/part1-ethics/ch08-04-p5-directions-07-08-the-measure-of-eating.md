# Chapter 8: Part 4, Title 1, Direction 7 (continued) and Direction 8 (the measure of eating; the table as a snare)

*This file begins partway through Direction 7 ("Understand well what is most conducive to your health..."), after its first paragraph, and ends partway through Direction 8; the next file continues it.*

*In the margin: The measure of eating.*

It is a very great oversight in the education of youth that they are not taught early some common and necessary precepts about diet, acquainting them with what tends to health and life, and what to sickness, pain, and death; and it would be no unprofitable or unnecessary thing if princes took a course that all their subjects might have some such common needful precepts familiarly known (as if it were in the books that children first learn to read in, together with the precepts of their moral duty); for it is certain that men do not love death or sickness, and that all men love their health and life; and therefore those that do not fear God would be much restrained from excess by the fear of sickness and of death: and what an advantage this would be to the commonwealth you may easily perceive, when you consider what a mass of treasure it would save, besides the lives, and health, and strength of so many subjects. [420] And it is certain that most people have no considerable knowledge of what measure is best for them; but the common rule that they judge by is their appetite. They think they have eaten enough when they have eaten as long as they liked; and not before. If they could eat more with an appetite, and not be sick after it, they never think they have been guilty of gluttony or excess.

First, therefore, you must know that appetite is not to be your rule or measure, either for quantity, quality, or time. [421] For, 1. It is irrational, and reason is your ruling faculty, if you are men. 2. It depends on the temperament of the body, and the humors and diseases of it, and not merely on the natural need of food. A man in a dropsy is most thirsty, that has least cause to drink: though frequently in a putrid or malignant fever a draught of cold drink would probably be death, yet the appetite desires it nevertheless. Stomachs that have acid humors commonly have a strong appetite, however weak the digestion is, and most of them could eat with an appetite more than twice as much as they ought to eat. And on the contrary, some others do not desire so much as is necessary to their sustenance, and must be urged to eat against their appetite. 3. Most healthy people in the world have an appetite for much more than nature can well digest, and would kill themselves if they pleased their appetites; for God never gave man his appetite to be the measure of his eating or drinking, but to make that pleasant to him which reason bids him take. 4. Man's appetite is not now so sound and regular as it was before the fall; but has grown more rebellious and unruly, and diseased as the body is: and therefore it is now much more unfit to be our measure than it was before the fall. 5. You see it even in swine, and many greedy children, that would at once kill themselves, if they did not have the reason of others to rule them. 6. Poison itself may be as delightful to the appetite as food; and dangerous foods, as those that are most wholesome. So that it is most certain that appetite is not fit to be the measure of a man. Yet this is true as well, that when reason has nothing against it, then an appetite shows what nature takes to be most agreeable to itself; and reason therefore has something for it (if it has nothing against it); because it shows what the stomach is likely best to agree with and digest; and it is some help to reason to discern when it is prepared for food.

Secondly, it is certain also that the present feeling of ease or sickness is no certain rule to judge your digestion or your measure by; for though some tender, relaxed, windy stomachs are sick or troubled when they are overloaded, or exceed their measure, yet with most it is not so; unless they exceed to very swinishness, they are not sick upon it, nor feel any hurt at present by lesser excesses, but only the imperfection of digestion corrupts the humors, and prepares for sicknesses by degrees (as was said before); and one feels it a month after in some diseased evacuations; and another a year after; and another not for many years, till it has turned to some incurable disease (for the diseases that are bred by such long preparations are ordinarily much more incurable than those that come only from sudden accidents and alterations, in a cleaner body). Therefore to say, I feel it does me no harm, and therefore it is no excess, is the saying of an idiot, that has no foreseeing reason, and does not resist an enemy while he is garrisoning, fortifying, and arming himself, but only when it comes to blows: or like him that would go into a pesthouse, and say, I feel it does me no harm; but within a few days or weeks he will feel it. As if the beginning of a consumption were no hurt to them, because they do not feel it! Thus living like a beast will at last make men judge like beasts; and brutify their brains as well as their bellies.

*In the margin: Rules for the measure of eating.*

Thirdly, it is certain also that the common custom and opinion is no certain rule; indeed, certainly it is an erring rule; for judging by appetite has brought men ordinarily to take excess to be only temperance. All these then are false measures.

If I should here presume to give you any rules for judging of a right measure, physicians would think I went beyond my calling, and some of them might be offended at a design that tends so much to their impoverishing, and those that serve the greedy worm would be more offended. Therefore I shall only give you these general hints. 1. Nature is content with a little; but appetite is never content till it has drowned nature. [422] 2. It is the perfection of digestion, and goodness of the nourishment, that is more conducive to health than the quantity. 3. Nature will more easily overcome twice the quantity of some light and easily digested nourishment than half as much of gross and heavy foods. (Therefore those that prescribe just twelve ounces a day, without distinguishing foods that differ so much, are much mistaken.) 4. A healthy, strong body must have more than the weak and sickly. 5. Middle-aged persons must have more than old folks or children. [423] 6. Hard laborers must have more than easy laborers; and these more than the idle, or students, or any that stir but little. 7. A body of close pores, that discharges little by sweat or transpiration, must have less, especially of moisture, than another. 8. So must a cold and phlegmatic constitution. 9. So must a stomach that corrupts its food, and casts it forth by periodical bilious evacuations. 10. That which troubles the stomach in the digestion is too much, or too bad, except with very weak, sickly persons. 11. So is that too much or bad which makes you more dull for study, or more heavy and unfit for labor (unless some disease is the principal cause). 12. A body that by excess is already filled with crudities should take less than another, that nature may have time to digest and use them up. 13. Everyone should labor to know the temperament of their own bodies, and what diseases they are most inclined to, and so have the judgment of their physician or some skillful person, to give them such directions as are suitable to their own particular temperament and diseases. 14. Hard laborers err more in the quality than the quantity, partly through poverty, partly through ignorance, and partly through appetite, while they refuse that which is more wholesome (as plain bread and beer) if it is less pleasing to them. 15. If I may presume to conjecture, ordinarily very hard laborers exceed in quantity by about a fourth part; shopkeepers and persons of easier trades ordinarily exceed by about a third part; pleasure-loving gentlemen and their servingmen, and other servants of theirs that have no hard labor, usually exceed by about half in half (but still I except persons that are extraordinarily temperate through weakness, or through wisdom); and the same gentlemen usually exceed in variety, costliness, elaborateness, and time, much more than they do in quantity (so that they are gluttons of the first magnitude). The children of those that do not govern their appetites, but let them eat and drink as much and as often as they desire it, usually exceed by above half in half, and lay the foundation of the diseases and miseries of all their lives. [424] All this is about the truth, though the belly does not believe it.

When you have once grown wise enough about what in measure, and time, and quality is fittest for your health, do not go beyond that upon any urging of appetite, or of friends; for all that is beyond that is gluttony and indulgence in physical pleasure, in its degree.

**Direction 8. If you can lawfully avoid it, do not make your table a snare of temptation to yourselves or others.**

I know a greedy appetite will make any table that has only necessities a snare to itself; but do not yourselves unnecessarily become devils, or tempters to yourselves or others. [425] 1. For quality, do not study deliciousness too much: unless for some weak, disordered stomachs, the best food is that which leaves behind it in the mouth neither a troublesome loathing, nor an eager appetite after more, for the taste's sake; but such as bread is, that leaves the palate in an indifferent moderation. The elaborate inventions of new and delicious dishes, merely to please the appetite, is gluttony inviting to greater gluttony; excess in quality to invite to excess in quantity.

*Objection.* But, you will say, I shall be thought stingy or mean, and reproached behind my back, if my table is so fitted to the temperate and abstinent.

*Answer.* This is the pleading of pride for gluttony; rather than you will be talked against by belly-gods, or ignorant, fleshly people, you will sin against God, and prepare a feast or sacrifice for Bacchus or Venus. The ancient Christians were torn by beasts, because they would not cast a little frankincense into the fire on the altar of an idol; and will you feed so many idol bellies so liberally to avoid their censure? Did I not tell you that greediness is an irrational vice? Good and temperate persons will speak well of you for it; and do you more regard the judgment and esteem of belly-gods? [426]

*Objection.* But it is not only dissolute, self-indulgent persons that I mean; I have no such at my table; but it will be the matter of criticism even to good people, and those that are sober.

*Answer.* I told you some measure of gluttony has become a common sin; and many are tainted with it through custom, that otherwise are good and sober: but shall they therefore be left as incurable? or shall they make all others as bad as they? And must we all commit that sin, which some sober people have grown to favor? You bear their censures about different opinions in religion, and other matters of difference; and why not here? The deluded Quakers may be witnesses against you, that while they run into the contrary extreme, can bear the deepest censures of all the world about them. And can you not for honest temperance and sobriety bear the censures of some disordered or guilty persons that are of another mind; certainly in this they are no temperate persons, when they plead for excess, and the baits of indulgence in physical pleasure and intemperance.

2. For variety also, do not make your table unnecessarily a snare: have no greater variety than the weakness of stomachs, or variety of appetites, requires. Unnecessary variety and pleasantness of foods are the devil's great instruments to draw men to gluttony (and I would wish no good people to be his cooks or caterers): when the very brutish appetite itself begins to say of one dish, I have enough, then comes another to tempt it to more excess, and another after that to more. All this that I have said, I have the concurrent judgment of physicians in, who condemn fullness and variety as the great enemies of health, and nurseries of diseases. And is not the concurrent judgment of physicians more valuable about matters of health than your private opinions, or appetites? yet when sickness requires variety, it is necessary.

3. Do not sit too long at the table: for besides the sin of wasting time, it is only the way to entice down a little and a little more: and he that would be temperate if he sat only a quarter of an hour (which is ordinarily enough) will exceed when he has the temptation of half an hour (which is enough for the entertainment of strangers); much more when you must sit out an hour (which is too much in all conscience): though greedy eating is not good, yet sober feeding may satisfy nature in a little time.

4. See that your provisions are not more costly than is necessary: though I know there must be a difference allowed for persons and times, yet see that no cost is spent unnecessarily; and let sober reason, and not pride and gluttony, judge of the necessity: we commonly call him the rich glutton, Luke 16, that fared sumptuously every day; it is not said that he ate any more than other men, but that he fared sumptuously. [427] You cannot answer for it comfortably to God, to lay that out on the belly which might do more good another way: it is a horrid sin to spend such a store of wealth unnecessarily on the belly as is ordinarily done. The cheapest diet (*ceteris paribus*) must be preferred.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[420] Multum confert cogitatio exitus, quod cum omnibus vitias sit commune, tamen huic proprium. Petrarch. (Thinking about the outcome helps much; which, though it is common to all vices, is yet especially proper to this one. Petrarch. Rendered loosely; "vitias" is printed so in the source, apparently for *vitiis*, "vices.")

[421] Temperantia voluptatibus imperat: alias odit atque abigit: alias dispensat et ad sanum modum dirigit; nec unquam ad illas propter ipsas venit. Senec. (Temperance rules over pleasures: some it hates and drives away; others it portions out and directs to a healthy measure; and it never comes to them for their own sake. Seneca.) Scit optimum esse modum cupidorum, non quantum velis, sed quantum debeas sumere. Senec. (It knows that the best measure of desires is to take not as much as you wish, but as much as you ought. Seneca. "Cupidorum" is printed so; it may stand for *cupidinum* or *cupiditatum*, "desires.")

[422] Venter parvo contentus est, si das illi quod debes, non quod potes. Senec. (The belly is content with little, if you give it what you owe it, not what you can. Seneca.)

[423] Juvenum virtus est, nihil nimis. Socrat. (The virtue of the young is: nothing too much. Socrates.)

[424] Venter præcepta non audit. Senec. (The belly does not listen to precepts. Seneca.)

[425] If you will not take this counsel, at least make it your custom after the meal to set before your guests a basin and a feather, or a provang, to vomit it up again, that you may show some mercy to their bodies, if you will show none to their souls.

[426] A pleasure-seeker craving to be admitted by Cato among his close friends, Cato answered him, I cannot live with one whose palate is wiser than his brain. Erasmus.

[427] The old fashion in countrymen's houses was not amiss, where the story of this rich glutton and Lazarus used to be painted over their tables on their walls.

## Translator's notes

- **Range:** this file starts at the margin note "The measure of eating," partway through Direction 7 of Part 4, Title 1 (the directive sentence of Direction 7 and its first paragraph are in the previous file), and ends partway through Direction 8, after its fourth numbered point; the next file continues with an objection about the "scandal of covetousness."
- **Title:** Baxter's "Tit." (Title) is a heading or division within a Part; Title 1 of Part 4 holds the directions against gluttony.
- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Luke 16 (flag):** Baxter cites "Luke xvi." for the rich man "that fared sumptuously every day" but does not put the words in quotation marks, so his wording is kept. This is the King James wording of Luke 16:19. The NET reads "who feasted sumptuously every day." This matters to his argument: he leans on the point that the text does not say the rich man ate more than others, only that he "fared" (lived, was provided for) sumptuously, that is, expensively. The NET's "feasted" leans more toward eating in quantity, which weakens the distinction he draws. "The rich glutton" is Baxter's name for the man (traditionally called Dives); the text calls him only "a rich man."
- **Unmarked allusion:** "the fall" (Genesis 3).
- **Medical claims:** Baxter's account of diet (humors, digestion in stages, "acid humors," "crudities," his percentages of overeating, the twelve-ounce rule he criticizes) is the physiology of his day and his own estimate. It is kept as his, not offered as modern medical advice.
- **temperament:** Baxter's "temperature" (of the body), meaning its constitution or balance of humors, not heat. Changed in both places.
- **humors:** kept. In the older physiology, the bodily fluids (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile) whose balance was thought to govern health. **phlegmatic:** having too much phlegm, so cold and moist in constitution. **bilious evacuations:** discharges of bile, as by vomiting. **transpiration:** loss of moisture through the skin. **crudities:** undigested matter in the body. **dropsy:** an older name for swelling from fluid build-up (edema). **putrid or malignant fever:** older names for severe fevers; we make no claim about which modern diseases they correspond to. **consumption:** a wasting disease, often tuberculosis. **pesthouse:** a house where plague victims were isolated.
- **digestion:** Baxter's "concoction" (twice), the older physiological term for digesting food. **corrupts:** his "doth vitiate."
- **brutify:** kept; to make brutish, like a beast.
- **the greedy worm:** kept as printed. Probably the belly or appetite pictured as a worm; we have not confirmed his exact sense.
- **half in half:** kept as printed. It seems to mean that about half of what these people eat is excess (or that they eat about half again as much as they need); we are not sure which, so we kept his phrase.
- **elaborateness:** Baxter's "curiosity," meaning elaborate refinement in food, not inquisitiveness. **elaborate (inventions):** his "curious."
- **easily digested:** his "passable," meaning easily passing through the body, not "adequate."
- **greediness:** his "gulosity," gluttonous greed.
- **dissolute, self-indulgent:** his "riotous, luxurious." In his day "luxurious" usually meant given to excess or lust, not merely comfortable.
- **criticism:** his "obloquy." **stingy or mean:** his "niggardly or sordid."
- **Quakers:** Baxter's polemic, kept as he wrote it. His point is that the Quakers bear the world's censure for their "contrary extreme" (their strictness), so his readers can bear censure for simple temperance.
- **Bacchus or Venus:** the Roman god of wine and goddess of love, standing for drink and lust.
- **The ancient Christians ... frankincense:** Baxter's reference to the Roman persecutions, when Christians who refused to offer incense to the gods could be condemned, some to the beasts. Kept as his.
- **ceteris paribus:** Latin, "other things being equal." Printed "_cæteris paribus_"; spelled here in the usual modern form.
- **provang** (note 425): kept. It appears to name an instrument (perhaps of whalebone) used to clear the stomach or to make one vomit; we have not confirmed its exact form.
- **Petrarch; Seneca; Socrates; Erasmus** (notes 420-424, 426): the attributions are Baxter's; we have not checked where the quotations occur in these writers. "Nothing too much" was a well-known Greek maxim; Baxter attributes it to Socrates. The Cato story in note 426 is given as from Erasmus, possibly his *Apophthegmata*; not confirmed.
- **Word swaps (Baxter's word, then ours):** betimes, early; meat (food), food; healthful (of people or a body), healthy; grateful, pleasant; presently, at once; withal, as well; is like best to close with, is likely best to agree with; overcharged, overloaded; aforesaid, as was said before; twelvemonth, year; uncurable, incurable; nay, indeed; intimations, hints; easilier, more easily; differencing, distinguishing; do much mistake, are much mistaken; evacuateth, discharges; waste (them), use (them) up; mere (bread), plain; voluptuous, pleasure-loving; sensuality, indulgence in physical pleasure (twice); importunity, urging; necessaries, necessities; distempered, disordered (twice); at meat, at the table; tice, entice; bestowed, spent; answer it, answer for it; was wont to be, used to be; use after meat to, make it your custom after the meal to; bason, basin; sensualist, pleasure-seeker; familiars, close friends; of Cato, by Cato.
- **Small grammatical fills:** "acquainting them with what tends"; "knowledge of what measure"; "wise enough about what"; "exceed by about" (Baxter: "exceed about"); "do not yourselves ... become" (Baxter: "do not you ... become"). Each adds or alters only what modern grammar needs.
