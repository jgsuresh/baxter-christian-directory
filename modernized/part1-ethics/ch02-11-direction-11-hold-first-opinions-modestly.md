# Chapter 2: Direction 11

**Direction 11. Do not let your first opinions about the disputed difficulties in religion, where Scripture is not very plain, be too dogmatic, confident, or fixed; but hold them modestly, with a due suspicion of your unripe understandings, and with room for further information, supposing it possible or probable that, with better instruction, evidence, and maturity, you may change your minds in such things.**

I know that the factions who take up their religion on the authority of their party are against this direction: thinking that you must first find the right church, and then hold all that the church holds; and therefore change your mind in nothing that you receive this way. I know also that some libertines and half believers would corrupt this direction by extending it to the most plain and necessary truths, persuading you to hold Christianity itself as only an uncertain, probable opinion.

But as God's foundation stands sure, so we must be surely built on his foundation. He who does not believe the essentials of Christianity as a certain, necessary revelation of God is not a Christian, but an unbeliever. And he who does not believe all that he understands in the word of God believes nothing on the authority of that word. Indeed faith has its weakness in those who are sincere; and they are compelled to lament the remnants of unbelief, and cry, "Lord, increase our faith; help our unbelief." But he who approves of his doubting, and wants it so, and thinks the revelation is uncertain, and such as will warrant no firmer a belief, I should scarcely say this man is a Christian. Christianity must be received as a divine, infallible revelation. But controversies about less necessary things cannot be decided dogmatically by the ignorant or by young beginners, without hypocrisy, or a human faith passing under the name of a divine one. I am far from lessening your divine belief of all that you can understand in Scripture, and, by implicit trust, of all the rest in general. And I am far from diminishing the credibility of any truth of God. But the reasons for this direction are these:

1. When it is certain that you have only a dark, uncertain grasp of any point, to think it is clear and certain is only to deceive yourselves by pride. And to cry out against all uncertainty as skepticism, which yet you cannot set aside, is only to revile your own weakness, and the common weakness of mankind, and foolishly to suppose that every man can be as wise and certain, whenever he chooses, as he should be. Now reason and experience will tell you that a young, unfurnished understanding is not likely to see the evidence of difficult points as, by nearer approach and better advantage, it may do.

2. If your conclusions are dogmatic, resting on mere self-conceit, you may be in error for all you know; and so you are only confident in an error. And then how far may you go in leading others astray, and censuring dissenters, and come back when you have done, and confess that you were all this while mistaken yourselves!

3. For a man to be confident that he knows what he does not know is only the way to keep him ignorant, and to shut the door against all means of further information. When the opinion is fixed by prejudice and self-conceit, there is no ready entrance for the light.

4. And to be confident without grounds, so young, is not only to take up your teacher's word instead of a faith and knowledge of your own, but also to forestall all diligence to know more: and so you may set aside all your studies, except only to know what those men hold whose judgments are your religion: too popish and easy a way to be safe.

5. If you must never change your first opinions or understandings, how will you grow in understanding? Will you be no wiser as an adult than you were in childhood, and no wiser after long study and experience than before? Nature and grace tend to increase.

Indeed, if you were never so dogmatic in your opinions, you could not resolve to hold them to the end: for light is powerful, and may change you whether you will or not: you cannot tell what that light will do which you have never seen. But prejudice will make you resist the light, and make it harder for you to understand.

I speak this from much experience and observation. Our first unripe understandings of things will certainly be greatly changed, if we are studious and our understandings improve. Study the controversies about grace and free will, or about other such points of difficulty, when you are young, and it is two to one that maturity will afterward make them quite another thing to you. For my own part, my judgment has changed from many of my youthful, confident opinions: and where it holds the same conclusion, it rejects abundance of the arguments as worthless which it once rested in. And where I keep to the same conclusions and arguments, my understanding of them is not the same, but I see more satisfying light in many things which I took only on trust before. And if I had resolved to hold to all my first opinions, I must have given up most of my studies, and lost much truth which I have discovered, and not made that my own which I did hold; and I must have resolved to live and die a child.

The sum is, Hold fast the substance of religion, and every clear and certain truth which you see in its own evidence: and also reverence your teachers, especially the universal church, or the generality of wise and godly men; and do not be hasty to take up any private opinion; and especially to contradict the opinion of your governors and teachers in small and disputed things. But yet, in such matters, receive their opinions only with a human faith, until indeed you have more, and therefore with a supposition that time and study are very likely to change your understandings; and with a reserve, to study impartially and welcome the truth, and not to sit still just where you were born.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Source oddity:** the last line of the assigned range (`[Sidenote: What to do when controversies do divide the church.]`) is the margin note for the paragraph that begins Direction 12 in the next section. We kept it at the end of this file so that every line of the range is accounted for; it belongs with Direction 12.
- **"Lord, increase our faith; help thou our unbelief"** (paragraph 3): Baxter gives no reference and puts the words in quotation marks. They blend two verses: Luke 17:5 and Mark 9:24. The NET reads "Increase our faith!" (Luke 17:5, the apostles speaking) and "I believe; help my unbelief!" (Mark 9:24, the father of the boy). We kept Baxter's blended wording, modernized ("help thou our unbelief" to "help our unbelief"), as his paraphrase, and added no reference to the text. These references are ours, not Baxter's. Since he joins the two into one prayer, the NET wording does not change his sense.
- **"God's foundation standeth sure"** (paragraph 3): an unmarked, unquoted allusion, probably to 2 Timothy 2:19 (a reference not in Baxter's text; we have not confirmed that he means this verse). The NET reads "God's solid foundation remains standing," which fits the sense Baxter uses.
- **dogmatic:** Baxter's "peremptory" (decisive and not open to question). **self-conceit:** his "self-conceitedness"; we kept "conceit" in this compound, since the sense, an inflated opinion of one's own judgment, is still clear. **skepticism:** his "scepticism."
- **compelled to lament:** Baxter's "fain to lament" ("fain" here means "obliged"). **whenever he chooses:** his "when he list." **given up:** his "forborne." **for all you know:** his "for aught you know." **worthless:** his "vain." **authority:** his "credit" (in "on the credit of their party" and "the credit of any truth"; the second we rendered "credibility"). **welcome:** his "entertain." **as an adult:** his "at age."
- **without grounds:** Baxter's "ungroundedly."
- **libertines, half believers, factions:** Baxter's own words, kept; he does not define them here. We read them as people who reject restraint, people who hold the faith loosely, and party-minded groups (our reading, unconfirmed).
- **never so dogmatic:** Baxter's "never so peremptory," meaning "ever so dogmatic."
- **implicit** (paragraph 3: "implicitly of all the rest in general"): Baxter's sense is trust without examining the particulars. We rendered it "by implicit trust."
- **understanding(s) / apprehension(s):** his "apprehensions" are what a person grasps or takes a matter to be; we rendered it "understandings," "grasp," or "opinions" by context. **prejudice:** a judgment formed beforehand, as in Baxter's day; kept.
- **Christianity; Christian:** capitalized in modern form (the source has lowercase). **unbeliever:** Baxter's "infidel."
- **too popish and easy a way to be safe:** kept as Baxter wrote it. "Popish" and "papist" are his words for Roman Catholic; this is his polemic.
- **governors:** kept as Baxter's word for those in authority over the reader (church and civil leaders, and perhaps others); he does not say.
- **the universal church:** Baxter's own phrase, kept; he does not say here which body he means, and we do not supply one.
- **two to one:** left as written (a common expression for "very likely").
- **grace and free will:** left as written; Baxter does not name the writers or positions he has in mind.
- **sit still just where you were born:** left as written; he means remaining in the opinions of one's birth and upbringing.
- **Cross-reference not checked:** Direction 12 begins immediately after this range.
