# Chapter 8: Part 4, Title 2: what drunkenness is, its kinds and degrees, and its causes (1–2)

**Title 2. Directions against drunkenness, and all excess of drink.**

I. The most that I have said against gluttony will serve against excess of drink also, therefore I need not repeat it. Drunkenness, in the largest sense, extends both to the affection and to the effect: and so he is a drunkard (that is, by reckoning, in the sight of God) who would drink too much if he had it, and is not restrained by his will, but by necessity.

Drunkenness in the effect or act is sometimes taken more largely, sometimes more strictly. Largely taken, it signifies all drinking to excess to please the appetite. Two things here make up the crime: 1. Love of the drink, or pleasing the appetite, which we call gulosity. 2. Excess in drinking; which excess may be in quantity or quality.

Drunkenness strictly taken signifies drinking till reason has received some hurt: and of this there are many degrees. He that has in the least degree disturbed his reason, and disabled or hindered it from its proper office, is drunk in that degree: and he that has overturned it, or quite disabled it, is stark drunk, or drunk in a greater degree.

All excess of drink is sinful gulosity or indulgence in physical pleasure, of the same nature with gluttony, and falls under all my last reproofs and directions. And in some persons that can sit it out, and bear much drink without intoxication, the sin may be greater than in some others, that by a smaller quantity are drunk by a surprise, before they are aware; but yet, *cæteris paribus*, the overthrow of the understanding makes the sin to be much the greater; for it has all the evil that the other degrees have, with more. It is a voluptuous excess in drink to the impairment of reason. Gulosity is the general nature of it: excess is the matter: impairment of reason is its special form.

It is excess of drinking when you drink more than, according to the judgment of sound reason, tends to fit your body indirectly or directly for its proper duty, without a greater hurt. Sometimes the immediate benefit is most to be regarded (as, if a man had some present duty of very great importance to perform). The present benefit consists, 1. In the abatement of such a troublesome thirst or pain as hinders you from doing your duty. 2. In adding that refreshment and alacrity to the spirits as makes them fitter instruments for the operations of the mind and body. That measure which does one or both of these without greater hurt is not too great. I say, without greater hurt; because if any should in a dropsy or a fever prefer a little present ease and alacrity before his health and life, it would be excess. Or if any man ordinarily drinks more than nature will well digest, and which causes the indigestion of his food, and consequently crudities, and consequently a dunghill of phlegm and unhealthy humors fit to engender many diseases, this is excess of drinking, though he feels it ease him and make him cheerful for the present time. And this is the common case of most bibbers or tipplers that are not stark drunkards: they feel a present ease from thirst, and perhaps a little alacrity of spirits, and therefore they think that measure is no excess, which yet tends to crudities and diseases, and the destruction of their health and life.

Therefore (except in some great, extraordinary case of necessity) it is not so much the present as the future foreseen effects which must direct you to know your measure. Reason can foresee, though appetite cannot. Future effects are usually great and long; when present effects may be small and short. He that will do that which tends to the hurt of his health for the present easing or pleasing of his thirsty appetite sins against reason, and plays the beast. You should be so well acquainted with your bodies, and the means of your own health, as to know first whether the enduring of the thirst, or the drinking to quench it, is likely to be the more hurtful to your health, and more a hindrance to your duty.

And for the present alacrity which strong drink brings to some, you must foresee that you do not purchase it at too dear a rate, by a longer dullness or disablement afterwards: and take care that you do not take an alien, counterfeit hilarity, consisting in mere physical delight, for that serenity and just alacrity of the spirits as fits you for your duty. For this also is a usual (and willful) self-deceit of pleasure-seekers: they make themselves believe that a cup of sack or strong drink gives them a true assisting alacrity, when it only causes a physical delight, which does more to hinder and corrupt the mind than truly further it in its duty: and differs from true alacrity as paint from beauty, or as a fever does from our natural heat.

You see then that intemperance in drinking is of two sorts: 1. Bibbing, or drinking too much. 2. Drunkenness (in various degrees). And these intemperate bibbers are of several sorts. (1.) Those that, when they have overheated themselves, or are feverish, or have any ordinary diseased thirst, will please their appetites, though it is to their hurt; and will venture their health rather than endure the thirst. Though in fevers, dropsies, coughs, it should be the greatest enemy to them, yet they are such beastly servants to their appetites that drink they must, whatever comes of it: though physicians forbid them, and friends dissuade them, they have so much of the brute and so little of the man that appetite is quite too hard for reason with them. These are of two sorts: one sort keep the soundness of their reason, though they have lost all the strength and power of it, for lack of a resolved will; and these confess that they should abstain, but tell you they cannot, they are not so much men. The other sort have given up their very reason (such as it is) to the service of their appetites: and these will not believe (till the cough, or gout, or dropsy, etc. make them believe it) that their measure of drinking is too much, or that it will do them hurt; but say that it would hurt them more to refrain from it; some through real ignorance, and some made willingly ignorant by their appetites.

(2.) Another sort of bibbers there are, much worse than those, who have no great, diseased thirst to excuse their gulosity, but call it a thirst whenever their appetite would have drink; and accustom themselves ordinarily to satisfy such an appetite, and drink almost as often as the throat desires it, and say it is but to quench their thirst; and never charge themselves with intemperance for it. These may be known from the first sort of bibbers by the quality of their drink: it is cold small beer that the first sort desire, to quench a real thirst; when reason bids them endure it, if other means will not quench it. But it is wine, or strong drink, or some drink that has a delicious taste, which the second sort of bibbers use, to please the appetite, which they call their thirst. And of these luxurious tipplers, next to stark drunkards, there are also various degrees, some being less guilty, and some more. [433]

1. The lowest degree are they that will never ordinarily drink but at meals: but they will then drink more than nature requires, or than is profitable to their health.

2. The second degree are they that are accustomed to drink between meals, when their appetite desires it, to the hindering of digestion, and the increase of crudities and catarrhs, and to the secret, gradual corrupting of their humors, and generating of many diseases; and this without any true necessity, or the approval of sound reason, or any wise physician: yet they tipple but at home, where you may find the pot by them at unseasonable times.

3. The third degree are many poor men that have not drink at home, and when they come to a gentleman's house, or a feast, or perhaps an alehouse, they will pour in for the present to excess, though not to drunkenness, and think it is no harm, because it is but seldom; and they drink such small drink all the rest of the year that they think such a fit as this sometimes is medicinal to them, and tends to their health.

4. Another rank of bibbers are those that, though they do not haunt alehouses or taverns, yet have a throat for every health or pledging cup that does not reach to drunkenness; and are accustomed ordinarily to drink many unnecessary cups in a day to pledge (as they call it) those that drink to them; and custom and courtesy are all their excuse.

5. Another degree of bibbers are common alehouse haunters, that love to be there, and to sit many hours perhaps in a day, with a pot by them, tippling, and drinking one to another. And if they have any bargain to make, or any friend to meet, the alehouse or tavern must be the place, where tippling may be one part of their work.

6. The highest degree are they that are not apt to be stark drunk, and therefore think themselves less faulty, while they sit at it, and make others drunk, and are strong themselves to bear away more than others can bear. They have the drunkard's appetite, and measure, and pleasure, though they have not his giddiness and loss of wits.

(3.) And of those that are truly drunk also, there are many degrees and kinds. As some will be drunk with less and some with more; so some are only possessed with a little diseased levity, and talkativeness, more than they had before: some also have distempered eyes, and stammering tongues: some also proceed to unsteady, reeling heads, and stumbling feet, and unfitness for their callings: some go further, to sick and vomiting stomachs, or else to sleepy heads: and some proceed to stark madness, quarreling, railing, bawling, hooting, ranting, roaring, or talking nonsense, or doing mischief: the furious sort being like mad dogs that must be tied; and the sottish, chattering, and spewing sort being commonly the derision of the boys in the streets.

II. Having told you what tippling and drunkenness is, I shall briefly tell you their causes; but briefly, because you may gather most of them from what is said of the causes of gluttony. 1. The first and grand causes are these three concurrent: a beastly, raging appetite or gulosity; a weakness of reason and resolution to rule it; and a lack of faith to strengthen reason, and of holiness to strengthen resolution. These are the very cause of all.

2. Another cause is their not knowing that their excess and tippling is really a hurt or danger to their health. And they are ignorant of this from many causes. One is, because they have been brought up among ignorant people, and never taught to know what is good or bad for their own bodies, but only by the common talk of the mistaken common people. Another is, because their appetite so masters their very reason that they can choose to believe that which they would not have to be true. Another reason is, because they are of healthy bodies, and therefore feel no hurt at present, and presume that they shall feel none hereafter, and see some abstemious persons weaker than they (who did not begin to be abstemious till some chronic disease had first invaded them). And thus they do by their bodies just as wicked men do by their souls: they judge all by present feeling, and have not wisdom enough to take things foreseen into their deliberation and accounts: that which will be a great while from now they take for nothing, or an uncertain something next to nothing. As heaven and hell do not move ungodly men, because they seem a great way off; so, while they feel themselves in health, they are not moved with the threatening of sickness: the cup is in their hands, and therefore they will not set it aside, for fear of they know not what, that will befall them you know not when. As the thief that was told he should answer for it at the day of judgment said he would take the other cow too, if he should stay unpunished till then; so these belly-gods think they will take the other cup, if they shall but stay till so long from now. And thus because this temporal punishment of their gulosity is not speedily exercised, the hearts of men are fully set in them to please their appetites.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). No Scripture is quoted in this range.
- **Labels:** this file continues Part 4 ("Directions for governing the taste and appetite"). The source heads this division "_Tit._ 2." "Tit." is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," that is, a heading or division within a Part; Title 1 (against gluttony) is in the earlier files. This file covers the opening of Title 2 through the second cause of drunkenness; the third cause continues in the next file.
- **Numbering:** Baxter's section "I." (what drunkenness is) contains the sorts "(1.)", "(2.)", "(3.)" with a numbered list 1–6 inside "(2.)"; section "II." begins the causes, numbered 1, 2, and on into the next file. All kept as printed.
- **by reckoning** (first paragraph): Baxter's "reputatively," that is, he is accounted a drunkard in God's sight even if he is not drunk in act.
- ***cæteris paribus*** (fourth paragraph): Latin in Baxter's text, kept: "other things being equal."
- **impairment** (of reason): Baxter's "depravation," meaning the corrupting or impairing of reason. His "general nature ... matter ... special form" is the scholastic language of definition (genus, matter, and form).
- **indirectly or directly:** Baxter's "mediately or immediately."
- **refreshment:** Baxter's "refocillation," a reviving or warming of the spirits.
- **alacrity** (kept): cheerful readiness. **spirits:** in the physiology of Baxter's day, the fine vital fluids thought to carry the operations of body and mind.
- **Medical terms of Baxter's day (kept):** **dropsy** (an accumulation of fluid in the body, what is now called edema); **crudities** (undigested matter in the stomach); **humors** (the bodily fluids, such as phlegm, that the older medicine held governed health; Baxter's "vicious humours" become "unhealthy humors" and "vitiating" becomes "corrupting"); **catarrhs** (discharges of phlegm, colds); **chronic** (his "chronical"). His medical claims are those of his time.
- **indigestion of his food:** Baxter's "inconcoction of his meat"; **digestion:** his "concoction."
- **bibbers, bibbing** (kept): habitual drinkers, drinking too much, short of drunkenness; Baxter distinguishes them from "stark drunkards." **tipplers, tippling** (kept): drinking little and often.
- **gulosity** (kept): greediness of appetite, as in Title 1.
- **sack:** a strong white wine imported from Spain and the Canaries. **small beer:** weak beer, the ordinary drink of the day. **health / pledging cup:** a toast drunk to someone, which custom required others to answer ("pledge").
- **courtesy:** Baxter's "compliment," in the older sense of formal politeness or ceremony.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): sensuality to indulgence in physical pleasure; sensual to physical; sensualists to pleasure-seekers; moment to importance; gust to taste; divers to various; want to lack; forbear to refrain from; use themselves / use to to accustom themselves / are accustomed to; approbation to approval; bred up to brought up; the vulgar to the common people; healthful to healthy; prating to chattering; wit to wits; set it by to set it aside; hence (from now) to from now; answer it to answer for it; "it were excess" to "it would be excess"; drunken to drunk.
- **Kept words:** stark drunk; voluptuous; levity (lightness, silliness); distempered (disordered); callings (occupations); sottish (stupefied with drink); spewing; belly-gods; abstemious; "they are not so much men" (that is, they have not manhood enough to abstain).
- **"the thief ... would take the other cow too":** Baxter's anecdote, kept as he tells it; we have not traced a source for it.
- **Unmarked allusion:** "the hearts of men are fully set in them" echoes Ecclesiastes 8:11 in the King James Version ("Because sentence against an evil work is not executed speedily, therefore the heart of the sons of men is fully set in them to do evil"). Baxter gives no reference; kept in his wording.
- **"for fear of they know not what, that will befall them you know not when":** the shift from "they" to "you" is Baxter's; kept as printed.
- **Note 433 (Gildas):** the British writer Gildas, probably from his work on the ruin of Britain (*De Excidio Britanniae*); we have not checked the passage. Baxter uses it to show that clergy as well as laymen were given to drunkenness.
