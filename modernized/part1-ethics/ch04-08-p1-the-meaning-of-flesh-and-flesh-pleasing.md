# Chapter 4: The Master Sin of Indulgence in Physical Pleasure: what "flesh" means, and when flesh-pleasing is a sin

**Directions against the Master Sin; Indulgence in Physical Pleasure, Flesh-pleasing, or Voluptuousness.**

I will be the shorter on this also, because I have spoken so much already in my *Treatise of Self-Denial*. Before we come to more particular directions, it is necessary that we discern the nature and evil of the sin which we speak against. I will therefore, 1. Tell you what is meant by "flesh" here. And 2. What flesh-pleasing it is that is unlawful, and what indulgence in physical pleasure is. 3. Wherein the harmfulness of this sin consists. 4. I will answer some objections. 5. I will show you the signs of it. 6. The counterfeits of the contrary. 7. And the false signs, which make some accused wrongfully, by themselves or others.

*In the margin: What is meant by flesh.*

I. Because you may find in writings between the Protestants and papists that it has become a controversy whether by "flesh," in Scripture, (where this sin is mentioned,) is meant the body itself, or the soul so far as it is unregenerate, I will briefly first settle this question. When we speak of the unregenerate part, we do not mean that the soul has two parts, of which one is regenerate, and the other unregenerate: but as the partly blind eye has both light and darkness in the same subject, so it is with the soul which is regenerate but in part, that is, in an imperfect degree: and by the unregenerate part is meant the whole soul, so far as it is unregenerate. The word "flesh," in its primary signification, is taken for that part of the body, as such, without respect to sin; and next for the whole body, as distinct from the soul. But in respect to sin and duty, it is taken, 1. Sometimes for the sensory appetite, not as sinful in itself, but as desiring that which God has obliged reason to deny. 2. More frequently, for this sensory appetite, as inordinate, and so sinful in its own desires. 3. Most frequently, for both the inordinate sensory appetite itself, and the rational powers, so far as they are corrupted by it, and sinfully disposed to obey it, or to follow, inordinately, physical things. But then the name is primarily taken for the physical appetite itself, (as diseased,) and only by participation for the rational powers. For the understanding of which, you must consider, 1. That the appetite itself might innocently (even in innocency) desire a forbidden object; when it was not the appetite that was forbidden, but the desire of the will, or the actual taking it. That a man in a fever thirsts for more than he may lawfully drink, is not of itself a sin; but to desire it by practical volition, or to drink it, is a sin; for it is these that God forbids, and not the thirst, which is not in our power to extinguish. That Adam had an appetite to the forbidden fruit was not his sin; but that his will obeyed his appetite, and his mouth did eat. For the appetite and sensory nature are of God, and are in nature antecedent to the law. God made us men before he gave us laws; and the law commands us not to alter ourselves from what he made us, or anything else which is naturally out of our power. But it is the sin of the will and executive powers, to do that evil which consists in obeying an innocent appetite. The appetite is necessary, and not free; and therefore God does not direct his commands or prohibitions to it directly, but to the reason and free will. 2. But since man's fall, the appetite itself is corrupted and has become inordinate, that is, more impetuous, violent, and unruly than it was in the state of innocency, by the unhappy disorders that have befallen the body itself. For we find now by experience, that a man that accustoms himself to sweet and wholesome temperance, has no such impetuous strivings of his appetite against his reason (if he is healthy) as those have that are either diseased, or used to obeying their appetites. And if habit and health make so great an alteration, we have cause to think that the depravation of nature by the fall did more. 3. This inordinate appetite is sin, by participation; so far as the appetite may be said to be free by participation, though not in itself; because it is the appetite of a rational, free agent: for though sin is first in the will in its true form, yet it is not the will only that is the subject of it, (though primarily it is,) but the whole man, so far as his acts are voluntary: for the will has the command of the other faculties; and they are voluntary acts which the will either commands, or does not forbid when it can and ought. To lie is a voluntary sin of the man, and the tongue shares the guilt. The will might have kept out that sin, which caused a disorder in the appetite. If a drunkard or a glutton provokes a lustful, inordinate appetite in himself, that lust is his sin, because it is voluntarily provoked. 4. Yet such additions of inordinacy, as men stir up in any appetite, by their own actual sins and customs, are more aggravated and dangerous to the soul, than that measure of disorder which is merely the fruit of original sin. 5. This inordinateness of the sensory appetite, with the mere privation of rectitude in the mind and will, is enough to cause man's actual sin. For if the horses are headstrong, the mere weakness, sleepiness, negligence, or absence of the coachman is enough to contribute to the overthrow of the coach: so if the reason and will had no positive inclinations to evil or physical objects, yet if they do not have so much light and love to higher things as will restrain the physical appetite, it has positive inclination enough in itself to forbidden things to ruin the soul by actual sin. 6. Yet, though it is a great controversy among theologians, I believe that in the rational powers themselves, there are positive, habitual, inordinate inclinations to physical, forbidden things. For as actually it is certain the reason of the proud and greedy contrives, and often approves the sin, and the will embraces it; so these are done so constantly in a continued stream of action by the whole man, that it seems apparent that the same faculties which run out in such strong and constant action, are themselves the subjects of much of the inclining, positive habits: and if it is so in additional, acquired sin, it is likely it was so in original sin. 7. Though sin is formally subjected first in the will, yet materially it is first in the sensory appetite (at least this sin of flesh-pleasing or indulgence in physical pleasure is). The flesh or sensory part is the first desirer, though it is sin no further than it is voluntary. 8. All this set together tells you further, that the word "flesh" signifies the physical inclinations of the whole man; but first and principally, the corrupted physical appetite; and the mind and will's (whether privative or positive) concurrence, but secondarily, and as falling in with the senses. The appetite, 1. Goes before reason. 2. And resists reason. 3. And at last corrupts and entices reason and will, to be its servants and purveyors.

And that the name "flesh" primarily signifies the sensory appetite itself, is evident in the very derivation of the name. Why else should the habits or vices of the rational powers be called "flesh" any more than "spirit," or anything else? If it were only in respect of their object, they should be called "the world" also, because that is their object. It is a certain rule, that That faculty is most predominant in man, whose object is made his chiefest end. Sensory delights being made the happiness and end of the unsanctified, it follows that the sensory faculties are predominant; which being called "flesh," (by a nearer trope,) the mind from it receives the name. The Scriptures also show this plainly: I do not remember any one place in the Old Testament where there is any probability that the word "flesh" should signify only the rational soul as unrenewed. Matthew 16:17, "flesh and blood did not reveal this to you;" that is, mortal man has not revealed it. Matthew 26:41, "The spirit is willing, but the flesh is weak;" that is, your bodies are weak, and resist the willingness of your souls: for sinful habits are not here called weak. John 3:6, "What is born of the flesh is flesh;" that is, man by natural generation can beget but natural man, called "flesh" from the visible part; and not the spiritual life, which nature is now destitute of. Romans 7:25, "with my flesh I serve the law of sin;" that is, with my sensory powers, and my mind so far is captivated thereto. Romans 8:1, 5, flesh and spirit are often opposed: "They that are of the flesh, mind the things of the flesh," etc.; that is, they in whom the sensory interest and appetite are predominant: for it is called "the body" here, as well as "the flesh," verses 10, 11, 13. The mind is here included; but it is as serving the flesh and its interest. Galatians 5:16, 17, 19, flesh and spirit are in the same manner opposed. And 2 Peter 2:18, the lusts of the flesh are in this sense mentioned. And Ephesians 2:3; Romans 7:18; 13:14; 1 Corinthians 5:5; 1 Peter 2:11; in which there is mention of "fleshly lusts, which fight against the spirit," and "fleshly wisdom, making provision for the flesh," etc. And Colossians 2:18, there is indeed the name of a fleshly mind, which is but a mind deceived and subservient to the flesh; so that the flesh itself, or sensory interest and appetite, are first signified in all or most places, and in some the mind, as subservient thereto.

It is of the greater consequence that this be rightly understood, lest you be tempted to imitate the libertines, who think the flesh or sensory part is capable of no moral good or evil, and therefore, all its actions being indifferent, we may be indifferent about them, and look only to the superior powers: and others, that think that the Scripture by "flesh" means only the rational soul as unrenewed, do thereupon cherish the flesh itself, and pamper it, and feed its unruly lusts, and never do anything to tame the body; but pray daily that God would destroy the flesh within them, that is, their sinful habits of reason and will, while they cherish the cause, or neglect a chief part of the cure. And on the contrary, some papists that look only at the body as their enemy, are much in fastings, and bodily exercises, while they neglect the mortifying of their carnal minds.

*In the margin: What flesh-pleasing is a sin.*

II. How far flesh-pleasing is a sin, I will distinctly open to you in these propositions: 1. The pleasing or displeasing of the sensory appetite in itself considered, is neither sin nor duty, good nor evil; but as commanded or forbidden by some law of God; which is not absolutely done.

2. To please the flesh by things forbidden is undoubtedly a sin, and so it is to displease it too. Therefore this is not all that is here meant, that the matter that pleases it must not be things forbidden.

3. To overvalue the pleasing of the flesh is a sin; and to prefer it before the pleasing of God, and the holy preparations for heaven, is the state of carnality and ungodliness, and the common cause of the damnation of souls. The delight of the flesh or senses is a natural good; and the natural desire of it in itself (as is said) is neither vice nor virtue: but when this little natural good is preferred before the greater spiritual, moral, or eternal good, this is the sin of carnal minds, which is threatened with death, Romans 8:1, 5-8, 13.

4. To buy the pleasing of the flesh at too dear a rate, as the loss of time, or with care and trouble, above its worth, and to be too much set on making provisions to please it, shows that it is overvalued, and is the sin forbidden, Romans 13:14.

## Translator's notes

- **Heading and "sensuality":** Baxter's "Sensuality" becomes "Indulgence in Physical Pleasure" in the section heading, and "sensual" becomes "physical," following the project's decisions. In this passage Baxter is defining the word: the sin is the pursuit of the pleasures of the senses and bodily appetites generally, not only sexual ones. "Sensitive" (appetite, powers, part, delights, interest) becomes "sensory." **Flesh-pleasing; voluptuousness; flesh; carnal; carnality; mortifying:** kept as Baxter's terms (see GLOSSARY.md).
- **Section numbering:** the opening paragraph sets out seven heads (what flesh is; what flesh-pleasing is unlawful; the harmfulness of the sin; objections; signs; counterfeits of the contrary; false signs). This file covers head I and the beginning of head II. The next file continues.
- **papists:** Baxter's word for Roman Catholics. His comparison of "papists" and some others in the paragraph on the libertines is his own polemic, kept as written.
- **libertines:** kept; Baxter's word for those who hold that the body's actions have no moral weight. His description of those who "pray daily that God would destroy the flesh" is his characterization of a group he does not name.
- **purblind:** Baxter's word for an eye that is partly blind; modernized as "partly blind." **subject:** used in its old philosophical sense, the thing in which a quality (here sin or light and darkness) is found. Kept as "subject."
- **inordinate; inordinacy; inordinateness:** kept. Baxter means excessive and out of proper order. **privation of rectitude:** the lack of right order in the mind and will. **privative or positive:** an absence of good, or an active inclination to evil. **formally... materially; by participation; antecedent; executive powers; practical volition; purveyors; trope:** philosophical or technical terms kept as Baxter wrote them; "purveyors" are suppliers (the appetite makes reason and will serve and supply it).
- **venereous:** Baxter's word for sexual desire; rendered "lustful." **impetuous:** kept (forceful and rash). **temperance:** self-control in bodily appetites; kept.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): concur (to the overthrow), contribute; conceive, believe; needful, necessary; malignity, harmfulness; resolve (a question), settle; divines, theologians; preventeth, goes before; notation (of the name), derivation; denomination, name; distempers, disorders; use (and health), habit; covetous, greedy; felicity, happiness; "falling in with sense," "falling in with the senses"; "&c.," "etc."; "set on," kept. "Shall" in "I shall" becomes "will."
- **Scripture quotations** (NET Bible, fetched from labs.bible.org). Baxter's references are given in modern form but kept in his order (reference first, then quotation), since moving them would change his sentence order.
  - **Matthew 16:17:** NET "flesh and blood did not reveal this to you." Baxter's "Flesh and blood hath not revealed this unto thee" is the KJV; same sense.
  - **Matthew 26:41:** NET "The spirit is willing, but the flesh is weak." Same sense.
  - **John 3:6:** NET "What is born of the flesh is flesh." Baxter has "That which is born of the flesh is flesh" (KJV). Same sense.
  - **Romans 7:25:** NET "with my flesh I serve the law of sin." Same sense. The NET has "with my mind" as the contrast, which Baxter uses when he says the mind is captivated "so far."
  - **Romans 8:5 ("They that are of the flesh, mind the things of the flesh"):** this is the KJV rendering (Baxter's "of the flesh... mind"), kept in his wording, modernized only as "They that." The NET reads "For those who live according to the flesh have their outlook shaped by the things of the flesh." The NET's "live according to the flesh" and "outlook shaped" differ from "of the flesh" and "mind"; the sense Baxter draws (those in whom the fleshly interest is predominant) is not changed, though "mind" (KJV) carries the sense of setting the mind on, which "outlook" also conveys. He closes with "etc.," so his quotation is partial. His references "Romans 8:1, 5" and "verses 10, 11, 13" (where "the body" is spoken of) are as printed; Romans 8:10 in the NET says "your body is dead because of sin."
  - **"fleshly lusts, which fight against the spirit" (cited with 1 Peter 2:11 among others):** this is Baxter's own wording, modernized, not the NET. The NET of 1 Peter 2:11 reads "fleshly desires that do battle against the soul." Baxter's "spirit" where the verse has "soul" may be a blend with Galatians 5:17 (which he also cites); we have not confirmed. We kept his wording and flag the difference. His argument rests on the word "fleshly," which the NET keeps.
  - **"fleshly wisdom, making provision for the flesh":** Baxter's blend. "Fleshly wisdom" is the KJV wording of 2 Corinthians 1:12 (not cited here by Baxter); the NET there reads "human wisdom," so the word "flesh" does not appear. "Making provision for the flesh" echoes Romans 13:14, which he cites; the NET reads "make no provision for the flesh to arouse its desires." Kept in his modernized wording.
  - **Colossians 2:18:** Baxter refers to "a fleshly mind." The NET also reads "his fleshly mind," so the phrase is the same. The NET adds "puffed up with empty notions," which supports Baxter's gloss that it is a mind "deceived."
  - **Romans 8:1, 5-8, 13 and Romans 13:14** (end of the section): cited without quotation, kept as references.
- **Unmarked or unquoted references** kept as listed: 2 Peter 2:18 ("the lusts of the flesh"); Galatians 5:16, 17, 19; Ephesians 2:3; Romans 7:18; 1 Corinthians 5:5. "Rom. vii. 18" is as printed.
  - **NET flags on these:** 2 Peter 2:18 NET has "fleshly desires" (Baxter: "lusts of the flesh"); same sense. Ephesians 2:3 NET reads "indulging the desires of the flesh and the mind," which fits Baxter's point that the mind is involved but is named second. Romans 7:18 NET: "nothing good lives in me, that is, in my flesh." Baxter's quotations from these were not given, so nothing is compared.
- **"in the Old Testament":** Baxter's claim that he remembers no Old Testament place where "flesh" means only the unrenewed rational soul is his own, and we have not checked it.
- **Adam's appetite:** "his mouth did eat" is Baxter's wording; kept.
- **Cross-reference:** *Treatise of Self-Denial*, Baxter's own earlier book; not checked.
- **Source oddity:** the second sidenote reads "What flesh-pleasing is a sin" (sic); kept as printed.
