# Chapter 3: Grand Direction 1 (part 1 of the chapter)

**Chapter 3. The General Grand Directions for Walking with God, in a Life of Faith and Holiness: Containing the Essentials of Godliness and Christianity.**

I am next to direct you in that exercise of grace that is common to all Christians. Habits are for use: grace is given you, not only so that you may have it, but also so that you may use it. And it is fitting that we direct you how to use it, before we direct you how to know that you have it; because it is grace in exercise that you must discern; and habits are not perceived in themselves, but by their acts; and the more lively and powerful the exercise is, the more easily grace is perceived: so that this is the nearest and surest way to a certainty of our own sincerity:--he who uses grace most and best has the most grace; and he who has the most, and uses it most, may most easily be assured that he has it in sincerity and truth.

In these directions, I shall begin with those great internal duties, in which the very life of all religion consists; and the general practice of these principles and graces: and all these generals shall be briefly set together, for the ease of understanding and remembering them. And then I shall give you such particular directions as are needed, in subordination to those generals.

*In the margin: For a well-grounded faith.*

**Grand Direction 1. Strive to understand well the nature, grounds, reason, and order of faith and godliness; and to believe on such grounds, so well understood, that they will not allow you to waver, or to entertain a contrary belief.**

Ignorance and ungrounded or ill-grounded persuasions in matters of religion are the cause that abundance of people deceive themselves with the empty name and dead profession of a faith and religion that they never really possessed. I know there are low degrees of knowledge, comparatively, in many who are true believers; and that there may be much love and holiness where knowledge is very small or narrow, as to the objective extent of it; and that there is a knowledge that puffs up, while charity edifies; and that in many who have the narrower knowledge, there may be the firmest faith and adherence to the truth, which will conquer in the time of trial. But yet I must tell you that the religion you profess is not, indeed, your own religion, if you do not know what it is, and do not know in some measure the true grounds and reasons why you should be of that religion. If you have only learned to say your creed, or repeat the words of Christian doctrine, while you do not truly understand the sense; or if you have no better reasons why you profess the Christian faith than the custom of the country, or the command of princes or governors, or the opinion of your teachers, or the example of your parents, friends, or neighbors; you are not Christians indeed. You have a human belief or opinion, which objectively is true; but subjectively, in yourselves, you have no true, divine belief. I confess, there may be some insufficient, yes, and mistaken reasons, which a true believer may mistakenly make use of for the proof of certain fundamental truths; but then that same man has some other reason for his acceptance of that truth, which is more sound: and his faith is sound because of those sound, infallible principles, though there is a mixture of some other reasons that are unsound. The true believer builds on the rock, and gives deep rooting to the holy seed (Matthew 7:24; 13:5-8). Though some deluded men may tell you that faith and reason are such enemies that they exclude each other as to the same object; and that the less reason you have to prove the truth of the things believed, the stronger and more praiseworthy your faith is; yet when it comes to the trial, you will find that faith is no unreasonable thing; and that God requires you to believe no more than you have sufficient reason for, to warrant you and bear you out; and that your faith can be no more than your perception of the reasons why you should believe; and that God does suppose reason when he infuses faith, and uses reason in the use of faith. Those who believe and do not know why, or know no sufficient reason to warrant their belief, take a fancy, an opinion, or a dream for faith. I know that many honest-hearted Christians are unable to argue for their religion, or to give others a satisfactory account of the reasons of their faith or hope; but yet they have a true apprehension of some solid reasons in themselves; and they are not Christians they know not why: and though their knowledge is small as to the number of propositions known, yet it always extends to all that is essential to Christianity and godliness, and they do not believe they know not what; and their knowledge is greater in intensity, and in its value and operation, than the knowledge of the most learned ungodly man in the world.

Though I may not here digress, or stay so long as to open to you at length the nature, grounds, reason, and method of faith and godliness which I am persuading you to understand, yet I shall first lay before you a few propositions, which will be useful to you when you are inquiring into these things, and then open them a little to you.

*Proposition* 1. A life of godliness is our living to God as God, as being absolutely devoted to him.

2. A life of faith is a living upon the unseen, everlasting happiness as purchased for us by Christ, with all the necessities for it, and freely given us by God.

3. The contrary life of sense and unbelief is a living, under the power of the senses or flesh, to this present world, for want of such believing apprehensions of a better world as should lift the soul to it, and conquer the fleshly inclination to things present.

4. Though man in innocence, needing no Redeemer, might live to God without faith in a Redeemer; yet fallen man is not only unable to redeem himself, but also unable to live to God without the grace of the Redeemer. It was not only necessary that he satisfy God's justice for us, so that God might pardon and save us without any wrong to his holiness, wisdom, or government; but also that he be our teacher by his doctrine and his life, and that he reveal from heaven the Father's will, and that in him we may see, as in an object before us, the wonderful condescending love and goodness of a reconciled God and Father, and that effectually he enlighten, sanctify, and quicken us by the operations of his word and Spirit, and that he protect and govern, justify and glorify us; and be the Head of restored man, as Adam was the root of fallen man, and as the fallen spirits had their head: and therefore we must wholly live upon him as the Mediator between God and man, and the only Savior by merit and by efficacy.

5. Faith is a knowledge by certain credible testimony or revelation from God by supernatural or extraordinary means.

6. The knowledge of things naturally revealed (as the cause by the effect, etc.) comes in order before the knowledge or belief of things revealed supernaturally.

7. It is a matter of natural revelation that there is a God; [80] that he is infinite in his immensity and eternity, in his power, wisdom, and goodness; that he is the First Cause and ultimate End of all things; that he is the Preserver and overruling Disposer of all things, and the supreme Governor of the rational world, and the great Benefactor of all mankind, and the special favorer and rewarder of those who truly love him, seek him, and obey him: also that the soul of man is immortal; and that there is a life of reward or punishment to come, and that this life is but preparatory to that: that man is bound to love God his Maker, and serve him with all his heart and might; and to believe that this labor is not empty: that we must do our best to know God's will, so that we may do it. This, with much more (of which some part was mentioned in chapter 1), is of natural revelation, which unbelievers may know.

8. There is so admirable a harmony and correspondence of natural divinity with supernatural, the natural leading toward the supernatural, and the supernatural fitting in so suitably where the natural ends, or falls short, or is defective, that it greatly helps us in the belief of supernatural divinity. [81] Indeed, as the law of nature was exactly fitted to man in his natural innocent state; so the law and way of grace in Christ is so admirably and exactly fitted to the state of fallen man for his recovery and salvation, that the experience man has of his sin and misery may greatly prepare him to perceive and believe this most suitable gospel or doctrine of recovery. And though it may not be called natural, as if it were fitted to innocent nature, or as if it were revealed by natural ordinary means, yet it may be so called, as it is exactly suited to the restoration of fallen miserable nature; even as Lazarus's restored soul, though supernaturally restored, was the most natural companion of his body; or as bread, or milk, or wine, though it should fall from heaven, is in itself the most natural food for man.

9. The same things in divinity that are revealed naturally to all are revealed again supernaturally in the gospel; and therefore may and must be the matter both of natural knowledge and of faith.

10. When the malicious tempter casts in doubts of a Deity, or other points of natural certainty, it so much discredits his suggestions as may help us much to reject them when at the same time he tempts us to doubt the truth of the gospel.

11. There are many needed accompaniments to the objects of a divine faith, which are the matter of a human faith. (Of which more shortly.)

12. Christ, as Mediator, is the way, or principal means to God, as coming to restore man to his Maker. And so faith in Christ is but the means to bring us to the love of God, though in time they are connected.

13. Knowledge and faith are the eye of the new creature, and love is the heart; there is no more spiritual wisdom than there is faith; and there is no more life, or acceptable quality, or lovableness, than there is love to God.

14. All truths in divinity are revealed in order to a holy life; both faith and love are the principles and springs of practice.

15. Practice affords such experience to a believing soul as may greatly confirm him in the belief of those supernatural revelations that he had before received without that help.

16. The everlasting enjoyment of God in glory, being the end of all religion, must be next the heart, and most in our eye, and must give life to our whole religion as its object, and move us in every duty.

17. The pleasing of God being also our end, and both of these (enjoying him and pleasing him) being attainable in some small foretastes in this life, the effort of our souls and lives must be by faith to exercise love and obedience; for in this way God is pleased and enjoyed.

18. All things in religion are fitted to the good of man, and nothing to his hurt: God does not command us to honor him by anything that would make us miserable; but by embracing and magnifying his love and grace. [82]

19. But yet it is his own revelation by which we must judge what is finally for our good or hurt; and we may not imagine that our shallow or easily deceived wit is sufficient to discern, without his word, what is best or worst for us; nor can we reasonably argue from any present temporal adversity or unpleasant bitterness in the means, "This is worst for us, and therefore it is not from the goodness of God:" but we must argue in such cases, "This is from the goodness and love of God, and therefore it is best."

20. The grand impediment to all religion and our salvation, which hinders our believing, loving, and obeying alike, is the excessive physical inclination to carnal self and present transitory things, cunningly proposed by the tempter to ensnare us, and divert and steal away our hearts from God and the life to come. The understanding of these propositions will much help you in discerning the nature and reason of religion.

*In the margin: To use Christ and live upon him as our Mediator.*

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows the Latin passages in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[80] "Laert." says of the magi that they did Deorum vacare cultui: signa statuasque reprehendere: et eorum imprimis, qui mares esse deos et fœminas dicunt, errores improbare. Signa et statuas ex disciplinæ instituto è medio tulisse: (English: that they devoted themselves to the worship of the gods; criticized images and statues; and above all disapproved the errors of those who say the gods are male and female; and that by the rule of their discipline they removed images and statues from their midst.) and that some thought that the Jews came from them, pp. 4, 6. And Laertius himself says to those who make Orpheus the first philosopher, Videant certe qui ita volunt, quo sit censendus nomine, qui Diis cuncta hominum vitia, et quæ rarò à turpibus quibusque et flagitiosis hominibus geruntur, ascribit, p. 4. (English: Let those who wish it so see by what name he should be reckoned, who ascribes to the gods all the vices of men, and things that are rarely done even by the basest and most shameless of men. Rendered loosely.) He says also that the said magi held, and Theopompus with them, that men should live again and become immortal. He says the like of many other sects. It is a thing most irrational to doubt the being of the unseen worlds, and of the more excellent inhabitants of them; when we consider that this low and little part of God's creation is so full of inhabitants: if a microscope will show your very eyes a thousand visible creatures which you could never see without it, nor know that they had any being, will you not allow the pure intellectual sight to go much further beyond your microscope?

[81] Thales' sayings in Laert. are, Animas esse immortales: Antiquissimum omnium entium Deus; ingenitu senim: Pulcherrimum mundus, à Deo enim factus: Maximum locus; capit enim omnia: Velocissimum mens; nam per universa discurrit: Fortissimum necessitas; cuncta enim superat. Sapientissimum tempus: invenit namque omnia. *Q.* Utrum prius factum nox an dies? *R.* Nox, una prius die. *Q.* Latet ne Deos homo male agens? *R.* Ne cogitans quidem. *Q.* Quid difficile? *R.* Seipsum noscere. *Q.* Quid facile? *R.* Ab alio moveri. *Q.* Quid suavissimum? *R.* Frui. *Q.* Quid Deus? *R.* Quod initio et sine caret. pp. 14, 20, 21. (English: Souls are immortal. The oldest of all beings is God, for he is unbegotten; the most beautiful thing is the world, for it was made by God; the greatest is place, for it holds all things; the swiftest is the mind, for it runs through all things; the strongest is necessity, for it overcomes all things; the wisest is time, for it finds out all things. Question: Which was made first, night or day? Answer: Night, by one day. Question: Does a man who acts wrongly escape the gods' notice? Answer: Not even if he only thinks it. Question: What is hard? Answer: To know oneself. Question: What is easy? Answer: To be moved by another. Question: What is sweetest? Answer: To enjoy. Question: What is God? Answer: That which lacks beginning and end. "ingenitu senim" is a misprint for "ingenitus enim"; the last answer is printed as in the source, apparently garbled; rendered loosely.)

[82] Conjungi vult nos inter nos, atque connecti per mutua beneficia charitatis: adeo ut tota justitia et præceptum hoc Dei, communis sit utilitas hominum. O miram clementiam Domini! O ineffabilem Dei benignitatem! Præmium nobis pollicetur, si nos invicem diligamus; id est, si nos ea præstemus invicem, quorum vicissim indigemus: et nos superbo et ingrato animo, ejus remittimur voluntati, cujus etiam imperium beneficium est. Hieron. ad Celant. (English: He wishes us to be joined to one another and connected by the mutual benefits of love, so that the whole of justice, and this precept of God, is the common good of men. O wonderful clemency of the Lord! O unspeakable kindness of God! He promises us a reward if we love one another; that is, if we do for one another the things that in turn we need: and we, with proud and ungrateful mind, are sent back to the will of him whose very command is a kindness. Jerome, to Celantia. Rendered loosely, especially the last clause.) See my book of the "Reasons of the Christian Religion."

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Heading:** the source gives the chapter title and two opening paragraphs before the first Grand Direction; they are kept here under the chapter title. The last line of the range (the margin note "To use Christ and live upon him as our Mediator") belongs to the next Grand Direction, which begins in the following section; it is kept here only because it falls on the last source line.
- **Matthew 7:24; 13:5-8:** Baxter gives only references and no quoted words ("builds on the rock, and gives deep rooting to the holy seed"), so this is his paraphrase of the images (the NET has a wise man who "built his house on rock" at 7:24, and seed on "good soil" versus "rocky ground" where it "did not have sufficient root" at 13:5-8). No NET wording is substituted and no sense is changed.
- **Unmarked allusion:** "a knowledge that puffs up, while charity edifies" (1 Corinthians 8:1). Kept in Baxter's wording (no NET text fetched for it, since he gives no reference); "charity" here means love, and "edifies" means builds up. "Lazarus's restored soul" alludes to John 11.
- **Word swaps (Baxter's word, then ours):** labour to strive; suffer (allow) to allow; stagger to waver; delude to deceive (first paragraph) and "deluded men" kept; christians to Christians; neighbours to neighbors; mistakingly to mistakenly; learnedst to most learned; fastest (faith) to firmest; vain to empty; lapsed to fallen; condescending kept; connexed to connected; amiableness to lovableness; appurtenances to accompaniments; deceivable to easily deceived; closing with to embracing; fruition to enjoyment; endeavour to effort; insnare to ensnare; favourer to favorer; addicted to devoted; infidels to unbelievers; Saviour to Savior; "prevalency of sense" to "the power of the senses"; "inordinate sensual inclination" to "excessive physical inclination" (see the glossary: sensual); "Prop." to "Proposition"; "anon" to "shortly"; "a thousand visible creatures" is as printed.
- **Technical terms kept:** objective and subjective (in the first paragraph, "objective" means as concerns the thing believed, "subjective" means as concerns the believer himself); intensively, rendered "in intensity"; habits (settled dispositions, as in the glossary, "habit"); divinity (theology; "natural divinity" is what can be known of God by reason, "supernatural divinity" what is known by revelation); carnal self; Mediator; the First Cause.
- **Faith and reason:** Baxter argues against those who say faith excludes reason. His "infuses" means God gives faith into the soul.
- **Source oddity:** Proposition 7 refers back to "chap. 1"; this probably means chapter 1 of this book, which we have not checked.
- **Footnotes 80 and 81:** "Laert." is probably Diogenes Laertius (*Lives of the Philosophers*); we have not confirmed the edition or the page numbers Baxter gives. The "magi" are the Persian priestly class as Laertius describes them. Thales is the early Greek philosopher. The Q-and-A in note 81 is in Baxter's text; the final answer reads oddly in the Latin and may be corrupt.
- **Footnote 82:** the author is given as "Hieron. ad Celant." (probably Jerome's letter to Celantia); we have not verified it. "Reasons of the Christian Religion" is Baxter's own book.
- **Margin note:** the source places "For a well-grounded faith" before the Grand Direction heading, as rendered above.
