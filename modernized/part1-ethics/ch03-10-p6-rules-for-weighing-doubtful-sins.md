# Chapter 3: Grand Direction X (part 6 of 6)

*This section begins in the middle of Direction 27 ("Make not your own judgments or consciences your law"): it opens with Rule 6 of Baxter's rules for weighing one doubtful sin against another, and carries the rules through Rule 16. It ends with the margin note that begins the next section.*

**Rule 6.** Where I am equally uncertain of the duty to be omitted and of the sin to be committed, it is a greater sin to venture doubtfully upon committing a positive sin that is great (in case it proves a sin) than upon omitting a duty that (in case it proves a duty) is less; and on the contrary, it is worse to venture on omitting a great duty than on committing a small, positive sin. As, suppose my own or my neighbor's house is on fire, and I am in doubt whether I may take another man's water to quench it against his will; or if my own, or my child's, or my neighbor's life is in danger by famine, and I doubt whether I may take another man's apples, or pears, or heads of grain, or his bread, against his will, to save my own life or another's. Really, the thing is already made lawful or unlawful (which I now do not determine) by the law of God; but in my unavoidable uncertainty (if I am equally doubtful on both sides) it is a far greater sin (if it proves a sin) to omit the saving of the house or life than to take another man's water, or fruit, or bread, who has plenty (if this proves the sin). So if a king and nobles were in a ship, which would be taken and all destroyed by pirates unless I told a lie, and said, they are other persons; if I were equally in doubt which course to take, to lie or not (though sin has more evil than all our lives have good), yet a sinful omitting to save all their lives is a greater sin than a sinful telling of such a lie. Suppose I am in doubt whether I may lawfully save an ox, or donkey, or a man's life by labor on the Sabbath day? or David had doubted whether he might eat the consecrated showbread in his need? it is clear that the sinful neglect of a man's life is worse than the sinful violation of a Sabbath, or the sinful use of the consecrated bread. If I equally doubt whether I may use a ceremony, or a disorderly, defective form of prayer, and whether I should preach the gospel to save men's souls, where there are not others enough to do it; it is clear that sinfully to use a ceremony, or disorderly form of prayer, is, _cæteris paribus_, a lesser sin than sinfully to neglect to preach the gospel and to save men's souls. On the other side, suppose I lived in Italy, and could not have permission to preach the gospel there unless I would subscribe to the Trent Confession, or the 3rd canon of the Lateran Council under Innocent III.; one of which requires men to swear for transubstantiation, and to interpret the Scriptures only according to the unanimous consent of the fathers (who never unanimously consented in any exposition of the greatest part of the Scriptures at all); the other decrees the pope's deposing temporal lords, and releasing their subjects from their allegiance. On the one side, I doubt whether by subscribing I do not become guilty of justifying idolatry, perjury, and rebellion, and make myself guilty of the perjury of many thousand others: on the other side, I doubt whether I may disobey my superiors who command me this subscription, and may refrain from preaching the gospel, when yet I think that there are others to preach it, and that my worth is not so considerable that there would be any great loss in putting me out and putting in another; and God does not need me to do him service, but has instruments at command; and that I do not know how soon he may restore my liberty, or that I may serve him in another country, or else in sufferings at home; in such a case the sinful justifying of perjury or rebellion in whole countries is a far greater sin than the sinful omission of my preaching: for he that justifies perjury destroys the bonds of all societies, and turns the subjects loose against their sovereigns. Or if I, being a minister, were forbidden to preach the gospel where there is need, unless I would commit some sin; if I doubt on one side whether I should disobey my superiors, and on the other whether I should give up my calling, and neglect the souls of sinners; it is a lesser sin, _cæteris paribus_, to disobey a man sinfully than to disobey God, and to be cruel to the souls of men to their ruin sinfully. Or if I have made a vow, and sworn that I will throw away a penny or a shilling, and I am in doubt on one side whether I am not bound to keep it as a vow, and on the other whether it is not a sin to keep it, because to throw away any of my talents is a sin; in this case, the sinful throwing away of a penny or a shilling is not so great a sin as sinful perjury. If Daniel and the three witnesses had been in equal doubt whether they should obey the king or pray to God (as in Daniel 6) and renounce bowing to his idol (Daniel 3), the sinful giving up of prayer as then commanded, and the sinful bowing to the idol, would have been a greater sin than a sinful disobeying of the king's command in such a case, if they had been mistaken.

**Rule 7.** If I cannot discern whether the duty to be omitted, or the sin to be committed, is materially and in other respects the greater, then that will be to me the greater of the sins which my doubting conscience most strongly suspects to be sin, in its most impartial deliberation. For if other things are equal, certainly sinning against more or less conviction or doubt must make an inequality. As, if I could not discern whether my subscription to the Trent Confession, or my refraining from preaching, or my preaching though prohibited, would be the greater sin, in case they were all sinful; but yet I am most strongly suspicious of sinfulness in the subscription, and less suspicious of sinfulness in my refraining in such a case from preaching, and least of all suspicious of sinfulness in my preaching though prohibited: in this case to subscribe sinfully is the greatest sin, and to refrain sinfully from exercising my office is the next, and to preach without warrant is the least.

**Rule 8.** If I could perceive no difference in the degrees of evil in the omission and the commission, nor yet in the degrees of my suspicion or doubting, then that is the greater sin which I had greater help and evidence to have known, and did not.

**Rule 9.** If greater material evil is on one side than on the other, and greater suspicion or evidence of the sinfulness also, then that must needs be the greater sin.

**Rule 10.** If the greatness of the material evil is on one side, and the greatness of the suspicion and evidence is on the other, then the former (if sin) will be materially, and in itself considered, the worst; but the latter will be formally the greater disobedience to God. But the comparison will be very difficult. As, suppose that I swear to God that I will throw away a shilling, or that I will refrain from praying for a week together; here I take perjury to be a greater sin than my throwing away a shilling, or refraining from prayer a week: but when I question whether the oath should be kept or not, I have greater suspicion that it should not than that it should, because no oath may be the bond of the least iniquity. Here, if not keeping it proves a sin, I shall do that which is the greater sin in itself if I do not keep it; but I shall show more disobedience in keeping it, if it is not to be kept.

**Rule 11.** If it is a double sin that I suspect on one side, and but a single one on the other, it makes an inequality in the case. As, suppose that in my father's family there are heretics and drunkards, and I swear that in my place and calling I will make every effort to cast them out. My mother approves my vow; my father is against it, and releases me from it because I did not make it by his advice. On one side, I doubt whether I am bound, or may act against my father's will: on the other side, I as much doubt whether I am not perjured, and disobedient to my mother, if I do not do it, and whether I do not disobey God, who made it my duty to make an effort in the thing in my place and calling before I vowed it.

**Rule 12.** There is a great deal of difference between omitting the substance of a duty forever, and delaying it, or altering the time, and place, and manner. For instance, that which will justify or excuse me for shortening my prayer, or for praying but once a day, or at noon rather than in the morning, or for defect in method, or fervency, or expressions, may not justify or excuse me for denying, renouncing, or long refraining from prayer. And that which may excuse an apostle for not preaching in the temple or synagogues, or not having the emperor's or the high priest's permission or consent, or for not continuing in one city or country; would not excuse them if they had renounced their callings, or totally, as to all times, and places, and manner of performance, had ceased their work for fear of men.

**Rule 13.** If the duty to be omitted and the sin to be committed seem equal in greatness, and our doubt is equal as to both, it is commonly held safer to avoid the commission more carefully than the omission. For which many reasons are given.

**Rule 14.** There is usually much more matter for fear and suspicion, _cæteris paribus_, of sins to be committed than of duties to be omitted, when the commission is made necessary to the doing of the duty. Both because it is there that the fear begins: for I am certain that the good work is no duty to me if the act is a sin which is its necessary condition. Therefore, so far as I suspect the act to be sinful, I must needs suspect the duty to be no duty to me at that time: it is not possible that I should be rationally more persuaded that the duty is my duty than that the condition is no sin. If it were the saving of the lives of all men in the country, I could no further take it to be my duty than I take that to be no sin by which it must be done, it being a thing beyond controversy that we must not sin for the accomplishment of any good whatsoever. And also because the sin is supposed to be always sin, but few duties are at all times duties: and the sin is a sin to every man, but the duty may be another man's duty, and not mine. For instance: Charles V. imposes the Interim upon Germany: some pastors yielded to it; others refused it, and were cast out. Those who yielded pleaded the good of the churches, and the prevention of their utter desolation, but yet confessed that if the thing imposed was sinful, it was not their duty to do it for any good whatsoever, but to seek the good of the church as well as they could without it. The others, who were cast out, argued that so far as they were confident the Interim was sinful, they must be confident that nothing was their duty that could not be done without it, and that God knew best what is good for his church, and there is no accomplishing its good by sin and God's displeasure; and that they did not therefore forsake their ministry, but only lost the ruler's license; for they resolved to preach in one place or other until they were imprisoned, and God can serve himself by their imprisonment or death as well as by their preaching. And while others took their places who thought the Interim lawful, the churches were not wholly destitute; and if God saw it fitting, he could restore their fuller liberties again: in the meantime, to serve him, as all pastors did for three hundred years after Christ, without the license of the civil magistrate, was not to cast away their office. Another instance: the zealous papists in the reign of Henry III. in France thought that there was a necessity of entering the League, and making war against the king, because religion was in danger, the preservation of which is an unquestionable duty. The learned and moderate lawyers who were against them said that, there being no question but the king had the total sovereignty over them, they were sure it was a sin to resist the higher powers, and therefore no preservation of religion could be a duty or lawful to them which must be done by such a certain sin: sin is not the means to save religion or the commonwealth.

**Rule 15.** When a thing is not prohibited and sinful simply in itself, but because of some accidental or consequential evil that it tends to, there a greater accidental or consequential good may outweigh the evil, and make the thing become no sin, but a duty. It is a matter of exceeding difficulty to discern often whether a thing is simply and absolutely forbidden, or only by accident and changeably, and to discern which accident outweighs. There are so many observations that should here be taken in, and so much of a man's life and peace is concerned in it, that it deserves a treatise by itself. And therefore I shall not meddle with it any further here, lest an insufficient treatise be worse than none, in a case where error is so easy and dangerous.

**Rule 16.** As to the danger of the sinner himself, there is a great deal of difference between an error and sin of human frailty, when the service of God, and true obedience, and the common good are sincerely intended, and an error and sin of false-heartedness and sloth, when selfishness is the secret spring of the error, and carnal interest the real end, though God and his service are claimed. And usually the accompanying circumstances will show something of this to others. For instance, two magistrates and two ministers submit to some questioned imposition; all claim that the glory of God and his service is what moves them to submit. The one of the magistrates faithfully serves God afterward with his authority, and shows thereby that he meant it sincerely: the other does no good in his place, and shows his hypocrisy. One of the ministers preaches zealously, and privately labors as one who thirsts for the saving of souls: the other preaches formally, and coldly, and heartlessly, and never converts a soul, and neglects the work which he claimed was his end.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture:** no verse is quoted in this section, so nothing was fetched from the NET and there are no NET flags. Cited only: Daniel 6 (Daniel's prayer; Baxter's "Dan. vi.") and Daniel 3 (bowing to the idol; "Dan. iii."). Baxter's "Daniel and the three witnesses" means Daniel and his three companions (Daniel 3, 6).
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): saving an ox or ass on the Sabbath (compare Luke 14:5 and Matthew 12:11, which are not cited by Baxter); David and the consecrated showbread (compare 1 Samuel 21:1-6); "we must not sin for the accomplishment of any good whatsoever" (compare Romans 3:8, not cited by Baxter); "no oath may be the bond of the least iniquity" (a principle, not a quotation).
- **Beginning in the middle:** the file begins at Rule 6 and the Direction heading is not repeated. Numbering follows Baxter (Rule VI to XVI, rendered as Rule 6 to 16). The last lines are a margin note and a divider that belong to the next section (loving God), kept here because they fall in the line range.
- **_cæteris paribus_:** Latin, kept as printed ("other things being equal"; draft translation).
- **materially / formally** (Rules 7, 10): scholastic terms. "Materially" refers to the thing done in itself (its own size of evil); "formally" refers to the act as willed against what the conscience perceives, that is, the disobedience in it. Kept; Rule 10 itself shows the contrast.
- **commission / omission:** doing a forbidden thing / neglecting a required one. Kept.
- **positive sin:** a sin of doing something forbidden (as opposed to omitting a duty). Kept.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): hath / needeth etc. to has / needs; ass to donkey; shew-bread to showbread; ears of corn to heads of grain; labour to labor; neighbour to neighbor; endeavour to make an effort (Rule 11, "endeavour to cast them out" and "the thing"); dischargeth to releases; disobliging to releasing; apprehend to think; considerable to considerable (kept); perdition to ruin; unwarrantably to without warrant; preponderate to outweigh; ofttimes to often; tractate to treatise; meet to fitting; concomitants to accompanying circumstances; pretended / pretend to claimed / claim; studiously to carefully; licence to license; allowance (permission) to permission; necessity (need) to need (Rule 6, "where there is necessity" in the sense of need); alterably to changeably. "Doubting" and "suspicion" are kept as Baxter's words for the degree of one's uncertainty or fear that something is sin.
- **papists, heretics:** Baxter's own words; his polemic against Rome ("justifying idolatry," the Trent Confession, "the pope's deposing temporal lords") is kept as he wrote it. These are his characterizations, not ours.
- **Trent Confession; the 3rd canon of the Lateran Council under Innocent III:** Baxter's references. The Trent Confession is probably the profession of faith associated with the Council of Trent, but we have not checked. We have not confirmed his description of the Lateran canon. His claims about what they require are his.
- **Charles V. and the Interim; Henry III. and the League:** Baxter's examples, kept as he tells them. The Interim presumably refers to the religious settlement imposed by Charles V in Germany in the 1540s, and the League to the Catholic League in France in the 1500s; we make no claims beyond what Baxter says, and have not verified his account of what the pastors and lawyers argued.
- **Rule 15 (a treatise by itself):** Baxter declines to treat the matter further here; we do not know whether he did so elsewhere.
- **Rule 6, on the Trent subscription:** the sentence is long and uses a run of "that" clauses ("and that my worth is not so considerable...; and that I do not know how soon...") that depend on "I think" earlier in the sentence; we kept his structure.
- **Rule 12 ("an apostle ... the emperor's or the high priest's permission"):** Baxter's example from the book of Acts; not cited by him.
- **Rule 11:** the sentence "I as much doubt whether I am not perjured ..." is left as he wrote it, including "the thing" for the task of casting out heretics and drunkards.
- **"the sinful giving up of prayer as then commanded"** (end of Rule 6): Baxter's "forbearance of prayer as then commanded" means prayer as the king then forbade it, that is, giving up prayer because the king commanded it.
