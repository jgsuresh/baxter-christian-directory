# Chapter 9: The Sins of the Tongue (Items 8–30) and Direction 4

*This section begins partway through Direction 3 ("Understand and remember what are the sins of the tongue"), at item 8 of Baxter's list of the sins of the tongue.*

8. Lying also is a great and common sin of the tongue: of which more later.

9. Another sin of the tongue is hypocritical dissembling, which is worse than mere lying: when men's tongues do not agree with their hearts, but speak good words in prayer to God, or conversation with men, to cover evil intentions or affections, and to represent themselves to the hearers as better than they are.

10. Another is ostentation or proud boasting, either of men's wit and learning, or greatness, or riches, or honor, or strength, or beauty, or abilities, or piety, or anything that men are proud of. [481] As the faithful "make their boast in God" (Psalm 34:2; 44:8), and in the "cross of ... Christ," by which "they are crucified to the world" (Galatians 6:14); so the greedy "boast in their great riches" (Psalm 49:6), and the "workers of iniquity boast against the righteous, and the proud triumph and speak hard things" (Psalm 94:2–4). "Even against the Lord" do they boast, in their boasting against his people (Ezekiel 35:13). So far as pride prevails with men, they are apt to "boast themselves to be somebody" (Acts 5:36). Either openly, as the more foolish do, or cunningly by the help of fair pretenses, as the more ingenious proud ones do.

11. Another sin of the tongue is untimely speaking of common things when holy things should be preferred; as on the Lord's day, or at the time of public worship, or when the company, occasion, or opportunity call for holy speeches: worldly people are talking, as Saul, of their donkeys, when they should talk of a kingdom (1 Samuel 9–10). To speak about your callings and common affairs is lawful, provided it is moderately and in season; but when you talk all of the world and emptiness, and are never done, and will scarcely have any other talk in your mouths, and even on God's day will "speak your own words" (Isaiah 58:13), this is profane and sinful speaking.

12. Another common sin of the tongue is a tempting and persuading others to sin, enticing them to gluttony, drunkenness, wantonness, fornication, or any other crime; as men that "not only do them but also approve of those who practice them" (Romans 1:32). This is to be the instruments and servants of the devil, and most directly to do his work in the world. The same I may say of unjust excusing, minimizing, or defending the sins of others, or commanding, alluring, frightening, or encouraging them to it.

13. Another is a carnal manner of handling the sacred things of God, as when it is done with lightness, or with unsuitable elaborateness of words, or in a joking, trifling manner, especially by the preachers of the gospel themselves; and not with a style that is grave and serious, agreeable to the weight and majesty of the truth.

14. Another is an imprudent, rash, and slovenly handling of holy things; when they are spoken of so ignorantly, unskillfully, disorderly, or emotionally, as tends to dishonor them, and frustrate the desired good success. [482]

15. Another sin of the tongue is the reviling or dishonoring of superiors; when children speak irreverently and dishonorably to or of their parents; or subjects of their governors; or servants of their masters, either to their faces, or behind their backs. "They are not afraid to insult the glorious ones" (2 Peter 2:10; Jude 8).

16. Another is the imperious contempt of inferiors, domineering over them, provoking and discouraging them. Ephesians 6:4, "Fathers, do not provoke your children to anger."

17. Another sin of the tongue is idle talk and a multitude of useless words; a babbling talkativeness, or unprofitableness of speech; when it is speech that tends to no edification, nor any good use for mind, or body, or affairs.

18. Another sin is foolish talk, or jesting in levity and folly, which tends to possess the minds of the hearers with a disposition of levity and folly like the speakers. Ephesians 5:4, "... foolish talk, or coarse jesting—all of which are out of character." Honest mirth is lawful; and that is the best which is most sanctified, as being from a holy principle, and about a holy matter, or to a holy end: as "Rejoice in the Lord always" (Philippians 4:4). "Is anyone in good spirits? He should sing praises" (James 5:13). But such a light and frothy jesting, as is but the outlet of habitual levity by idle words, is not allowable. But especially those persons most odiously abuse their tongues and reason, who imitate idiots or fools, and use their wit to cover their jests with a seeming folly, to make them the more ridiculous, and make it their very profession to be the jesters of great men. They make a trade of heinous sin.

19. Another sin is "vulgar speech" (Ephesians 5:4); obscene and ribald talk; which the apostle calls "corrupt or rotten talk" (Ephesians 4:29); when wanton, filthy minds make themselves merry with wanton, filthy speeches. This is the devil's preparation for whoredom and all abominable uncleanness; for when the tongue is first taught to make an amusement of such filthy sins, and the ear to be delighted in it, or be indifferent to it, there remains but a small step to actual filthiness.

20. Another sin of the tongue is cursing; when men wish some harm causelessly or unwarrantably to others. If you speak but in a passion or in jest, and do not desire for them in your hearts the hurt which you name, it is nevertheless a sin of the tongue, as it is to speak blasphemy or treason in a passion or in jest; the tongue must be ruled as well as the heart. But if really you desire the hurt which you wish them, it is so much the worse. But it is worst of all, when emotional, factious men will turn their very prayers into cursings, calling for fire from heaven, and praying for other men's destruction or hurt; and claiming Scripture examples for it; as if they might do it unwarrantably, which others have done in other cases in a warranted manner.

21. Slandering is another sin of the tongue; when out of malice and ill will, men speak evil falsely of others to make them odious or do them hurt: or else through uncharitable credulity, easily believe a false report, and so report it again to others; or through rashness and unruliness of tongue, divulge it, before they test it, or receive either just proof, or any warranted call to mention it.

22. Another sin is backbiting and spreading ill reports behind men's backs, without any warrant. Whether the matter is true or false, as long as you either do not know it to be true, or if you do, yet spread it to make the person less respected, or at least without a sufficient cause, it is a sin against God, and a wrong to men.

23. Another sin is rash censuring, when you speak that evil of another which you have but an uncharitable surmise of; and take that to be probable which is but possible, or that to be certain which is but probable against another. [483]

24. Another sin is railing, reviling, or emotional, provoking words, which tend to the lessening of charity, and the breach of peace, and the stirring up of discord, and of a return of railing words from others, contrary to the love, and patience, and meekness, and gentleness which become saints.

25. Another sin is cheating, deceiving, overreaching words; when men use their tongues to defraud their neighbors, in bargaining for their own gain.

26. Another sin of the tongue is false witness-bearing, and false accusing; a sin which cries to God for vengeance, who is the justifier of the innocent.

27. Another sin of the tongue is the passing of an unrighteous sentence in judgment: when rulers absolve the guilty or condemn the just, and call evil good and good evil, and say to the righteous, "You are wicked" (Proverbs 24:24).

28. Another sin of the tongue is flattery; which is the more heinous the more hurtful it is. And it is most hurtful, 1. When it tends to delude men in the greatest things, even the state of their souls. The flattery of a preacher that deceives men as in the name of Christ is of all other flattery the most pernicious; to make the unregenerate believe that they are regenerate, and the ungodly to believe that they are godly, and the unjustified to believe that they are justified, and the children of Satan to believe that without conversion they may be saved; to make a worldling, a swearer, a glutton, a drunkard, a fornicator, a formal hypocrite, or a hater of holiness, believe that such as he may come to heaven without the sanctifying, renewing work of the Holy Spirit; this is the most outstanding service of the devil that the tongue of any man can do him, unless it be the very open opposers of religion. As the devil is more accustomed to flatter men to hell than to frighten them there, so are his ministers and instruments. And all doctrines of libertinism and looseness, which warrant men to do evil and to neglect a holy life, are of the two a more dangerous way of flattery than that which consists only in misapplication. Thus also carnal friends are accustomed to flatter a sinner into presumption and false hopes, when they see him convicted of his sin and misery, and say, Do not trouble yourself; God is merciful, and you have lived well, and been a good neighbor, and done nobody harm, and if such as you are not saved, God help a great many. Thus when a convicted sinner is striving to get out of the devil's snares, the servants of Satan rock him to sleep again, by false and flattering speeches and deceit. 2. Flattering is pernicious when it tends to the hurt of many; as when rulers are deceived and perverted by it to the destruction of the people and themselves. [484] Proverbs 26:28, "A lying tongue hates those crushed by it, and a flattering mouth works ruin." See 1 Thessalonians 2:5; Ezekiel 12:24; Psalm 12:2–3.

29. Another sin is a jeering, mocking, deriding, or scorning at others, either for their weaknesses of body or mind, or for their virtues, or through envy and malice, or pride, or a custom of deriding, scornful speech. "Mockers delighted in mockery" (Proverbs 1:22). See Psalm 22:7; 44:13; 79:4. Especially when sinners scorn at the reproofs and counsels of the godly, and cast them all back into their faces with contempt; for "whoever corrects a mocker is asking for insult" (Proverbs 9:7–8). "The scorner will not love one who corrects him" (Proverbs 15:12).

30. Another tongue sin is idolatry or false worship; the praise of idols, or praying to them, or making songs, or speeches, or disputes for them; as also the false worship of the true God. These among others are the sins of the tongue to be avoided. No wonder if there are yet more, for the "tongue represents" ὁ κοσμος της αδικιας, "the world of wrongdoing" (James 3:6).

**Direction 4. When you have thus understood the duties and sins of the tongue, and the greatness of them, the next thing which you must be most careful and diligent about is that you keep all that upon the heart which should be upon the tongue, and keep the heart clean from that which the tongue must be kept clean from. [485]**

The principal work must be about the heart; for "the mouth speaks from what fills the heart." 1. The tongue will be effectively governed in no other way; if the heart is upon the world, the tongue will most commonly be upon the world; you may force it a little against your hearts, but it will be to a very inconstant obedience; when you ever so little loosen the reins it is gone. If the heart is proud, the tongue will speak proudly; if the heart is lustful, or empty, or malicious, the words will ordinarily be so too. 2. Or if you can force the tongue to go against the heart, it is but a hypocritical reformation. An empty, a proud, a worldly, a wanton, a malicious or ungodly heart will condemn you, though the tongue was forced to speak humbly, chastely, patiently, or piously. Therefore if you would overcome the emptiness, or worldliness, or wantonness, or any other corruption of your speech, first set yourselves to overcome the same corruption in your hearts, and to revive and activate the contrary graces. And if you would use your tongues to the honor of God, and the edification of men, wind up the spring of those holy affections which must be as water to the mill. It is the use of the tongue to express the mind: and it is the use of holy speech to be the expression of a holy mind. And do you think to express that which you do not have? Will you make a duty of a lie? If you would speak of Christ or heaven with seriousness, see that your hearts are seriously set upon Christ and heaven. When you go into any company where you should speak for God, and for the hearers' good, try beforehand to get a deep impression on your hearts of those attributes or truths of God which you would express; and to revive the sense of that upon yourselves which you would make others aware of. Stir up within you the love of God, and the love of holiness and truth, and a love of the souls of those you speak to; and then you will be as a conduit which runs as soon as the tap is turned, because it is always full of water.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows each Latin passage in parentheses; the translations were prepared for this project and are not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check them before relying on fine points.

[481] Quod facere instituis noli prædicare: nam si facere nequiveris, rideberis. Pittaci Sent. in Laert. (Do not announce what you intend to do: for if you are unable to do it, you will be laughed at. A saying of Pittacus, in Laertius.)

[482] Didymus Alex., on James 3, of bridling the tongue, says, Non putandum est de peccato prolativi sermonis, quæ solœcismos et barbarismos quidam vocant, hæc fuisse dicta. (It must not be thought that these things were said about the sin of spoken language which some call solecisms and barbarisms. Rendered loosely.)

[483] Existimant loquacitatem esse facundiam, et maledicere omnibus, bonæ conscientiæ signum arbitrantur. Hieron Cont. Helvid. (They think talkativeness is eloquence, and they judge speaking evil of everyone to be a sign of a good conscience. Jerome, *Against Helvidius*.)

[484] Indignum hominem divitiarum gratiâ laudare noli. Bias in Laert. (Do not praise an unworthy man for the sake of his riches. Bias, in Laertius.)

[485] Loqui quæ sentis, et sentire quæ loqueris, ut Seneca. (To speak what you think, and to think what you speak, as Seneca [says].)--Fidum nihil lingua loqui valet, dum cordi duplex altè insedit sensus. Sent. Pittaci in Laertio. (The tongue can say nothing trustworthy while a double mind sits deep in the heart. A saying of Pittacus, in Laertius.) Bias percontanti homini impio quid esset pietas, nihil respondet; cumque ille silentii causam sciscitaretur, quia, inquit, de rebus nihil ad te pertinentibus quæris. Laert. (When an impious man asked Bias what piety was, he answered nothing; and when the man inquired the reason for his silence, "Because," he said, "you are asking about things that have nothing to do with you." Laertius.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). Every NET quotation below was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Title:** Chapter 9 is divided into six "Titles" (Baxter's "_Tit._"), meaning headings or divisions. This file falls within Title 1, the general directions for the tongue.
- **Where this file starts and ends:** it begins at item 8 of the list under Direction 3 (items 1–7 are in the previous file) and ends at the close of the paragraph under Direction 4.
- **"of which more later"** (item 8): Baxter's "of which more anon." Lying is treated in a later Title of this chapter (not checked here).
- **Psalm 34:2; 44:8** (item 10): "make their boast in God" is Baxter's wording, modernized, kept because it adapts two verses into the third person. The NET has "I will boast in the Lord" (34:2) and "In God we boast all day long" (44:8).
- **Galatians 6:14** (item 10): "cross of ... Christ" is the NET ("the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ"), shortened as Baxter shortens it. "They are crucified to the world" is Baxter's third-person adaptation and is kept as his; the NET has "through which the world has been crucified to me, and I to the world."
- **Psalm 49:6** (item 10): NET wording. Baxter's King James wording was "boast themselves in the multitude of their riches."
- **Psalm 94:2–4** (item 10): Baxter's wording, modernized, kept because it is a blend of the verses and the NET does not contain "against the righteous" or "hard things." The NET reads: "how long will the wicked celebrate? They spew out threats and speak defiantly; all the evildoers boast."
- **Ezekiel 35:13** (item 10): "Even against the Lord" is Baxter's own framing, kept; the NET has the Lord speaking: "You exalted yourselves against me with your speech."
- **Acts 5:36** (item 10): Baxter's wording kept. The NET reads "claiming to be somebody" (of Theudas). Baxter's point rests on the King James word "boasting," which the NET does not have; the NET's "claiming" still fits his point about pride but loses the link to boasting.
- **1 Samuel 9–10** (item 11): Saul looking for his father's donkeys ("asses" in Baxter) when Samuel was about to anoint him king. Baxter cites "1 Sam. ix. x."
- **Isaiah 58:13** (item 11): "speak your own words" is Baxter's (King James: "nor speaking thine own words"), kept, because the NET reads the phrase differently ("refraining from ... making business deals"). Baxter's application to idle talk on the Lord's day rests on the King James wording.
- **Romans 1:32** (item 12): NET wording. The King James has "have pleasure in them that do them"; the NET's "approve of those who practice them" makes the same point Baxter draws (encouraging others' sin).
- **2 Peter 2:10; Jude 8** (item 15): NET wording from 2 Peter 2:10. **The NET changes the sense Baxter leans on:** the King James "speak evil of dignities" let Baxter apply the verse to earthly superiors (parents, rulers, masters); the NET's "insult the glorious ones" is commonly understood as referring to angelic beings rather than human authorities. Jude 8 in the NET likewise reads "insult the glorious ones."
- **Ephesians 5:4** (items 18 and 19): NET wording. The King James had "filthy speaking" (item 19), "foolish talking," and "jesting, which are not convenient" (item 18). The NET's "vulgar speech" and "coarse jesting" are narrower: the NET limits the jesting forbidden to coarse jesting, while Baxter reads the verse as against light, idle jesting in general, while still allowing "honest mirth."
- **Philippians 4:4; James 5:13** (item 18): NET wording. The King James had "If any be merry, let him sing psalms"; the NET has "He should sing praises." Baxter's point (godly mirth) is unaffected.
- **Ephesians 4:29** (item 19): "corrupt or rotten talk" is Baxter's double rendering (King James "corrupt communication," plus his own "rotten," which is the literal sense of the Greek word), kept as his. The NET has "no unwholesome word." "Communication" (talk) is changed to "talk."
- **Proverbs 24:24** (item 27): "You are wicked" is Baxter's own inversion, kept. The verse in both the King James and the NET is about saying to the guilty, "You are innocent"; Baxter turns it to cover the opposite case. "Call evil good and good evil" is an unmarked allusion to Isaiah 5:20, kept in Baxter's wording.
- **Proverbs 26:28** (item 28): NET wording ("those crushed by it" for the King James "those that are afflicted by it").
- **Proverbs 1:22; 9:7–8; 15:12** (item 29): NET wording. The NET has "mockers" where the King James had "scorners" (1:22, 9:7), and "The scorner" at 15:12. At 9:7 Baxter's "getteth himself a blot" blends the two halves of the King James verse ("getteth to himself shame ... getteth himself a blot"); we give the NET's first half, "whoever corrects a mocker is asking for insult," which carries his point.
- **James 3:6** (item 30): Baxter gives the Greek ὁ κοσμος της αδικιας (kept as printed, without accents) and translates it "a world of iniquity" (King James). We give the NET's English around his Greek. The NET's "represents the world of wrongdoing among the parts of our bodies" is close to his sense that the tongue holds a whole world of evils.
- **"the mouth speaks from what fills the heart"** (Direction 4): NET wording of Matthew 12:34 (the same words end Luke 6:45). Baxter gave no reference; the reference is added here only. King James: "out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh."
- **Psalm 12:2–3; Psalm 22:7; 44:13; 79:4; 1 Thessalonians 2:5; Ezekiel 12:24:** references only in Baxter, converted to modern form.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): anon, later; conference, conversation; parts, abilities; pretences, pretenses; unseasonable, untimely; worldlings, worldly people; asses, donkeys; vanity/vain, emptiness/empty; so it be, provided it is; extenuating, minimizing; affrighting, frightening; thereto, to it; curiosity (of words), elaborateness; ludicrous, toyish, joking, trifling; passionately/passionate, emotionally/emotional; unreverently, irreverently; insulting over, domineering over; loquacity, talkativeness; vent, outlet; counterfeit (verb), imitate; preparative, preparation; sport, amusement; mischief, harm; pretending, claiming; warrantable, warranted; try (a report), test; venting, spreading; diminution, lessening; eminent, outstanding; useth to, is accustomed to; thither, there; convinced, convicted (aware of his sin, not argued into a belief); infirmities, weaknesses; unconstant, inconstant; loose the reins, loosen the reins; actuate, activate; endeavour, try; sensible of, aware of; cock, tap.
- **"in a passion"** (item 20): Baxter's "in passion," meaning in a fit of anger or strong feeling.
- **Kept words:** "dissembling" (pretending, disguising one's real mind); "wanton, wantonness" (lustful, lewd); "censuring" (harsh judging of others); "railing" (abusive scolding); "charity" (Christian love); "affections" (emotions and desires, as in the Glossary); "carnal" (unspiritual); "formal hypocrite" (one whose religion is outward form only); "libertinism" (doctrine that frees people from moral duty); "misapplication" (wrongly applying true doctrine to a particular person); "ingenious" (clever); "in season" (at the proper time); "conduit" (a pipe or fountain carrying water).
- **"the jesters of great men"** (item 18): the professional fools kept in noble households.
- **Laertius; Pittacus; Bias** (notes 481, 484, 485): Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of the Philosophers*, which records sayings of Pittacus and Bias, two of the traditional Seven Sages of Greece. We have not checked the exact Latin wording against an edition. The Seneca saying in note 485 is not located here. **Didymus Alex.** (note 482): probably Didymus the Blind of Alexandria; the work on James is not identified here. **Hieron Cont. Helvid.** (note 483): Jerome, *Against Helvidius*; the citation is not checked.
