# Chapter 8: Part 6, whether the love of sleep can be a reigning sin, and Directions 1–13 against excess of sleep

*This file begins partway through Part 6 (Directions against sinful excess of sleep), at Baxter's question whether the love of sleep can be a mortal sin; the reasons that go before it are in the previous file.*

*Question.* Can the love of sleep alone be the mortal, reigning sin in anyone? The reason of the doubt is, because the mortal sin is a sin of mistaken interest, that is, such as has a man's chief love, and is preferred before God; which it seems so small a thing as sleep or ease cannot be, but it seems a mere neglect or remissness in the way of duty, and not to be chosen as any man's happiness.

*Answer.* The sin that is set up against the love of God, as a man's ultimate end and happiness, is flesh-pleasing in general, or carnal self-love: and he that is guilty of this can hardly be imagined to exercise his physical desire only in the way of sloth and sleep. It is certain that he prefers the greatest pleasure of his flesh which he can attain before the less: and therefore as to the habit or inclination, he is as much addicted to greed, gluttony, ambition, or other ways of indulgence in physical pleasure; and if they are within his reach, that he can hope to attain them, he will actually desire such greater pleasures, more than this. For there is no man that is an unregenerate pleasure-seeker, that has mortified greed, luxury, and pride, and yet is captivated only by sleep or sloth: the same grace which truly mortifies the greater would mortify the less. But it is possible that a beggar, or some such person, that has no other physical pleasure but idleness in view or hope, may exercise his love of physical pleasure principally this way. Not that at root he does not prefer riches and honor before his beggarly sloth and ease; but those desires having no matter to work upon, do not stir in him, because he has no hope of reaching such a thing. The sum is, 1. Carnal self-love is the great opposite to the love of God. 2. This self-love works towards carnal pleasure, and to the greatest most. 3. Habitually therefore the love of riches, honor, and voluptuousness is stronger than the love of ease. 4. Actually the love of ease may be the strongest in some. 5. But if those persons were as capable of the higher fleshly pleasures, they would love them actually more. 6. It is not the omitting of some particular duties through the love of ease which proves such a pleasure-loving, unsanctified state of soul; but the preferring of men's ease before a holy life in the main; as when men so far love their ease that they will not make it the chief of their desires and employments to "pursue his kingdom and righteousness" (Matthew 6:33).

The overcoming of excessive sleep is easy, if you are but thoroughly willing.

**Direction 1. The first thing to be done is to correct that sluggish, phlegmatic temperament of body which inclines you to it, which is chiefly to be done by such an abstinence or temperate diet as I gave directions for before.**

A full belly is fit for nothing else but sleep or lust. Reduce your diet to that measure which is needful to your health, and do not eat any more to please your appetites. And let fasting cure you when you have exceeded.

**Direction 2. Labor hard in your callings, that your sleep may be sweet while you are in it; or else you will lie in bed on pretense of necessity, because you cannot sleep well when you are there.**

Then you will say you must take it out in the morning, because you do not sleep in the night. But see that this is not caused by idleness. Weary your bodies in your daily labors; for "the sleep of the laborer is pleasant" (Ecclesiastes 5:12).

**Direction 3. See that you have a calling which will find you employment for all your time which God's direct service spares.**

Indeed, which somewhat urges you to diligence. Otherwise you will lie in bed, and say you have time to spare, or nothing to do. You can rise when you have a journey to be taken, or a business of pressing necessity to be done: keep yourselves under some constant necessity, or urgency of business at the least.

**Direction 4. Take pleasure in your callings, and in the service of God.**

Sluggards themselves can rise to that which they take much pleasure in; as to go to a merriment, or feast, or play, or game, or to a good bargain, or anything which they delight in. If you had a delight in your calling, and in reading the Scripture, and praying, and doing good, you could not lie contentedly in bed, but would long to be up and doing, as children to their play. The wicked can rise early to do wickedness, because their hearts are set upon it: they can be drunk, or steal, or whore, or plot their ambitious and greedy designs, when they should sleep. [471] And if your heart were set as much on good as theirs is on evil, would you not be as wakeful and as readily up?

**Direction 5. Remember the grand importance of the business of your souls which always lies on your hands, that the greatness of your work may rouse you up.**

What! lie idling in bed, when you are so far behind in knowledge, and grace, and assurance of salvation; and have so much of the Scripture and other books to read and understand? Do you not have grace to beg for a needy soul? Is not prayer better work than excess of sleeping? Great business in the world can make you rise, and why not greater?

**Direction 6. Remember that you must answer in judgment for your time: and what comfort will you have to say, I idled away so many hours in a morning?**

And what comfort at death when time is gone, to review so much cast away in sleep?

**Direction 7. Remember that God beholds you, and is calling you up to work.**

If you understood his word and providence, you would hear him, as it were, saying as the mariners to Jonah, "What are you doing asleep? Get up! Cry out to your god!" Will you lie sleeping inordinately when God stands over you, and calls you up? If the king, or any great person, or friend, only knocked at your door, you would rise at once to wait upon them. Why, God would speak with you by his word, or hear you speak to him by prayer; and will you lie still and despise his call?

**Direction 8. Remember how many are attending you while you sleep.**

If it is summer, the sun is up before you, that has gone so many thousand miles while you were asleep: it has given a day's light to the other half of the world since you lay down, and has come again to light you to your work, and will you let it shine in vain? All the creatures are ready in their places to assist you, and are you asleep?

**Direction 9. Consider whether you will allow your servants to do the like: they must be up and at work, or you will be offended, and tell them that they are no servants for you, and that you do not hire them to sleep.**

And do you not owe God more service than they owe you? Does God hire you to sleep? Is it any more lawful for you than them to sleep one minute more than is needful for your health? No, not a minute: if you are sicklier than they, that is another matter; (but see that fullness and idleness do not cause it;) but otherwise your riches are no excuse to you. Will you loiter more than they, because you receive more? and do less service, because you have more pay? Or is it your privilege to be so miserable as to lose that time which poor men save?

**Direction 10. Remember that your morning hours are the chief part of all the day, for any holy exercise, or special employment of the mind.**

The mind is fresh and clear, and there is less interruption by worldly business; whereas when others are up and about their business, you will have interruptions. Those that have tried it can say by experience that the morning hours are the flower of their time, for prayer or studies; and that early rising is a great part of the art of redeeming time.

**Direction 11. Remember how many are condemning you by their diligence, while you are idling away your time.**

How many holy persons are then at prayer in secret, wrestling fervently with God for their salvation; or reading and meditating in his word! What do they get while you are sleeping! The blessed man does delight in the law of the Lord, and meditate in it day and night; and you love your ease, and are sleeping day and night: will not all these be witnesses against you? So will the diligent in their callings; and so will the worldlings and wicked that rise early to their sin. How many thousand are hard at work while you are sleeping! Have you not work to do, as well as they?

**Direction 12. Remember that indulgence in physical pleasure or flesh-pleasing is the great condemning sin that turns the heart from God: and if it is odious in a drunkard or fornicator, why is it not so in you?**

Mortify the flesh, and learn to deny it in its inordinate desires, and your sin is almost cured.

**Direction 13. For then the practical part is easy when you are willing: it is only agreeing with someone to awaken you, and a little cold water will wash away your drowsiness if you consent.**

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). All NET wording here (Matthew 6:33, Ecclesiastes 5:12, Jonah 1:6, and Psalm 1:2 in the note below) was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Matthew 6:33:** Baxter quotes the King James "seek the kingdom of God and his righteousness." The NET has "But above all pursue his kingdom and righteousness"; only the words matching Baxter's quoted phrase are used. The NET's "his kingdom" does not name God within the quoted words (the "his" refers back to the Father in the preceding verse), and "pursue" replaces "seek." His point (making God's kingdom the chief of one's desires) stands; the NET's "above all," left outside the quotation, fits it well.
- **Ecclesiastes 5:12:** Baxter's King James quotation has "the sleep of the labouring man is sweet"; the NET has "pleasant." His own earlier word in the Direction ("that your sleep may be sweet") is kept as his. The "for" before the quotation is Baxter's, printed inside his quotation marks in the source; it is placed outside them here.
- **Jonah 1:6 (Direction 7):** Baxter gives no reference; the reference is added here only. He quotes the King James "What meanest thou, O sleeper? arise, call upon thy God." The NET reads "What are you doing asleep? Get up! Cry out to your god!", with "god" in lower case, since in the story the ship's captain, a pagan, is speaking. Baxter applies the words to God calling the sleeper up, so the NET's lower-case "god" cuts against the sense he leans on (his "thy God" reads naturally as the true God). Baxter also says "the mariners"; in the verse it is the captain ("shipmaster" in the King James) who speaks. Both are left as Baxter's.
- **Psalm 1:2 (Direction 11):** "The blessed man does delight in the law of the Lord, and meditate in it day and night" is an unmarked allusion to Psalm 1:1–2, not in quotation marks in the source, so Baxter's wording is kept. The NET has "he finds pleasure in obeying the Lord's commands; he meditates on his commands day and night."
- **Unmarked allusion (Direction 10):** "redeeming time" echoes the King James "redeeming the time" (Ephesians 5:16; Colossians 4:5). Not referenced in Baxter's text.
- **Margin note:** the source's margin note for this question ("Whether love of sleep may be a mortal sin?") stands on line 42091, just before this range, and is not repeated here; it is expected to appear in the previous file.
- **mortal, reigning sin** (kept): Baxter's technical terms. A "reigning" sin is one that rules the heart and so marks an unconverted state; "mortal" here means a sin of that damning kind, as opposed to the lesser (venial) sins of a believer.
- **mistaken interest:** Baxter's phrase, kept: a sin that rests on a wrong judgment of where one's true good lies, so that the sinner prefers it to God.
- **Habitually / Actually** (points 3–5 of the Answer): kept. These are scholastic terms: "habitually" refers to the settled disposition or inclination (the "habit"), "actually" to what is at the moment desired or done.
- **carnal** (kept): unspiritual, ruled by the flesh.
- **luxury** (kept): in Baxter's time this word could mean indulgence in pleasure or lust generally, not only costly living; it may well mean the former here, but we have not tried to settle which.
- **Word swaps (Baxter's word, then ours):** chiefest, chief; felicity, happiness; sensual (desire), physical; sensuality, indulgence in physical pleasure / love of physical pleasure (by context); sensualist, pleasure-seeker; covetousness / covetous, greed / greedy; sensual (state of soul), pleasure-loving; "Not but that radically he preferreth," "Not that at root he does not prefer" (the archaic "Not but that" needed a light rewording; "radically" means "at the root," not "extremely"); temper (of body), temperament; immediate (service), direct (God's direct worship, as opposed to the work of a calling); "a journey to be gone," "a journey to be taken"; Yea, Indeed; slugging / slugged, idling / idled (to "slug" meant to lie lazily); behindhand, behind; presently, at once; "did but knock," "only knocked"; lawfuller, more lawful; interpellations, interruptions; executive (part), practical (the carrying-out of the resolve).
- **phlegmatic** (kept, Direction 1): in the humoral medicine of Baxter's day, a body dominated by phlegm was thought to be sluggish and drowsy. This is his period's medical theory.
- **calling** (kept): one's ordinary occupation or trade, seen as appointed by God.
- **Direction 8:** Baxter speaks of the sun as having "gone so many thousand miles" in the night; this is his everyday way of speaking and is left as he wrote it.
- **Direction 13** consists of a single sentence, so there is no following paragraph.
- **Part numbering:** the next Part in the source (just after this range) is numbered VIII; there is no Part VII in the source. Baxter's numbering is kept in the files that follow.
