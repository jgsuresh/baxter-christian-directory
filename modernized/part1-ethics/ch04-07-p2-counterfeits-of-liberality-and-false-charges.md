# Chapter 4: Counterfeits of freedom from greed, and false charges of greed

*This range begins partway through Direction 1 (understanding the nature and malignity of the sin), at its points numbered V and VI; no new Direction heading is given. It ends at the close of point VI.6, which may not be the end of the section.*

V. The counterfeits of liberality or freedom from greed, which deceive the worldling, are such as these: 1. He thinks he is not greedy because he has a necessity of doing what he does for more. Either he is in debt or he is poor, and scarcely has anything to live on; and the poor think that none are worldlings and greedy but the rich. But he may love riches that wants them, as much as he that has them. If you have a necessity of laboring in your callings, you have no necessity of loving the world, or of caring inordinately, or of being discontented with your estate. Impatience under your wants shows a love of the world and flesh, as much as other men's finery that possess it.

2. Another thinks he is not a worldling, because if he could but have necessities, even food and clothing, and conveniences for himself and family, he would be content; and it is not riches or great matters that he desires. [250] But if your hearts are more set upon the getting of these necessities or little things, than upon the preparing for death, and making sure of the heavenly treasure, you are miserable worldlings still. And the poor man that will set his heart more upon a poor and miserable life, than upon heaven, is more inexcusable than he that sets his heart more upon lordships and honors than upon heaven; though both of them are but the slaves of the world, and have as yet no treasure in heaven (Matthew 6:19-21). And, moreover, you that are now so greedy for a little more, if you had that, would be as greedy for a little more still; and when you had that, for a little more yet. You would next wear better clothing, and have better fare; and next you would have your house repaired, and then you would have your land enlarged, and then you would have something more for your children, and you would never be satisfied. You think otherwise now; but your hearts deceive you; you do not know them. If you do not believe me, judge by the case of other men that have been as confident as you, that if they had but so much or so much they would be content; but when they have it, they would still have more. And this, which is your claim, is the common claim of almost all the greedy: for lords and princes think themselves still in as great necessity as you think yourselves: as they have more, so they have more to do with it; and usually are still wanting as much as the poor. The question is not how much you desire? but to what use, and to what end, and in what order?

3. Another thinks he is not greedy, because he does not covet anything that is his neighbor's: he thinks that greed is only a desiring that which is not our own. But if you love the world and worldly plenty inordinately, and covet more, you are greedy worldlings, though you do not wish it from another. It is the worldly mind and love of wealth that is the sin at the root: the ways of getting it are but the branches.

4. Another thinks he is no worldling, because he uses no unlawful means, but the labor of his calling, to grow rich. The same answer serves to this. The love of wealth for the satisfying of the flesh is unlawful, whatever the means be. And is it not also an unlawful means of getting, to neglect God and your souls, and the poor, and shut out other duties for the world, as you often do?

5. Another thinks he is no worldling, because he is contented with what he has, and covets no more. When that which he has is a full provision for his fleshly desires. But if you over-love the world, and delight more in it than God, you are worldlings, though you desire no more. He is described by Christ as a miserable, worldly fool (Luke 12:19-20), that says, "You have plenty of goods stored up for many years; relax, eat, drink, celebrate!" To over-love what you have, is worldliness, as well as to desire more.

6. Another thinks he is no worldling, because he gives God thanks for what he has, and asked it of God in prayer. But if you are a lover of the world, and make provision for the desires of the flesh, it is but an aggravation of your sin, to ask God to be a servant to your fleshly lusts, and to thank him for satisfying your sinful desires. Your prayers and thanks are profane and carnal: they were no service to God, but to your flesh. As if a drunkard or a glutton should beg of God provision for their greedy throats, and thank him for it when they have it: or a fornicator should pray God to be a pander to his lusts, and then thank him for it: or a wanton gallant should make fine clothes and gallantry the matter of his prayer and thanksgiving.

7. Another thinks he is no worldling, because he has some thoughts of heaven, and is loath to be damned when he can keep the world no longer, and prays often, and perhaps fasts with the Pharisee twice a week, and gives alms often, and pays tithes, and wrongs no man. [251] But the Pharisees were greedy for all these (Luke 16:14). The question is not whether you think of heaven, and do something for it? But whether it be heaven or earth which you seek first, and make the end of all things else, which all are referred to? Every worldling knows that he must die, and therefore he would have heaven at last for a reserve, rather than hell. But where is it that you are laying up your treasure, and that you place all your happiness and hopes? And where are your hearts? on earth, or in heaven? (Colossians 3:1-3; Matthew 6:20-21). The question is not whether you give now and then an alms to deceive your consciences, and part with so much as the flesh can spare, as a swine will do when he can eat no more? but whether all that you have be devoted to the will of God, and made to stoop to his service and the saving of your souls, and can be forsaken rather than Christ forsaken (Luke 14:33).

8. Another thinks that he is not greedy, because it is but for his children that he provides: and "if someone does not provide for his own ... [he] is worse than an unbeliever" (1 Timothy 5:8). But the text speaks only of providing necessities for our families and kindred, rather than cast them on the church to be maintained. If you so overvalue the world, that you think it the happiness of your children to be rich, you are worldlings and greedy, both for yourselves and them. It is for their children that the richest and greatest make provision, that their posterity may be great and wealthy after them: and this makes them the more worldlings, and not the less; because they are greedy for after-ages, when they are dead, and not only for themselves.

9. Another thinks he is no worldling, because he can speak as harshly of greedy men as any other. But many a one reviles others as greedy that is greedy himself; indeed, greedy men are most apt to accuse others of greed, and of selling too dearly, and buying too cheap, and giving too little, because they would get the more themselves. And many preachers, by their reading and knowledge, may make a vehement sermon against worldliness, and yet go to hell at last for being worldlings. Words are cheap.

10. Another thinks he is not greedy, because he intends to leave much to charitable uses when he is dead. I confess that much is well: I wish more would do so. But the flesh itself can spare it, when it sees that it must lie down in the grave. If they could carry their riches with them and enjoy them after death, they would do it no doubt: to leave it when you cannot keep it any longer, is not worthy of thanks. So the glutton, and drunkard, and whoremonger, and the proud must all leave their pleasure at the grave. But do you serve God or the flesh with your riches while you have them? And do you use them to help or to hinder your salvation? Do not deceive yourselves, for God is not mocked (Galatians 6:7).

VI. Yet many are falsely accused of greed upon such grounds as these: 1. Because they possess much and are rich: for the poor take the rich for worldlings. But God does not give to all alike: he puts ten talents into the hands of one servant, and but one into another's: and to whom men commit much, of them will they require the more. [252] Therefore, to be entrusted with more than others is no sin, unless they betray that trust.

2. Others are accused as greedy, because they do not satisfy the greedy desires of those they deal with, or that expect much from them, and because they do not give where it is not their duty, but their sin to give. Thus the buyer says the seller is greedy; and the seller says the buyer is greedy, because they do not answer their greedy desires. An idle beggar will accuse you of uncharitableness, because you do not maintain him in sinful idleness. The proud expect you should help to maintain their pride. The drunkard, and the dissolute, and gamblers expect their parents should maintain their sin. No man that has anything, shall escape the censure of being greedy, as long as there is another in the world that covets that which he has: selfishness looks to no rules but their own desires.

3. Others are judged greedy, because they do not give that which they have not to give. Those that do not know another's estate, will pass conjectures at it; and if their handsome apparel or deportment, or the common report, make men think them richer than they are, then they are accounted greedy, because their bounty does not answer men's expectations.

4. Others are thought greedy, because they are hardworking in their callings, and thrifty, and saving, not willing that anything be lost. But all this is their duty: if they were lords or princes, idleness and wastefulness would be their sin. God would have all men labor in their several callings, that are able: and Christ himself said, when he had fed many thousands by miracle, yet "Gather up the broken pieces that are left over, so that nothing is wasted." The question is, How they use that which they labor so hard for, and save so sparingly. If they use it for God, and charitable uses, there is no man takes a more upright course. He is the best servant for God, that will be hardworking and sparing, that he may be able to do good.

5. Others are thought greedy, because, to avoid hypocrisy, they give in secret, and keep their works of charity from the knowledge of men. These shall have their reward from God: and his wrath shall be the reward of their presumptuous censures.

6. Others are thought greedy, because they lawfully and peaceably seek their right, and do not let the unjust and greedy wrong them at their pleasure. It is true, we must let go our right, whenever the recovering of it will do more hurt to others than it will do us good. But yet the laws are not made in vain: nor must we encourage men in greed, thievery, and deceit, by letting them do what they please: nor must we be careless of our Master's talents; if he entrusts us with them, we must not let everyone take them from us to serve his lusts with.

## Baxter's notes

Reproduced as in the source (English modernized). A draft English translation follows the Latin in parentheses; the translation was prepared for this project and is not from a published edition, so have a Latinist check it before relying on fine points.

[250] It was one of Chilon's sayings, Lapideis cotibus aurum examinari: auro autem bonorum malorumque hominum mentem cujusmodi sit comprobari: i. e. As the touchstone tests gold, so gold tests men's minds, whether they are good or bad. Laertius in Chil. p. 43. (English: Gold is tested by touchstones; but by gold the mind of good and bad men, of whatever kind it is, is proved. Laertius, in the life of Chilon, p. 43.)

[251] Luke 18:11-13; Matthew 6:16, 18.

[252] Luke 12:48; 16:9-10; Matthew 25; 2 Corinthians 8:14-15.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **Luke 12:19-20 (point 5):** Baxter's quotation, "Soul, take thy ease, eat, drink, and be merry, thou hast much goods laid up for many years," is the King James wording, in a different order from the verse. NET has no "Soul" at the start of the saying (it reads "I will say to myself, ..."), "relax" for "take thine ease," and "celebrate" for "be merry." We give the NET words for the extent he quotes. Baxter cites verses 19 and 20; the quoted words are from verse 19, and verse 20 ("You fool!") is the verse that makes the man a "fool."
- **Luke 16:14 (point 7):** Baxter's "the Pharisees were covetous" is the King James sense; NET reads "The Pharisees (who loved money)." Baxter's text gives no quotation here, so we kept his sentence, with "greedy" for "covetous." The sense he leans on, love of money, is the same.
- **1 Timothy 5:8 (point 8):** NET wording, from "if someone does not provide for his own, especially his own family, he has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever." We quote the parts Baxter quotes, with "..." for the skipped words and "[he]" to join them. Baxter's "infidel" is NET's "unbeliever" (the standing swap). The NET's "especially his own family" matches Baxter's reading that the text concerns our own households.
- **Galatians 6:7 (point 10):** Baxter has no quotation marks, so we kept his words, "God is not mocked." NET reads "God will not be made a fool." The sense is the same.
- **John 6:12 (VI, point 4):** Baxter gives no reference for "Gather up the fragments that remain, that nothing be lost." It appears to be John 6:12 (not in Baxter's text). We give the NET wording.
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording): ten talents and one talent, and "to whom men commit much, of them will they require the more" (VI, point 1; footnote 252 gives Luke 12:48 and Matthew 25; NET at Luke 12:48 reads "from the one who has been entrusted with much, even more will be asked"); the Pharisee who fasts twice a week and pays tithes (Luke 18:12; Baxter's footnote 251 gives the passage); "God is not mocked."
- **Matthew 6:19-21; Matthew 6:20-21; Colossians 3:1-3; Luke 14:33:** references only, with no words quoted. Baxter's "Col. iii. 1-3; Matt. vi. 20, 21" is given as "Colossians 3:1-3; Matthew 6:20-21."
- **greedy, greed:** Baxter's "covetous, covetousness" (standing swap). **covet** (verb, point 3 and VI point 2) is kept, since it is still ordinary English.
- **worldling:** kept, Baxter's word for one whose heart is set on this world.
- **finery:** Baxter's "bravery" (point 1: splendid show and display). **necessities:** his "necessaries." **clothing:** his "raiment." **ask:** his "desire" in "desire God to be a servant" (point 6). **harshly:** his "hardly" (point 9). **dearly:** his "dear" (selling at too high a price). **dissolute:** his "riotous." **gamblers:** his "gamesters." **expect:** his "look" in "The proud look you should help." **hardworking:** his "laborious." **intends:** his "purposeth." **worthy of thanks:** his "thankworthy." **report:** his "fame" (VI, point 3). **more upright:** his "righter." **loath:** his "loth." **indeed:** his "yea." **most apt:** his "aptest." **escape:** his "scape." **please:** his "list" (as in "what they list"). **entrusts:** his "intrusts."
- **scarcely has anything to live on** (point 1): Baxter's "scarcely hath whereon to live." **inexcusable:** his "unexcusable" (point 2).
- **inordinately** (point 1 and 3): kept; it means beyond proper measure.
- **callings** (point 1, 4, VI point 4): kept; Baxter means a person's trade or occupation, seen as given by God. **several callings:** "several" is kept in its older sense of "various, each their own." **estate** (point 1, VI point 3): kept; one's condition and possessions.
- **still wanting as much as the poor** (point 2): kept as written; "wanting" means lacking or feeling a lack.
- **aggravation** (point 6): kept; it means a making worse. **wanton gallant** (point 6): kept; a pleasure-loving, showily dressed young man of fashion. **pander:** one who procures for another's lusts. **for a reserve** (point 7): kept; as a backup in case the world fails.
- **which all are referred to** (point 7): kept as written; the "end" to which all other things are directed.
- **pass conjectures** (VI, point 3): kept; make guesses.
- **Source oddities:** In point 5, "When that which he has is a full provision for his fleshly desires" is a sentence fragment in the source, and we keep it as one. In point VI.2, "selfishness looks to no rules but their own desires" mixes a singular subject with a plural "their"; kept. This range ends at the end of point VI.6; the next file presumably continues the section.
- **Chilon** (note 250): Baxter's "Laertius in Chil." probably means Diogenes Laertius's life of Chilon, one of the traditional Seven Sages of Greece (not confirmed here). The Latin is reproduced as printed.
