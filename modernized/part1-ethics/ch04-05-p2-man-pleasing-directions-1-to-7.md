# Chapter 4: Directions against Inordinate Man-pleasing, end of Direction 1 and Directions 2-7

*This range begins partway through Direction 1, at its numbered sub-point 8 (the first Direction of the section, whose heading is in the previous file); it ends with the end of Direction 7.*

8. Man's nature is so prone to go too far in valuing our esteem with men, that we should more fear lest we err on that hand, than on the other, in undervaluing it. And it is far safer to do too little than too much, in the vindicating of our own reputation, whether by the magistrate's justice, or by disputing, or any contentious means.

9. We must not wholly rest on the judgment of any, about the state of our souls, nor take their judgment of us for infallible; but use their help that we may know ourselves.

10. If ministers, or councils called general, do err and contradict the word of God, we must do our best to discern it; and discerning it, must desert their error rather than the truth of God. As Calvin, and after him Paræus on 1 Corinthians 4:3, say, "We must give an account of our doctrine to all men that require it, especially to ministers and councils: but when a faithful pastor perceives himself oppressed with unrighteous and perverse designs and factions, and that there is no place for equity and truth, he ought to be careless of man's esteem, and appeal to God, and fly to his tribunal. And if we see ourselves condemned, our cause being unpleaded, and judgment passed, our cause being unheard, let us lift up our minds to this magnanimity, as despising men's judgment, to expect with boldness the judgment of God;" and say with Paul, "With me it is a small matter to be judged of you, or of man's judgment; I have one that judges me, even the Lord."

11. God must be enough for a gracious soul, and we must know that "his good favor restores one's life," and his "loyal love is better than life itself": and this must be our care and labor, that "whether we are alive or away, we make it our ambition to please him": and if we have his approbation it must satisfy us, though all the world condemn us. [171] Therefore having faithfully done our duty, we must leave the matter of our reputation to God; who, if our ways please him, can make our enemies to be at peace with us, or be harmless to us as if they were no enemies. As we must quietly leave it to him what measure of wealth we shall have, so also what measure of honor we shall have. It is our duty to love and honor, but not to be beloved and honored.

12. The prophecy of our Savior must be still believed, that the "world will hate us;" and his example must be still before our eyes, who submitted to be spit upon, and scorned and buffeted, and slandered as a traitor or usurper of the crown, and "emptied himself," and "endured the cross," and "disregarding its shame;" leaving us an example that we should "follow in his steps. He committed no sin nor was deceit found in his mouth. When he was maligned, he did not answer back; when he suffered, he threatened no retaliation, but committed himself to God who judges justly." [172] This is the treatment that must be the Christian's expectation, and not to be well spoken of by all, nor to have the applause and honor of the world.

13. It is not only the approbation of the ignorant and ungodly that we must thus think little of; but even of the most learned and godly themselves, so as to bear their censures as an easy burden, when God is pleased this way to try us; and to be satisfied in God alone, and the expectation of his final judgment. [173]

**Direction 2. Remember that the favor and pleasing of man is one of your snares, that would prevail against your pleasing God: therefore watch against the danger of it, as you must do against other earthly things.**

**Direction 3. Remember how silly a creature man is; and that his favor can be no better than himself.** The thoughts or words of a mortal worm are matters of no considerable value to us.

**Direction 4. Remember that it is the judgment of God alone, that your life or death for ever depends upon; and how little you are concerned in the judgment of man.** 1. An humbled soul, that has felt what it is to have displeased God, and what it is to be under his curse, and what it is to be reconciled to him by the death and intercession of Jesus Christ, is so taken up in seeking the favor of God, and is so troubled with every fear of his displeasure, and is so delighted with the sense of his love, as that he can scarcely have time to mind so small a matter as the favor or displeasure of a man. God's favor is enough for him, and so precious to him, that if he finds that he has this, so small a matter as the favor of a man will scarcely be missed by him.

2. God only is our supreme Judge, and our governors as officers limited by him: but for others, if they will be usurpers, and set themselves in the throne of God, and there let fly their censures upon things and persons which concern them not, why should we seem much concerned in it? If a beggar steps up into a seat of judicature, and there condemns one, and fines another, will you fear him, or laugh at him? Who are you that judge another man's servant? To his own master does he stand or fall. Men may step up into the throne of God, and there presume to judge others according to their interests and passions: but God will quickly pull them down, and teach them better to know their places. How like is the common censure of the world, to the game of boys, that will hold an assize, and make a judge, and try and condemn one another in play! And have we not a greater Judge to fear?

3. It is God only that passes the final sentence, from whom there is no appeal to any other: but from human judgment there lies an appeal to God. [174] Their judgment must be judged of by him. Things shall not stand as now men censure them. Many a bad cause is now judged good, through the multitude or greatness of those that favor it: and many a good cause is now condemned. Many a one is taken as a malefactor because he obeys God and does his duty. But all these things must be judged over again, by him that has denounced "Beware, those who call evil good and good evil, who turn darkness into light and light into darkness" (Isaiah 5:20). "The one who says to the guilty, 'You are innocent,' peoples will curse him, and nations will denounce him" (Proverbs 24:24). It would be ill with the best of the servants of Christ, if the judgment of the world must stand, who condemn them as fools, and hypocrites, and what they please: then the devil's judgment would stand. But he is the wise man that God will judge to be wise at last; and he only is the happy man that God calls happy. The erring judgment of a creature is but like an ignorant man's writing the names of several things upon an apothecary's boxes; if he writes the names of poisons upon some, and of antidotes on others, when there are no such things within them, they are not to be estimated according to those names. [175] How different are the names that God and the world do put upon things and persons now! And how few now approve of that which God approves of, and will justify at last! How many will God judge heterodox and wicked, that men judged orthodox, and worthy of applause! And how many will God judge orthodox and sincere, that were called heretics and hypocrites by men! God will not verify every word against his servants, which angry men, or contentious disputants, say against them. The learning, or authority, or other advantages of the contenders, may now bear down the reasons and reputations of more wise and righteous men than they, which God will restore and vindicate at last. The names of Luther, Zuinglius, Calvin, and many other excellent servants of the Lord, are now made odious in the writings and reports of papists, by their impudent lies; but God judges otherwise, with more righteous judgment. Oh what abundance of persons and causes will be justified at the dreadful day of God, which the world condemned! And how many will be there condemned, that were justified by the world! O blessed day! most desirable to the just, most terrible to the wicked and every hypocrite. How many things will then be set straight, that now are crooked! and how many innocents and saints will then have a resurrection of their murdered names, that were buried by the world in a heap of lies, and their enemies never thought of their reviving! O look to that final judgment of the Lord, and you will take men's censures but as the shaking of a leaf.

4. It is God only that has power to execute his sentence, to our happiness or misery. "There is only one who is lawgiver and judge—the one who is able to save and destroy" (James 4:12). If he says to us, "Come, you who are blessed," we shall be happy, though devils and men should curse us; for those that he blesses shall be blessed. If he condemns to hell, the applause of the world will fetch no man out, nor give him ease. A great name on earth, or histories written in their applause, or a gilded monument over their bones, are a poor relief to damned souls. And the barking of the wicked, and their scorns on earth, are no diminution to the joy or glory of the souls that shine and triumph with Christ. It is our Lord that has "the keys of death and of Hades" (Revelation 1:18). Please him, and you are sure to escape, though the pope, and all the wicked of the world, should thunder out against you their most direful curses. Woe to us if the wicked could execute all their malicious censures! then how many saints would be in hell! But if it is God that justifies us, how inconsiderable a matter is it, who they are that condemn us, or what their claims are! Romans 8:33.

**Direction 5. Remember that the judgment of ungodly men, is corrupted and directed by the devil; and to be overruled by their censures, or too much to fear them, is to be overruled by the devil, and to be afraid of his censures of us.** And will you honor him so much? Alas! it is he that puts those thoughts into the minds of the ungodly, and those reproachful words into their mouths. To prefer the judgment of a man before God's, is odious enough, though you did not prefer the devil's judgment.

**Direction 6. Consider what a slavery you choose, when you thus make yourselves the servants of every man, whose censures you fear, and whose approbation you are ambitious of.** 1 Corinthians 7:23, "You were bought with a price. Do not become slaves of men:" that is, do not needlessly enthrall yourselves. What a task have men-pleasers! they have as many masters as beholders! No wonder if it takes them off from the service of God; for the "friendship with the world means hostility toward God;" and he that will thus be a friend of the world, "makes himself God's enemy" (James 4:4). They cannot serve two masters, God and the world. You know men will condemn you, if you are true to God: if, therefore, you must needs have the favor of men, you must take it alone without God's favor. A man-pleaser cannot be true to God, because he is a servant to the enemies of his service; the wind of a man's mouth will drive him about as the chaff, from any duty, and to any sin. How servile a person is a man-pleaser! how many masters has he, and how mean ones! It perverts the course of your hearts and lives, and turns all from God to this unprofitable way. [176]

**Direction 7. Remember what a pitiful reward you seek.** "Truly," says our Lord, concerning hypocrites and man-pleasers, "they have their reward" (Matthew 6:2). O miserable reward! The thought and breath of mortal men, instead of God--instead of heaven; this is their reward! Their happiness will be to lie in hell, and remember that they were well spoken of on earth! and that once they were accounted religious, learned, wise, or honorable! and to remember that they preferred this reward before everlasting happiness with Christ! If this be not gain, your labor is all lost, which you lay out in hunting for applause. If this be enough to spend your time for, and to neglect your God for, and to lose your souls for, rejoice then in the hypocrite's reward.

## Baxter's notes

[171] Psalm 30:5; 63:3; 2 Corinthians 5:9; Romans 8:33-34.

[172] Matthew 10; John 15; Matthew 27; Hebrews 12:1-3; 1 Peter 2:21-23.

[173] We must go further than Seneca, who said, Male de me loquuntur, sed mali; moverer si de me Mar. Cato, si Lælius sapiens, si duo Scipiones ista loquerentur: nunc malis displicere, laudari est. (English: They speak ill of me, but they are bad men; I would be moved if Marcus Cato, if wise Laelius, if the two Scipios were saying such things about me: now, to displease bad men is to be praised. Seneca, as Baxter names him; draft translation.)

[174] See Dr. Boys' Postil. p. 42, 43. Marlorat. in 1 Corinthians 4:3.

[175] The open daylight of truth does not show the masks, and mummeries, and triumphs of the world, half so stately and gallant as candlelight does. Lord Bacon's Essay of Truth. Why lies are loved.

[176] Offendet te superbus contemptu; dives contumelia, petulans injuria, lividus malignitate: pugnax contentione, ventosus et mendax vanitate: non feres a suspicioso timeri, a pertinace vinci, a delicato fastidiri. Senec. de Ira, l. 3. c. 8. (English: The proud man will offend you by contempt; the rich man by insult, the petulant by injury, the envious by malice; the quarrelsome by contention, the windbag and liar by vanity: you will not bear to be feared by the suspicious, to be beaten by the stubborn, to be disdained by the fastidious. Seneca, *On Anger*, book 3, chapter 8; draft translation, rendered loosely in the last clause.)

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). Each quoted verse was fetched from labs.bible.org. The Latin translations above are drafts prepared for this project and need a Latinist's check.
- **Start of range:** the file begins at sub-point 8 of Direction 1; the heading and earlier sub-points are in the preceding file.
- **Direction labels:** Baxter's "Direct. II.-VII." are rendered "Direction 2-7." The first sentence of each is in bold with its label.
- **1 Corinthians 4:3 (Calvin and Paræus):** the cited words are the authors' remarks, not Scripture. The closing words Baxter gives to Paul ("With me it is a small matter to be judged of you ... I have one that judges me, even the Lord") are his blend of 1 Corinthians 4:3-4 in King James wording and are kept as his, modernized. The NET has: "it is a minor matter that I am judged by you or by any human court ... The one who judges me is the Lord" (4:3-4).
- **Psalm 30:5 (flag):** Baxter's "in his favour is life" (King James). The NET has "his good favor restores one's life," which says that God's favor gives life back, not that life is found in it. His point (God's favor is life) is close but not identical. We moved his "that" outside the quotation marks. **Psalm 63:3:** NET "loyal love" for Baxter's "loving-kindness"; NET reads "experiencing your loyal love is better than life itself." Baxter's "his" is changed to fit; Baxter gave no references in the body; the references are only in note 171 (Psalm 30:5; 63:3), so none is added to the text.
- **2 Corinthians 5:9 (flag):** Baxter's "whether living or dying we may be accepted of him" (King James: "accepted"). The NET has "whether we are alive or away, we make it our ambition to please him." The NET makes the verse about our ambition to please him, not about being accepted by him; "away" means away from the body. Baxter's argument (his approval is enough) leans on "accepted." Baxter gave no reference in the body; note 171 lists 2 Corinthians 5:9, so none is added to the text.
- **"world will hate us" (item 12):** Baxter's own wording, with no reference; it paraphrases John 15 (note 172). The NET at John 15:18 reads "If the world hates you, be aware that it hated me first." Kept as his.
- **Philippians 2:7 (flag):** Baxter's "made himself of no reputation" (King James). The NET has "emptied himself." "Reputation" is Baxter's point (Christ gave up his standing); the NET wording is about self-emptying, so the connection to reputation is Baxter's inference. **Hebrews 12:2:** NET "endured the cross, disregarding its shame" for his "despised the shame"; because the NET is a participle ("disregarding"), the quoted phrase does not fit the sentence as smoothly as Baxter's verb. The references are not in Baxter's body (only Hebrews 12:1-3 in note 172).
- **1 Peter 2:21-23 (flag):** Baxter's "who did no sin ... committed all to him that judges righteously." The NET has "He committed no sin ... committed himself to God who judges justly," so "committed all to him" becomes "committed himself to God." His "us" is the NET's "you." Baxter gave no reference in the body (note 172 lists 1 Peter 2:21-23), so none is added to the text. The NET text has sentence breaks inside the passage; we kept the NET's punctuation in the quotation. NET 2:21 reads "leaving an example for you to follow in his steps"; Baxter's "that we should" stays outside the quotation marks.
- **Isaiah 5:20 (flag):** Baxter's "woe to them that call evil good ..." (King James). The NET has "Beware, those who call evil good and good evil." Baxter's "denounced a" before the quotation is shortened to "denounced" so the NET's "Beware" fits; the NET lacks the word "woe," which Baxter's "denounced" leans on. The NET continues "who turn bitter into sweet..." which Baxter does not quote.
- **Proverbs 24:24 (flag):** NET "the guilty" for Baxter's "the wicked"; "You are innocent" for "Thou art righteous"; "denounce" for "abhor"; NET has "peoples" (plural) for Baxter's "people." The legal sense ("guilty/innocent") in the NET is narrower than "wicked/righteous."
- **Romans 14:4 (unmarked allusion, item 3, Direction 4, point 2):** Baxter's "Who art thou that judgest another man's servant? To his own master doth he stand or fall," given with no reference or quotation marks; kept in his wording, modernized. NET: "Who are you to pass judgment on another's servant? Before his own master he stands or falls."
- **James 4:12 (flag):** the NET has "There is only one who is lawgiver and judge—the one who is able to save and destroy." The NET adds "and judge," which is not in Baxter's "one lawgiver." The NET begins "But there is only one"; we begin the quotation at "There."
- **Matthew 25:34 (unmarked):** Baxter's "Come ye blessed" has no reference. NET: "Come, you who are blessed by my Father." We quoted the phrase "Come, you who are blessed." **Revelation 1:18 (flag):** NET has "the keys of death and of Hades"; Baxter's King James has "hell." Since Hades is the realm of the dead, not necessarily the place of punishment, the NET wording is narrower than Baxter's "death and hell."
- **Romans 8:33:** Baxter's printed reference, with no quotation; his clause ("it is God that justifies") follows the verse (NET: "It is God who justifies").
- **1 Corinthians 7:23:** the NET reads "You were bought with a price. Do not become slaves of men." Baxter's "be not ye the servants of men" is "servants" in the King James; the NET's "slaves" is stronger, and Baxter's own gloss ("do not needlessly enthrall yourselves") speaks of being enthralled, which fits "slaves." The sentence break in the NET is kept inside the quotation.
- **James 4:4 (flag):** Baxter's two quotations ("friendship of the world is enmity to God"; "a friend of the world, is an enemy to God") are King James wording. The NET reads "friendship with the world means hostility toward God" and "whoever decides to be the world's friend makes himself God's enemy." We kept his sentence and quoted only the NET words that fit it; "a friend of the world" stays outside the quotation marks. The NET adds "decides," a deliberate choice, which Baxter's "will thus be" also conveys.
- **Matthew 6:25 corrected to Matthew 6:2:** Baxter prints "Matt. vi. 25," but the words "they have their reward" are at Matthew 6:2 (and also 6:5 and 6:16, on almsgiving, prayer, and fasting). Matthew 6:25 concerns anxiety. NET: "I tell you the truth, they have their reward!" We kept Baxter's "Verily" as "Truly" (the NET at 6:5 begins "Truly I say to you").
- **Matthew 6:24 (unmarked allusion, Direction 6):** "They cannot serve two masters, God and the world" echoes Matthew 6:24 ("God and money" in the NET); Baxter's "the world" is his own.
- **Word swaps (Baxter's word, then ours):** "set light by" to "think little of"; "usage" to "treatment"; "scarce(ly)" to "scarcely"; "have while to mind" to "have time to mind" ("while" meaning time); "what they list" to "what they please"; "in sport" to "in play"; "enthral" to "enthrall"; "give him case" to "give him ease" (the source prints "case," plainly a misprint); "what be their pretences" to "what their claims are," following the project decision on "pretence."
- **Kept as Baxter wrote:** "silly" (in his day it could mean frail or simple, not only foolish); "buffeted" (struck repeatedly); "approbation" (approval); "censure(s)" (judgment, usually adverse); "magnanimity" (greatness of mind); "unpleaded" (not argued for); "judicature" (a court of law); "assize" (a court session; a project technical term); "malefactor" (wrongdoer); "heterodox / orthodox"; "mummeries" (in Bacon's note, pretended shows); "direful"; "pitiful" (here, miserable). **papists**, **Zuinglius** (Baxter's form of Huldrych Zwingli's name) and his strong words about Rome in Direction 4, point 3 are his polemic, kept exactly.
- **Seneca (note 173):** Baxter's Latin is kept as printed; "Mar. Cato" is probably Marcus Cato and "Lælius" Laelius, but we have not confirmed which persons Seneca means beyond what Baxter names. **Seneca (note 176):** book and chapter as Baxter gives them; we have not checked them against an edition. Note the printed "non feres" is kept as printed.
- **Note 174:** "Dr. Boys' Postil" and "Marlorat" are named as Baxter prints them; we have not identified the editions. **Note 175:** "Lord Bacon's Essay of Truth" is Baxter's wording; the quoted sentence is modernized only in its verb ("does").
