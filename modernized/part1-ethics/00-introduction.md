# Part 1: Christian Ethics

**Or, Directions for the Ordering of the Private Actions of Our Hearts and Lives, in the Work of Holy Self-Government, to and under God**

## The Introduction [6]

The eternal God having made man a rational and free agent, able to understand and choose the good, and refuse the evil; to know, and love, and serve his Maker, and by adhering to him in this life of trial, to attain to the blessed sight and enjoyment of his glory in the life to come, has not failed to provide him with such necessities, without which these ends could not successfully be sought. When we had lost our moral capacity of pleasing him, that we might enjoy him, he restores us to it by the wonderful work of our redemption. In Christ he has reconciled the world to himself; and has given them a general amnesty, contained in the covenant of grace, of which nothing but men's stubborn and final unwillingness can deprive them. To procure their consent to this gracious covenant, he has given to his ministers the "message of reconciliation;" commanding us to plead with men "on Christ's behalf," "as though God were making his plea through us," to "be reconciled to God" (2 Corinthians 5:18–20); and to show them first their sin and misery, and proclaim and offer the true remedy, and to let them know that all things are now ready, and by pleading their duty, their necessity, and their benefit, to compel them to come in (Matthew 22:4; Luke 14:17, 23).

But so great is the blindness and stubbornness of men that the majority refuse consent; being deceived by the pleasures, and profits, and honors of this present world; and make their supposed necessities or business the matter of their excuses, and the unreasonable reasons of their refusal, neglect, and delays, until death surprises them, and the door is shut; and they knock, and cry for mercy and admission, when it is too late (Matthew 25:10–12).

Against this willful neglect and presumption, which is the principal cause of the damnation of the ungodly world, I have written many books already. [7] But because there are many that profess themselves sincerely willing, not only to be saved but also to be Christ's disciples; to learn of him, to imitate him, and be conformed to him, and to do the will of God if they could but know it; I have determined, by God's assistance, to write this book for the use of such, and to give them from God's word those plain directions that are suited to the various duties of their lives, and may guide them safely in their walk with God to life eternal. Do not expect here copious and earnest exhortations, for that work I have done already; and have now to do with such as say they are made willing, and desire help against their ignorance, so that skill and will may work together for their salvation. I shall labor to speak as plainly as I can, because I especially intend it for the ignorant; and yet to be reasonably exact in the directions, lest such readers lose the benefit by mistakes. And I must speak to many cases, because I speak to families, where all are not in the same condition, and the same persons are not always the same. And therefore if I should not be brief in the particulars, I should be too long in the whole; and tediousness might deprive some readers of the benefit.

In families some are (too commonly) ungodly, in a carnal, unrenewed state; and some are godly, in a state of grace. [8] These are to be considered as Christians simply, with respect to God, or in their relations to others: these relations are either ecclesiastical, civil, or domestic (family relations).

Accordingly, my intended method is: 1. To direct ungodly, carnal minds how to attain a state of grace. 2. To direct those that have saving grace how to use it, both in the contemplative and active parts of their lives; in their duties of religion, both private and public; in their duties to men, both in their ecclesiastical, civil, and family relations. And, by the way, to direct those that have grace how to discern it, and take comfort from it; and to direct them how to grow in grace and persevere to the end.

And if any reader should be discouraged at the number of duties and directions set before him, I entreat him to consider: 1. That it is God, and not I, who imposes all these duties on you; and who will question his wisdom, goodness, or power to make laws for us and all the world? 2. That every duty and direction is a mercy to you, and therefore should not be matter of grief to you, but of thanks. They are but like the commands of parents to their children, when they tell them to eat their food, and wear their clothes, and go to bed, and do not eat poison, and do not tumble in the dirt; and do not cut your fingers, and beware of fire and water, etc. To leave out any such law or duty would be but to deprive you of an excellent mercy; you will not cut off or cast away any part of your body, any vein, or sinew, or artery, on the pretext that the number makes them troublesome, when the diminishing of that number would kill or maim you. A student is not offended that he has many books in his library; nor a tradesman that he has plenty of tools; nor the rich at the number of his farms or flocks. Believe it, reader, if you do not bring a malicious, quarrelsome mind, you will find that God has not burdened but blessed you with his holy precepts, and that he has not appointed you one unnecessary or unprofitable duty; but only such as tend to your contentment, and joy, and happiness. [9]

O let it be the daily, earnest prayer of me and you that our hearts do not prove false and unwilling to follow the directions that are given us, lest we condemn ourselves in the things that we approve. Your practice now will show whether it is through lack of will or skill, if from now on you unfaithfully neglect your duty. If you are willing, obey now what is plainly taught to you, and show by your diligence that you are willing.

## Translator's notes

- **carnal** (paragraph 4): kept. Baxter means "unspiritual, ruled by the flesh," not "sexual." See the glossary.
- **amnesty** (paragraph 1): Baxter's "general act of oblivion," the legal term for a pardon that "forgets" past offenses.
- **Luke 14:17, 23** (paragraph 1): the 1846 edition prints "Luke xi. 17, 23," which appears to be a misprint, since "all things are now ready" and "compel them to come in" are Luke 14:17 and 14:23. We corrected the reference here and flag it so you can check it against the original.
- **2 Corinthians 5:18–20** (paragraph 1): Baxter loosely paraphrases the King James wording. We use the NET wording for the verses he cites, keeping the extent of what he quotes.
- **Scripture translation:** quoted Scripture in this project is from the NET Bible®. See [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md).
- **approve** (last paragraph): Baxter's "allow" in "the things which we allow" has the older sense of "approve," as in Romans 14:22.
- **benefit** (paragraph 1): Baxter's "commodity" meant advantage or benefit.
- **"the same persons are not always the same"** (paragraph 3): Baxter means that one person's condition and needs change over time.
