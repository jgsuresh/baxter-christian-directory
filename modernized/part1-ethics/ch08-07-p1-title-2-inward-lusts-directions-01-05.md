# Chapter 8: Part 5, Title 2: against inward lusts, Directions 1–5

**Title 2. Directions against inward, filthy lusts.**

**Direction 1. Because with most the constitution of the body has a great hand in this sin, your first care must be about the body, to bring it to a condition less inclined to lust; and here the chief remedy is fasting and much abstinence.**

And this may the better be borne, because for the most part it is persons so strong as to be able to endure it that are under this temptation. If your temptation is not strong, less abstinence from food and drink may serve the purpose (for I would prescribe you no stronger medicine than is needed to cure your disease). But if it is violent, and lesser means will not succeed, it is better that your bodies be somewhat weakened than your souls corrupted and undone. Therefore in this case, 1. Eat no breakfasts or suppers; but one meal a day, except a bite or two of bread and a sip or two of water in the morning, and yet not too full a dinner; and nothing at night. 2. Drink no wine or strong drink, but water if the stomach can bear it without sickness (and usually in such hot bodies it is healthier than beer). 3. Eat no hot spices, or strong, or heating, or windy foods: eat lettuce and such cooling herbs. 4. If need requires it, be often bled, or purged with such purges as copiously evacuate serous fluid, and do not only irritate. 5. And often bathe in cold water. But the physician should be consulted, so that they may be safely done.

If you think this course too costly a cure, and would rather cherish your flesh and lust, you are not the persons that I am now directing; for I speak to those only who are willing to be cured, and to use the necessary means that they may be cured. If you are not brought to this, your conscience needs better awakening. I am sure Christ says that when the bridegroom was taken from them, his disciples should "fast" (Mark 2:19–20). And even hardworking Paul was "many times without food" (2 Corinthians 6:5; 11:27), and "kept his body under and brought it into subjection, lest by any means, when he had preached to others, he himself should be a castaway" (1 Corinthians 9:27). And I am sure that the ancient Christians that lived in solitude, and many of them ate nothing but bread and water, or poorer fare than bread, did not think this cure too costly. [457] Yes, smaller necessities than this engaged them in "fasting" (1 Corinthians 7:5). This unclean devil will scarcely be cast out except by "prayer and fasting" (Mark 9:29).

And I must tell you that fullness naturally cherishes lust, as fuel does the fire. Fullness of bread prepared the Sodomites for their filthy lusts. It is no more a wonder that a stuffed paunch has a lustful fury than that the water runs into the pipes when the cistern is full, or than it is a wonder to see a dunghill bear weeds, or a carcass to be full of crawling maggots. Plutarch speaks of a Spartan who, being asked why Lycurgus made no law against adultery, answered, There are no adulterers with us: but says the other, What if there should be any? says the Spartan, Then he is to pay an ox so great as shall stand on this side of the river Taget and drink of the river Eurota: says the other, That is impossible: and says the Spartan, *Et quo pacto Spartæ existat adulter in qua divitiæ, deliciæ, et corporis adscititius cultus probro habentur? et contra verecundia, modestia, ac obedientiæ magistratibus debitæ observatio decori laudique dantur?* that is, And how can there be an adulterer at Sparta, where riches, delights, and strange attire, or ornament are a disgrace or reproach? and on the contrary bashfulness, modesty, and the observance of due obedience to magistrates is an honor and praise? And if rich men think it their privilege to fare sumptuously and satisfy their appetites, they must take it for their privilege to feed their lust. But God gives no man plenty for such uses; nor is it any excuse for eating and drinking much, because you have much, any more than it would be for your cooks to put much salt in your food, more than in poorer men's, because you have more. [458] He that observes the filthy and pernicious effects of that gluttony which is considered rich men's honor and happiness will never envy them that miserable happiness, but say rather as Antisthenes, *Hostium filiis contingat in deliciis vivere*, [459] Let it befall the children of my enemies to live in delights; except that the curse is too heavy for a Christian to use against any of his enemies. But for himself he must remember that he is the servant of a holy God, and has a holy work to do, and holy sacrifices to offer to him, and therefore must not pamper his flesh, as if he were preparing a sacrifice for Venus. For, as 1 Thessalonians 4:3–4 says, "this is God's will: that you become holy, that you keep away from sexual immorality, that each of you know how to possess his own body in holiness and honor, not in lustful passion like the Gentiles who do not know God." As the philosopher answered Antigonus when he asked him whether he should go to a merry feast that he was invited to, You are the son of a king; [460] so it is answer enough for a Christian against temptations to indulgence in pleasure, I am the son of the most holy God. If you are invited to feasts where urging or allurement is likely to make you break your bounds, do not go, or go back when you see the bait. As Epaminondas in Plutarch, finding excess at a feast that he was invited to, went away when he saw it, saying, *Ego te sacrificare, non lascivire putaram*; so say, I came to dine and not to be wanton or luxurious; to support my body for duty, and not to pamper it for lust. Plutarch marvels at the folly of those men that detest the charms of witches lest they hurt them, and do not fear but love the charms of dishes, which hurt a thousand where witches hurt one. Withdraw the fuel of excess, and the fire of lust will of itself go out; or at least this enemy must be besieged and starved out, when it cannot be conquered by storm.

**Direction 2. Beware of idleness, and be wholly taken up in diligent business of your lawful callings, when you are not occupied in the more immediate service of God. [461]**

David in his idleness or leisure caught those sparks of lust which in his troubles and military life he was preserved from. Idleness is the soil, the cultivation, and the opportunity of lust. The idle person goes to school to the devil; he sets all other employment aside, so that the devil may have time to teach him, and deal with him, and solicit him to evil. [462] Do you wonder that he is thinking about lustful objects, or that he is taken up in feasting and drinking, in lewdness and wantonness? Why, he has nothing else to do: whereas a hardworking, diligent person has a body subdued and hardened against the *mollities*, the effeminateness of the wanton; and a mind employed and taken up with better things. Leave your body and mind no leisure to think of tempting, filthy objects, or to look after them. As Jerome says, *Facito aliquid operis, ut semper diabolus inveniat te occupatum*: Be always doing some work, so that the devil may always find you busy. And do not for your fleshly ease slacken your labors and indulge your flesh. Rise early and go late to bed, and put yourself under a necessity of diligence all the day: undertake and engage yourself in as much business as you are able to go through, so that if you would, you may not be able to give any indulgence to the flesh; for if you are not always pressed by necessity, lust will serve itself by idleness, and the flesh will lie down if it does not feel the spur: therefore the rich and idle are more lustful and filthy than the poor laboring people. The same bed is the place of sloth and lust. Hear a heathen, and do not refuse to imitate him. Seneca says, No day passes me in idleness: part of the night I reserve for studies: I do not purposely set myself to sleep, but yield to it when it overcomes me; and when my eyes are wearied with wakefulness, and are falling, I hold them to their work:—I would rather it went ill with me than delicately or tenderly. If you are delicate or tender, the mind little by little becomes effeminate, and is dissolved into the likeness of the idleness and sloth in which it lies. I sleep very little, and take only a short nap: it is enough for me to have stopped being awake: sometimes I know that I slept, sometimes I only suspect it. [463] Aristotle says, Nature made nothing to be idle. And Plato calls idleness the plague of mortals. If you are resolved to serve and please your flesh, then never ask advice against your lust; for it is part of the pleasure of it; and then no wonder if you refuse this medicine as too bitter, and the remedy as too costly. But if you are resolved to be cured and to be saved, do not balk at the pains: give yourself up totally to your business, and lust will die for lack of food.

**Direction 3. If you would be free from lust, keep far enough from the tempting object.**

If possible, do not live in the house with any person that you feel yourself endangered by; if that is not possible, avoid their company, especially in private: abhor all lascivious and immodest actions. Do you give yourself the liberty of wanton dalliance, and lustful embraces, and yet think to be free from lust? will you put your hand into the fire, when you are afraid of being burned? Either you have the power of your own heart, or you do not: if you have, why do you not quench your lust? if you have not, why do you cast it upon greater temptations, and put it further out of your power than it is? Flee from a tempting object for your safety, as you would flee from an enemy for your life. These loving enemies are more dangerous than hating enemies: they get the key of our hearts, and come in and steal our treasure with our consent, or without resistance; when an open enemy is suspected and shut out.

**Direction 4. Command your eyes, and, as in Job 31:1, make a covenant with them, that you may not think on tempting objects: shut these windows, and you preserve your heart.**

Do not gaze upon any alluring object. A look has kindled that fire of lust in many a heart that has ended in the fire of hell. It is easier to stop lust at these outward doors than drive it out when it has tainted the heart. If you cannot do this much, how can you do more? An ungoverned eye fetches fire to burn the soul that should have governed it. [464]

**Direction 5. Do not linger in the pleasant snares of lust, if you feel even the least beginnings of it; but quickly cast water on the first discerned spark, before it breaks out into a flame.**

The amorous poet can teach you this, Ovid, *de Rem. Am.* [465] If ever delay is dangerous, it is here. For delay will bring on such engagements to sin that you must come off at a far higher cost. If the food is indigestible, it is best not to look on it; it is the next best not to touch or taste it; but if once it goes down, it will cost you sickness and pain to get it up again; and if you do not, you perish by it.

## Translator's notes

- **Scripture translation:** NET Bible®; see [NOTICE.md](../../NOTICE.md). Every verse quoted as NET was fetched from labs.bible.org.
- **Labels:** this file opens Title 2 of Part 5. "Tit." in the source is Baxter's abbreviation for "Title," a heading or division within a Part. Title 1 of Part 5 dealt with fornication and all uncleanness; Title 2 turns to inward lust.
- **Mark 2:19–20:** NET "at that time they will fast." Only the word "fast" is quoted; the sense is unchanged.
- **2 Corinthians 6:5; 11:27:** the King James Baxter quotes has "in fastings" (6:5) and "in fastings often" (11:27). The NET has "in hunger" (6:5) and "many times without food" (11:27); we quote 11:27. **This changes the sense Baxter leans on:** he cites Paul's voluntary fasting as a discipline, while the NET wording can describe going without food from hardship rather than by choice. "Hardworking Paul" is Baxter's "painful Paul" (old sense: painstaking, laborious).
- **1 Corinthians 9:27:** Baxter turns the King James ("I keep under my body, and bring it into subjection: lest that by any means, when I have preached to others, I myself should be a castaway") into the third person. As an adapted quotation, his wording is kept, lightly modernized. The NET reads: "Instead I subdue my body and make it my slave, so that after preaching to others I myself will not be disqualified." The NET's "disqualified" (an athletic image) is milder than "castaway," which Baxter's readers would likely hear as "rejected by God."
- **1 Corinthians 7:5:** the King James has "that ye may give yourselves to fasting and prayer." **The NET has no "fasting" here** ("so that you may devote yourselves to prayer"), following Greek manuscripts that lack the word. Baxter's quoted word "fasting" is kept in his quotation marks; his proof for fasting at this verse does not stand in the NET.
- **Mark 9:29:** the King James has "This kind can come forth by nothing, but by prayer and fasting." **The NET reads "This kind can come out only by prayer,"** without "and fasting," again following manuscripts that lack the words. Baxter's quoted "prayer and fasting" is kept; his proof for fasting here does not stand in the NET.
- **1 Thessalonians 4:3–4:** NET wording. Baxter's quotation runs into verse 5 ("as the gentiles that know not God"); we keep his reference. The NET's "become holy" replaces the King James "sanctification," "sexual immorality" replaces "fornication," "his own body" replaces "his vessel," and "lustful passion" replaces "the lust of concupiscence." "Vessel" in the Greek is read by some as one's body and by others as one's wife; the NET's "body" fits Baxter's use (governing one's own body). "As 1 Thessalonians 4:3–4 says": "says" is added for grammar.
- **Job 31:1** (Direction 4): not quoted, only alluded to ("make a covenant with them"). The NET reads "I made a covenant with my eyes; how then could I entertain thoughts against a virgin?" Baxter's "that you may not think on tempting objects" is his own extension. "As in Job 31:1": "in" is added for grammar; Baxter means "as Job did."
- **Unmarked allusions** (kept in Baxter's wording; references are ours): "fullness of bread" of Sodom (Ezekiel 16:49; NET "abundance of food"); "lewdness and wantonness" (Baxter's "chambering and wantonness," Romans 13:13; NET "sexual immorality and sensuality"); David's sin in leisure (2 Samuel 11:1–4); "This unclean devil" echoes the "unclean spirit" of Mark 9:25.
- **Dated medicine:** "constitution" is Baxter's "temperature," and "condition" his "temper," old terms for the body's balance of qualities (hot, cold, moist, dry). "Hot bodies," "heating" and "cooling" foods, "windy" foods (causing gas), bloodletting, and purges that "evacuate serous fluid" (Baxter's "serosity," the watery humors) belong to the medicine of his day. They are kept as his, with his own caution to consult a physician. "To bring it to" is his "to reduce it unto" (old sense: bring back, bring).
- **Meals:** "dinner" here is the main midday meal and "supper" the evening meal. "Food" is Baxter's "meat" (food in general) throughout.
- **Word swaps** (Baxter's word, then ours): serve turn → serve the purpose; physic → medicine; prevail → succeed; dear → costly / higher (cost); sup → sip; a bit or two → a bite or two; unless → except; let blood → bled; advised with → consulted; had rather → would rather; had need of → needs; meaner → poorer; yea → yes; but (by prayer) → except; a carrion → a carcass; accounted → considered; felicity → happiness; but that → except that; to (any of his enemies) → against; voluptuousness → indulgence in pleasure; urgency → urging; like to → likely to; take heed of → beware of; exercised → occupied; vacancy → leisure; catched → caught; culture → cultivation; treat with → deal with; still (doing, pressed) → always; remit → slacken; put thyself upon → put yourself under; watching → wakefulness / being awake; similitude → likeness; sufficeth → is enough; stick not at → do not balk at; want → lack; dwell → live; embracements → embraces; fly → flee; occasion → bring on; undigestible → indigestible; shamefacedness → bashfulness; contrarily → on the contrary.
- **Kept words:** *callings* (a person's lawful occupation or vocation); *dalliance* (amorous play); *effeminate, effeminateness* (Baxter's and Seneca's word for softness and self-indulgence, kept as he wrote it); *mollities* (Latin, "softness," which Baxter glosses himself); *luxurious* ("wanton or luxurious"): in Baxter's day the word could mean lustful as well as extravagant, and here probably carries both; *strange attire* (unusual or foreign dress); *paunch*; *pernicious*.
- **"so say"** (Epaminondas passage): Baxter's "so say thou"; the modern imperative drops the pronoun.
- **Word order:** "and ate many of them nothing but bread" is given as "and many of them ate nothing but bread"; "therefore are the rich and idle more lustful" as "therefore the rich and idle are more lustful"; "Gaze not," "dwell not," "refuse not," "stick not" as "Do not gaze," etc. These are the only changes of order, made for modern grammar.
- **The Spartan story** (Plutarch): Baxter gives the Latin and his own translation, both kept. "Taget" and "Eurota" are kept as printed; they are probably Taygetus (a mountain range by Sparta) and the Eurotas (Sparta's river), though Baxter calls both rivers. We have not checked his source.
- **Antisthenes** (note 459): the Latin is Baxter's, with his own translation in the text. **The philosopher and Antigonus** (note 460): Baxter does not name the philosopher; we have not identified him.
- **Epaminondas:** the Latin *Ego te sacrificare, non lascivire putaram* is not translated by Baxter. Draft: "I thought you were offering a sacrifice, not running riot."
- **Jerome:** Baxter's "Hierom." The Latin and its translation are Baxter's.
- **Seneca:** Baxter's English in the text is his own rendering of the Latin in note 463; we have modernized his English. The passage is probably drawn from Seneca's *Moral Letters*, but we have not checked which letters. "Sufficeth me to have ceased watching" (rendered "it is enough for me to have stopped being awake") translates *satis est mihi vigilare desiisse*.
- **Aristotle, Plato:** the sayings are given as Baxter gives them; we have not located them.
- **Notes 458–460, "Laert. 1. 7."** etc.: "Laert." is probably Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of the Philosophers*. The "1." in each reference is probably a misprint or misreading for "l." (*liber*, book); kept as printed.
- **Notes 461–462 and Direction 5:** "Ovid, *de Rem. Am.*" is Ovid's *Remedia Amoris* ("Remedies for Love"). Note 465 is quoted from it. The line in note 461 is probably from the same poem (not checked). "The amorous poet" is Baxter's description of Ovid, known for his love poetry.
- **Note 464:** "Panthra" is kept as printed; it is probably Panthea, a captive whom Cyrus refused to look at in the story told by Xenophon (not checked). Plutarch's *de Curiositate* is his essay "On Curiosity" (or "On Being a Busybody"); "p. 142" refers to an edition we have not identified.
- **Note 465:** the Latin verse is set as a block quote; the line break after "Nam mora dat vires.----" and the dashes marking skipped lines are as in the source.
- **Baxter's claims** that most under this temptation are strong enough to fast, and that the rich and idle are more lustful than the laboring poor, are his own, kept as his.
